01

Emergency Management & Immediate Actions

Cooling is the treatment — not a supportive measure alongside the treatment. Cool first, transport second.
1
Confirm the diagnosis and get a core temperature immediately
Rectal temperature is the most accurate field/ED measurement. Heat stroke = core temperature elevation (commonly >40°C) plus CNS dysfunction (altered mental status, irrational behavior, seizure, coma). If rectal temperature isn't available in a collapsed athlete, use time-based immersion protocols rather than waiting for a monitoring device.
2
Begin active cooling immediately — cold- or ice-water immersion first-line
Ice-water immersion (1–5°C) or cold-water immersion (9–12°C) achieves the fastest cooling rate of any method and should be prioritized where available. Active cooling is recommended over passive methods across the board — this is a strong recommendation, not a preference.
3
Target: core temperature <39°C within 30 minutes of recognition
Prioritize any cooling method that achieves a rate ≥0.155°C/min. Similar cooling strategies apply to both classic (nonexertional) and exertional heat stroke — the underlying urgency is the same regardless of cause.
4
Cool first, transport second
Begin cooling on-site before transport whenever feasible — delays in initiating cooling are directly associated with worse outcomes. Activate EMS simultaneously with starting cooling, not sequentially after.
5
If immersion isn't feasible, use the fastest available alternative
Rotating ice-water-soaked towels, tarp-assisted cooling (TACO), or continuous cold-water dousing are reasonable alternatives — all clearly inferior to immersion in laboratory cooling-rate comparisons, but studied in real-world use with good outcomes when immersion genuinely isn't practical (mass-casualty settings, limited equipment).
6
Monitor core temperature continuously during cooling
Cease active cooling once rectal temperature reaches roughly 38.3–39°C to avoid overshooting into iatrogenic hypothermia — continue monitoring afterward since temperature can drift after cooling stops.
7
Avoid pharmacologic temperature-control agents entirely
No evidence supports acetaminophen, NSAIDs, salicylates, or dantrolene for heat stroke — avoid all of them. This isn't a fever responding to a hypothalamic-set-point drug; none of these have a mechanism that addresses the actual problem.
8
Supportive care in parallel
Cold IV fluids as an adjunct to cooling, airway protection for depressed mental status, and treatment of seizures if they occur — but prophylactic antiseizure medication or prophylactic antibiotics should be used only in a research context, not routinely.
The Currie response is a real but transient phenomenon — don't let it stop cooling

Cold-water immersion can cause a brief initial rise or plateau in core temperature (peripheral vasoconstriction trapping heat centrally) before the expected drop begins. This effect is minor (roughly 0.1–0.2°C) and short-lived — it is not a reason to abandon or delay immersion, which remains the fastest and most effective cooling method despite this transient response.

02

Brief Review

Heat stroke is severe hyperthermia with central nervous system dysfunction, subclassified as classic (nonexertional — typically elderly, chronically ill, or those with impaired thermoregulation during ambient heat exposure) or exertional (typically young, healthy individuals engaged in strenuous activity, often in heat). Both are true medical emergencies: ICU mortality can approach 60% in severe cases, and roughly 30% of survivors experience long-term cognitive or motor dysfunction. Cell death in the CNS increases exponentially with both temperature and duration of exposure — the entire treatment paradigm exists to interrupt that exponential curve as fast as possible.

The 2025 Society of Critical Care Medicine guideline — the current definitive reference — issued two strong recommendations, five good practice statements, and one research-only statement. The overall message is unusually unambiguous for a critical care guideline: active cooling, as fast as possible, using the best available method, started immediately, with essentially no role for pharmacologic temperature control.

Prevention & Set-Up (In Place of "Room Setup")

  • Have an immersion cooling setup ready before it's needed — at any event, facility, or unit where heat stroke risk is plausible (athletic events, military training, high-heat occupational settings), not assembled reactively after a collapse.
  • Set up cooling capability during the first weeks of a heat-exposure season and whenever wet-bulb globe temperature crosses a defined threshold — this is standard athletic emergency action plan guidance, not something to improvise mid-season.
  • Know your fastest available alternative if immersion isn't feasible — rotating ice-water towels and TACO both have real supporting outcome data and should be part of the plan, not a last-minute improvisation.
  • Recognize medication-related risk factors: drugs that increase heat production (amphetamines, thyroid agonists, weight-loss supplements) or impair thermoregulation (anticholinergics, phenothiazines, diuretics, beta-blockers, antipsychotics, SSRIs) all raise individual risk and are worth knowing in a patient's history.
  • Have EMS activation and cooling happen simultaneously, not sequentially — "cool first, transport second" needs to be a rehearsed protocol, not a judgment call made under pressure.

Rationale for Heat Stroke Management

Why cold-water immersion isn't just "preferred" but categorically faster: immersion couples the body's entire surface area to a cold conductive medium simultaneously, while every alternative (fanning, misting, ice packs at discrete sites) relies on evaporation or localized conduction across a much smaller effective contact area. This is a physics difference, not a marginal clinical preference — laboratory and field data consistently show immersion cooling 6 to 10 times faster than the next-best widely available alternatives, which is why guideline language doesn't hedge on this point the way it does on many other critical care interventions.

Why the guideline expresses urgency as a specific numeric cooling rate rather than just "cool quickly": CNS cell death increases exponentially with both temperature and duration of exposure — meaning the actual physiologic damage isn't linear with time, and a vague "cool as fast as reasonably possible" instruction doesn't capture how much a delay of even a few extra minutes can matter. Anchoring to ≥0.155°C/min and a 30-minute target temperature gives providers something concrete to plan equipment and staffing around, rather than a qualitative goal that's easy to underachieve without realizing it.

Why no pharmacologic agent is recommended, including drugs that seem intuitively related: antipyretics like acetaminophen work by resetting the hypothalamic thermoregulatory set point — but heat stroke isn't a set-point fever, it's a failure of the body's ability to dissipate heat faster than it's being generated or absorbed. There's no elevated set point to reset. Dantrolene, despite its unrelated but conceptually adjacent role in malignant hyperthermia (see that crisis file), specifically showed no benefit in reducing mortality or cooling time in heat stroke trials — different mechanism of heat generation entirely (excessive skeletal muscle metabolic heat production from a triggering anesthetic, versus environmental/exertional heat exceeding dissipation capacity), so a drug effective for one doesn't transfer to the other.

Why "cool first, transport second" is stated as a specific operational principle rather than assumed: transport time, even short transport time, is dead time with respect to the exponential CNS injury curve described above unless active cooling is already underway during that transport. Making this an explicit, named principle — rather than trusting individual judgment in the moment — pushes teams toward starting cooling on-site by default, which the outcome data (including reported 100% survival when cooling began within 10 minutes of collapse) directly supports.

Why the transient initial temperature rise on immersion (the Currie response) doesn't change the recommendation: peripheral vasoconstriction from sudden cold exposure can briefly trap heat centrally, producing a small, short-lived uptick or plateau before the expected drop. This is a normal, minor physiologic response (roughly 0.1–0.2°C) rather than a sign the intervention is failing — understanding this mechanism prevents a provider from second-guessing or discontinuing the single most effective treatment available based on a brief, expected, and clinically insignificant reading.

Workup & Disposition

Labs: CBC, comprehensive metabolic panel, liver function tests, coagulation panel (DIC risk in severe cases), creatine kinase (concurrent rhabdomyolysis is common — see the dedicated Crush Syndrome crisis file for the broader hyperkalemia/AKI management framework that applies here too), and lactate. Continuous core temperature monitoring throughout the ED/ICU course, not just during active cooling.

CT imaging for altered mental status not clearly explained by heat stroke alone, or that fails to improve as expected with cooling — don't delay active cooling to obtain imaging first.

Monitor for multi-organ involvement: acute kidney injury, hepatic injury, coagulopathy/DIC, and rhabdomyolysis can all develop or worsen over the following 24–72 hours even after successful initial cooling — this is not a single-intervention-and-done presentation.

Disposition: ICU admission is standard for any confirmed heat stroke given the mortality and long-term morbidity data above, plus the need for continued monitoring of the multi-organ complications described.

Consider burn evaluation in classic heat stroke specifically — direct contact with surfaces like pavement, which can exceed 71°C, can produce concurrent burn injury that's easy to overlook amid the systemic presentation.
