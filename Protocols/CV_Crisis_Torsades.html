Emergency Management & Immediate Actions

Unstable or degenerating to VF: defibrillate without delay. Otherwise, magnesium first — and confirm the QT is actually prolonged before assuming magnesium will work.
1
Unstable or pulseless — defibrillate immediately
Torsades can degenerate into VF; treat with the same unsynchronized defibrillation strategy. Don't delay shock to give medications first if the patient is unstable or pulseless.
2
IV magnesium sulfate — first-line, regardless of serum magnesium level
1–2 g IV over 1–2 minutes if unstable/in extremis (slow IV push is reasonable), or over 5–15 minutes if more stable. Give it even when a measured magnesium level is normal — magnesium suppresses the arrhythmia through a mechanism independent of correcting a deficiency.
3
Withdraw all QT-prolonging drugs immediately
A Class I recommendation — stop the offending agent(s) as soon as identified, don't wait until after the arrhythmia resolves.
4
Correct electrolytes aggressively
Replete potassium to a supratherapeutic 4.5–5.0 mmol/L target (not just the normal range) — this shortens the QT interval and reduces recurrence risk. Correct hypocalcemia if present.
5
Recurrent despite magnesium and electrolyte correction — increase the heart rate
Temporary transvenous overdrive pacing at 100–120 bpm (occasionally up to 140 bpm) is the therapy of choice for drug-refractory, recurrent torsades — increasing heart rate shortens the QT interval and eliminates the pauses that trigger the arrhythmia.
6
Isoproterenol if pacing isn't immediately available
2–10 mcg/min infusion (or a 10–20 mcg IV push), titrated to a heart rate around 100 bpm. Use only for acquired (not congenital) long QT with clearly pause-dependent torsades — isoproterenol is contraindicated in congenital long QT syndrome and in patients with ischemic heart disease or hypertension.
7
Avoid making it worse
Do not use amiodarone, procainamide, sotalol, or other Class Ia/III antiarrhythmics — all further prolong the QT and can worsen torsades. Do not use calcium channel blockers to terminate a wide-complex tachycardia of unknown origin. Lidocaine is a reasonable alternative if an antiarrhythmic is needed, since it doesn't prolong the QT.
8
Refractory cases — consider point-of-care ionized magnesium monitoring
Total serum magnesium correlates poorly with the physiologically active ionized fraction in critically ill patients — case reports describe genuinely refractory torsades responding to further magnesium dosing once guided by ionized magnesium measurement rather than total magnesium alone.
Magnesium won't help if the QT is normal

If the rhythm is polymorphic VT with a normal QT interval, magnesium is not the treatment — this distinguishes true torsades from other forms of polymorphic VT and points toward conventional antiarrhythmic therapy instead. Confirm QT prolongation before assuming this is torsades.

02

Brief Review

Torsades de pointes ("twisting of the points") is a distinctive form of polymorphic ventricular tachycardia associated with a prolonged QT interval, characterized on ECG by gradually changing QRS amplitude that appears to twist around the isoelectric baseline. QT prolongation predisposes to early afterdepolarizations and spatial dispersion of ventricular refractoriness — the electrophysiologic substrate that allows this specific reentrant rhythm to develop. Episodes are often self-terminating but frequently recurrent, and can degenerate into ventricular fibrillation.

A QTc is considered prolonged above 450 ms in men and 460 ms in women; a QTc >500 ms carries a 2–3-fold increased risk of torsades. The long QT interval can be congenital or acquired — acquired prolongation is most often medication-related, with antiarrhythmics, antipsychotics, antiemetics, antifungals, and certain antimicrobials among the most frequently implicated drug classes in real-world pharmacovigilance data.

Prevention & Set-Up (In Place of "Room Setup")

  • Obtain a baseline ECG before starting a QT-prolonging drug in any patient with an existing electrolyte abnormality, already on another QT-prolonging agent, or starting a high-risk agent.
  • Identify higher-risk patients proactively: organic heart disease, low baseline potassium/magnesium, a prior torsades episode, bradycardia, or baseline QT prolongation all raise risk when a QT-prolonging drug is introduced.
  • Modify dosing thresholds: if QTc reaches 560–600 ms after starting a QT-prolonging therapy, reduce the dose; discontinue the drug and admit immediately if lightheadedness, syncope, or increasing ventricular ectopy develops.
  • Anesthesia-specific risk awareness: several commonly used anesthetic and sedative agents (propofol, dexmedetomidine, ketamine) have documented associations with QT prolongation and, rarely, drug-induced torsades — worth factoring into anesthetic planning for a patient with known QT risk.
  • Maintain potassium and magnesium in the normal-to-high range proactively in any patient on multiple QT-prolonging agents, rather than only correcting reactively after an event.
  • Provide a QT-prolonging drug list (e.g., crediblemeds.org/qtdrugs.org) to patients with a history of torsades or known long QT syndrome, and educate them to avoid unnecessary combinations.

Rationale for Torsades Management

Why magnesium works even with a normal serum level: torsades arises from early afterdepolarizations triggered by prolonged repolarization — magnesium suppresses these afterdepolarizations directly at the cellular level, largely independent of whether the patient has a measurable magnesium deficiency. This is also why magnesium is considered safe to give even in diagnostic uncertainty: in true torsades it works, and in polymorphic VT with a normal QT (where magnesium won't help) it also won't make things worse, unlike a QT-prolonging antiarrhythmic given on the wrong assumption.

Why overdrive pacing and isoproterenol both work through the same underlying principle: faster heart rates shorten the QT interval and eliminate the pauses that create the short-long-short initiating sequence characteristic of torsades. Pacing does this mechanically and reliably; isoproterenol does it pharmacologically but carries real contraindications (ischemic disease, hypertension, and congenital — as opposed to acquired — long QT syndrome, where accelerating the heart doesn't address the underlying channelopathy the same way).

Why potassium gets repleted above the normal range specifically for this indication: standard "normal" potassium (3.5–5.0) is adequate for most cardiac purposes, but the QT-shortening effect specifically benefits from pushing potassium to the higher end (4.5–5.0) — this is a deliberate, indication-specific target rather than simply correcting a deficiency back to baseline.

Why amiodarone and other Class Ia/III agents are avoided despite being standard VT drugs elsewhere: these agents work by prolonging repolarization — exactly the mechanism that caused torsades in the first place. Using a QT-prolonging antiarrhythmic to treat a QT-prolongation-driven arrhythmia is directly counterproductive, which is why this is one of the clearest examples in resuscitation medicine of a "standard" drug being actively wrong for a specific rhythm.

Why the evidence base here is thinner than the confident-sounding guidance suggests: honest appraisal (First10EM among others) notes there are no randomized trials establishing pacing's efficacy for torsades, and the observational data supporting magnesium — while consistent and long-standing since the 1980s — earns only a modest evidence grade (Class IIb) in ACLS terms. The guidance is followed because decades of consistent clinical experience and a clear, plausible mechanism support it, and because the alternative (untreated pause-dependent recurrent torsades) is dangerous enough that acting on strong observational signal is the reasonable choice — not because the trials are definitive.

Differential Diagnosis

MimicKey distinguishing features
Polymorphic VT with a normal QT (e.g., ischemic VT)The critical distinction — magnesium is ineffective here; standard antiarrhythmic therapy (amiodarone, procainamide) is appropriate instead, the opposite of true torsades management.
Monomorphic VTConsistent QRS morphology beat-to-beat, unlike the twisting amplitude pattern of torsades; standard VT algorithm and antiarrhythmics apply.
Ventricular fibrillation (de novo, not degenerated from torsades)No organized QRS complexes at all; treated with standard defibrillation and ACLS regardless of the distinction once truly fibrillating.
Catecholaminergic polymorphic VT (CPVT)Occurs with a normal QT interval, classically triggered by exercise/catecholamine surge in younger patients — a distinct channelopathy from long QT syndrome, with different genetics and treatment (beta-blockade, flecainide) rather than magnesium.
Brugada syndrome-related VFA different ECG pattern (coved ST elevation in right precordial leads) and different arrhythmic mechanism — not QT-prolongation-driven.
08

Workup & Disposition

Confirm and characterize: a 12-lead ECG once the rhythm resolves to measure the QTc directly, basic metabolic panel with magnesium and calcium, and a careful medication reconciliation to identify every potential QT-prolonging agent — not just the most obvious one.

Continuous monitoring: torsades frequently recurs even after apparent resolution — continuous cardiac monitoring is essential, not optional, following any episode.

Distinguish acquired from congenital: for a first episode without an obvious drug or electrolyte trigger, or in a younger patient, consider a personal and family history screen for congenital long QT syndrome — this changes long-term management (beta-blockade, possible ICD, family screening) substantially from a purely acquired, reversible cause.

Disposition: ICU-level monitoring for any patient with a torsades episode until the QTc has normalized and the precipitating factor is clearly identified and corrected/removed.

Documentation and follow-up: flag the chart with the specific culprit medication(s) if drug-induced, and provide the patient with a QT-prolonging drug avoidance list before discharge — recurrence risk with re-exposure to the same or a similar agent is real and preventable.
