01

Emergency Management & Immediate Actions

Standard ACLS runs in parallel, but the actual path to ROSC here is stabilizing the membrane and correcting the potassium — plan for a longer resuscitation than usual.
1
Calcium chloride immediately — don't wait for lab confirmation
1 g IV (10 mL of 10% solution) through a well-running peripheral line or central line. If hyperkalemia is clinically suspected (dialysis patient, renal failure, crush injury, known history) with arrest or malignant ECG findings, give it on suspicion — repeat every 5 minutes until the QRS narrows or ECG changes resolve.
2
Insulin plus dextrose — the most reliable shifting agent
Regular insulin 10 units IV with dextrose 25 g (50 mL of D50W) if the patient isn't already hyperglycemic. This is the single most potent, most evidence-supported intervention for actually lowering serum potassium acutely — expect a drop of roughly 0.5–1.5 mEq/L over 15–60 minutes.
3
Nebulized albuterol — after insulin/glucose, not before
10–20 mg nebulized (far above a standard bronchodilator dose) as an additive shifting agent. Give it after insulin/glucose — a beta-agonist given first, before the insulin effect is underway, can transiently worsen serum potassium in some patients.
4
Sodium bicarbonate — situational, not routine
50–100 mEq IV if concurrent metabolic acidosis is documented or strongly suspected. Do not give bicarbonate reflexively just because an arrest has been prolonged and acidosis is presumed — that specific practice has no proven benefit and can worsen intracellular acidosis.
5
Continue standard ACLS in parallel — but expect a longer code
Epinephrine 1 mg IV/IO every 3–5 minutes per standard protocol, defibrillation for shockable rhythms. Continue high-quality CPR until the hyperkalemia is actually corrected — this may take considerably longer than a typical resuscitation, and ROSC when it occurs will be driven primarily by calcium's membrane-stabilizing effect and the shifting agents, not by additional epinephrine.
6
Activate emergency dialysis
The only intervention that actually removes potassium from the body rather than shifting or buffering it. Involve nephrology immediately in any known or suspected renal failure patient — this is the definitive fix everything else is bridging toward.
7
Reassess and redose calcium as needed
Calcium's stabilizing effect wears off in roughly 20–30 minutes (up to an hour by some estimates) — if ECG changes recur or the QRS re-widens, redose rather than assuming a single dose was sufficient for the duration of the resuscitation.
8
Suspected digoxin toxicity — avoid calcium
If digitalis toxicity is a concern, avoid calcium (historically linked to a "stone heart" phenomenon in this specific combination) and use magnesium sulfate 2 g over 5 minutes instead for arrhythmias in this setting.
Treat the ECG and the clinical picture, not just a lab number

A prior blood gas, known dialysis status, or crush injury mechanism is enough to start treatment before a confirmatory potassium level returns. Waiting for lab confirmation in a patient with malignant ECG changes or arrest wastes the exact minutes the myocardium doesn't have.

02

Brief Review

Hyperkalemia is defined as serum potassium above roughly 5.0–5.5 mEq/L, present in about 2% of hospitalized patients. Mild elevation is usually asymptomatic; severe elevation (commonly cited around 6.5–7 mEq/L or higher, especially with an acute rate of rise) narrows the gap between resting and threshold membrane potential in cardiac myocytes, slowing conduction and predisposing to life-threatening arrhythmias, including cardiac arrest.

Common causes include chronic kidney disease or acute kidney injury with impaired excretion, medications (NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors/ARBs, potassium-sparing diuretics), cellular potassium release (burns, hemolysis, rhabdomyolysis, tumor lysis), acidosis-driven extracellular shift, and endocrine causes such as adrenal insufficiency. Cardiac arrest from hyperkalemia is one of the few arrest etiologies where the underlying cause is both rapidly identifiable and directly, pharmacologically reversible — which is exactly why it gets its own dedicated management pathway distinct from standard ACLS.

ECG severity doesn't track cleanly with the absolute potassium number. Some patients with dramatic lab values show minimal ECG change, while others decompensate at more moderate levels — treat the ECG and clinical trajectory in front of you, not a number in isolation.

Prevention & Set-Up (In Place of "Room Setup")

  • Point-of-care potassium testing availability for dialysis patients, renal failure patients, or anyone with a mechanism (crush injury, burns, rhabdomyolysis) that predisposes to rapid hyperkalemia.
  • Continuous cardiac monitoring for any patient with known or suspected hyperkalemia, even before ECG changes are visible.
  • Avoid succinylcholine in patients with renal failure, burns, prolonged immobility, or other conditions predisposing to an exaggerated hyperkalemic response — see the Difficult/Failed Airway and general anesthesia-specific crisis files for related detail.
  • Medication review in at-risk patients (ACE inhibitors/ARBs, potassium-sparing diuretics, NSAIDs, potassium supplements) — identifying and holding contributing medications is part of both prevention and ongoing management.
  • Have calcium chloride, insulin, dextrose, and albuterol immediately accessible in any unit managing dialysis or renal failure patients regularly, rather than treating this as a rare-event kit to be located under pressure.

Rationale for Hyperkalemic Arrest Management

Why calcium is given first despite not lowering potassium at all: hyperkalemia narrows the gap between a myocyte's resting membrane potential and its threshold potential, making the cell dangerously easy to depolarize inappropriately. Calcium raises that threshold back toward normal, directly counteracting the arrhythmogenic mechanism within minutes — independent of, and much faster than, anything that actually changes the serum potassium level. It's cardiac membrane protection, not potassium treatment, which is exactly why it has to be paired with a shifting agent rather than used alone.

Why the COCA trial's findings on calcium don't overturn this specific indication: recent evidence (including a 2021 trial and a 2025 systematic review) has questioned routine calcium administration in undifferentiated cardiac arrest, with some data suggesting possible harm. That evidence base is about calcium given empirically in arrests of unknown cause — the hyperkalemia-specific indication, where a clear mechanistic rationale and consistent clinical experience support rapid ECG improvement, remains distinct and is why expert guidance continues to favor calcium specifically when hyperkalemia is the suspected or confirmed driver.

Why insulin/glucose is the preferred shifting agent over albuterol alone: insulin directly stimulates the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump, driving potassium into cells through the same mechanism the body normally uses — it's more potent and more predictable than beta-agonism alone. Albuterol works through a related but weaker pathway and requires a much larger-than-bronchodilator dose to have a meaningful potassium-shifting effect, which is why it's used as an addition to, not a substitute for, insulin/glucose.

Why albuterol specifically follows insulin rather than preceding it: beta-agonist stimulation can transiently increase serum potassium before its shifting effect takes hold, related to an initial catecholamine-driven potassium efflux. Giving it after insulin/glucose is already underway avoids stacking a transient worsening on top of a still-elevated potassium level during the most dangerous part of the timeline.

Why the resuscitation should run longer than a typical arrest: in most cardiac arrests, prolonged CPR without ROSC signals a poor trajectory. In hyperkalemic arrest specifically, the underlying cause is actively being corrected in real time by calcium, insulin/glucose, and (if activated) dialysis — ROSC is expected to follow correction of the potassium, not simply more time or more epinephrine. This is the clearest example in this crisis series of why "diagnosis-driven" resuscitation (see the Perioperative Cardiac Arrest file) changes not just the drugs given but the entire decision about how long to continue.

Differential Diagnosis

Mimic / considerationKey distinguishing features
Sodium channel blocker toxicity (TCA, cocaine, other)Also produces QRS widening, but the mechanism and treatment differ — sodium bicarbonate is central to management here (not just situational), and calcium's role is different; history of ingestion/exposure is the key clue.
HypocalcemiaCan independently prolong QT and predispose to arrhythmia; check ionized calcium directly rather than assuming hyperkalemia explains all ECG changes.
HypermagnesemiaCan cause bradyarrhythmias and conduction delay; often coexists with renal failure and hyperkalemia, so consider checking magnesium in the same patient population.
Digoxin toxicityCan present with hyperkalemia (in acute toxicity) but changes the treatment approach — avoid calcium, treat arrhythmias with magnesium, and consider digoxin-specific antibody fragments.
PseudohyperkalemiaArtificially elevated lab value from a hemolyzed sample or severe leukocytosis/thrombocytosis — send a repeat, non-hemolyzed sample once treatment is underway, but don't delay treatment for confirmation if the ECG and clinical picture already support true hyperkalemia.
Hyperacute T waves from early STEMICan superficially resemble the peaked T waves of hyperkalemia; look for reciprocal changes, regional distribution, and clinical context (chest pain, risk factors) to distinguish.

Workup & Disposition

Confirm and trend: a repeat, non-hemolyzed potassium level, along with glucose, renal function, and an ABG/VBG for acid-base status. Recheck potassium at defined intervals (commonly around 1, 2, 4, 6, and 24 hours) after treatment to confirm the trajectory and catch rebound as insulin's effect wears off (typically 2–4 hours).

Glucose monitoring: finger-stick glucose every 30–60 minutes for at least 4–6 hours post-insulin, given the well-documented risk of delayed hypoglycemia.

Identify and treat the underlying cause: renal function assessment, medication reconciliation (hold ACE inhibitors/ARBs, potassium-sparing diuretics, NSAIDs, potassium supplements), and workup for rhabdomyolysis, tumor lysis, or endocrine causes as clinically indicated.

Definitive potassium removal: dialysis remains the definitive treatment for refractory or renal-failure-associated hyperkalemia; potassium binders (patiromer, sodium zirconium cyclosilicate) or diuretics may have a role in less acute management but shouldn't be relied on for the acute, arrest-level presentation.

Disposition: ICU admission for any patient who arrested or had malignant ECG changes from hyperkalemia, with continuous cardiac monitoring until the potassium is clearly trending down and stable.
