TURP Syndrome

Hypotonic irrigant absorbed through open prostatic venous sinuses — acute dilutional hyponatremia that gets treated aggressively precisely because it's acute, plus the newer saline-irrigant variant that trades one problem for a different one.

01

Emergency Management & Immediate Actions

This is acute, not chronic, hyponatremia — that distinction changes the correction-rate rules and is the single most important thing to get right.
1
Alert the surgeon, finish the resection quickly
Every additional minute of open prostatic venous sinuses is more irrigant absorption. If TURP syndrome is suspected mid-case, the surgeon should complete resection and achieve hemostasis as efficiently as possible.
2
100% O₂, support airway/breathing/circulation
Secure the airway if consciousness is impaired. Treat hypotension and bradycardia as they arise — standard supportive measures while the diagnosis is confirmed.
3
Send serum sodium and electrolytes STAT
Check intraoperatively for any prolonged case (>90 minutes) or when clinical suspicion is raised — don't wait for symptoms to develop before checking if the resection has run long.
4
Severe symptomatic hyponatremia — 3% hypertonic saline
For seizures, coma, or marked mental status change: 100–150 mL IV bolus or continuous infusion, targeting a 6 mmol/L rise in the first 6 hours. Transfer immediately to ICU with continuous monitoring. Treat active seizures with benzodiazepines in parallel.
5
Cap total correction — don't overshoot
After the initial 6 mmol/L in 6 hours, limit further correction to roughly 2 mmol/L over the next 18 hours; total correction should not exceed 8–10 mmol/L in 24 hours. Rapid correction targeting >1 mmol/L/hour is appropriate specifically because this is acute (developing over hours), not chronic, hyponatremia — but the 24-hour cap still applies to avoid overshoot.
6
Loop diuretic — only if hemodynamically stable
Furosemide can help eliminate excess absorbed fluid in a volume-overloaded but stable patient. Avoid diuretic therapy in a hemodynamically unstable patient — it removes volume from a circulation that may already be compromised.
7
Refractory/severe cases — consider hemodialysis
Particularly when there's a large osmolal gap (significant hyperglycinemia/hyperammonemia from glycine irrigant) or renal impairment limiting other correction strategies — hemodialysis can rapidly correct both the sodium and remove the glycine metabolites simultaneously.
8
If a saline-based irrigant (TURis/bipolar) was used and the picture doesn't fit classic hyponatremia
Check an ABG/chemistry for hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis — large-volume normal saline absorption can cause this distinct picture, which responds to bicarbonate rather than vasopressors when hypotension is present.
Acute hyponatremia gets treated differently than chronic

The traditional "correct hyponatremia slowly to avoid osmotic demyelination" rule applies to chronic hyponatremia, where the brain has already adapted. TURP syndrome develops over hours during a single procedure — there's been no time for that adaptation, so rapid initial correction of severe symptoms is appropriate and the real risk being managed is cerebral edema, not overly-fast correction.

02

Brief Review

TURP syndrome is a systemic complication from excessive absorption of electrolyte-free irrigation fluid through open prostatic venous sinuses during transurethral resection of the prostate (also described with transurethral bladder resection, ureteroscopy, and hysteroscopy). It produces a combination of acute hypervolemia, dilutional hyponatremia, and — with glycine-based irrigants specifically — direct CNS toxicity from the glycine itself, which acts as an inhibitory GABA agonist while paradoxically potentiating NMDA receptors.

Reported incidence is under 1% (roughly 0.78–1.4% in recent series), reflecting the shift toward bipolar resectoscopes using saline irrigant, which have made the classic glycine-mediated syndrome less common than it once was. Despite the low incidence, mortality in severe cases has been reported as high as 25%, and onset can occur as early as 15 minutes into resection or be delayed up to 24 hours postoperatively.

Prevention & Set-Up (In Place of "Room Setup")

  • Limit resection time to under 90 minutes where possible — the single most direct lever, since absorption is time-dependent on open venous sinuses.
  • Prefer bipolar resectoscope with isotonic saline irrigant (TURis) over monopolar with glycine when available — eliminates the classic hyponatremic/glycine-toxic syndrome, though large-volume saline absorption can still cause volume overload and, less commonly, hyperchloremic acidosis.
  • Control irrigation bag height/pressure — lower hydrostatic pressure reduces the driving force for absorption through open sinuses.
  • Prophylactic furosemide has trial-level evidence for reducing hyponatremia incidence in monopolar TURP specifically — worth considering as an institutional protocol element for higher-risk cases, though it's not yet universal practice.
  • Consider neuraxial rather than general anesthesia for TURP when feasible — an awake patient can report early neurologic symptoms (confusion, visual changes, restlessness) that would otherwise go unnoticed under general anesthesia.
  • Intraoperative monitoring options for higher-risk or prolonged cases: transesophageal Doppler to detect acute hypervolemia, ethanol-tagged irrigant with breath testing as an absorption marker, or intraoperative voiding score assessment — useful adjuncts, though not universally deployed.
  • Check serum sodium intraoperatively for any case running long or with a large resected gland, rather than waiting for symptoms.

06

Rationale for TURP Syndrome Management Steps

Why rapid correction is appropriate here, unlike most hyponatremia teaching: the standard "correct slowly to avoid osmotic demyelination syndrome" rule exists because in chronic hyponatremia, brain cells have already adapted by extruding intracellular osmoles to match the low extracellular sodium — correcting too fast reverses that adaptation faster than the cells can respond, causing demyelination. TURP syndrome develops over the course of a single procedure; there's been no time for that cellular adaptation, so the actual immediate risk is cerebral edema from the acute drop, not overcorrection. This is why severely symptomatic acute hyponatremia is treated with deliberately faster initial correction than the classic chronic-hyponatremia teaching would suggest.

Why glycine causes symptoms out of proportion to the sodium number alone: glycine is itself an inhibitory neurotransmitter at GABA receptors and also potentiates NMDA receptors — it has direct CNS effects independent of the sodium level. This is why some patients with only moderately low sodium can present with severe neurologic findings (visual disturbance, coma): the glycine toxicity and the hyponatremia are compounding, not the same problem measured twice. It's also why measured serum osmolality often looks less deranged than the sodium alone would predict — glycine is an osmotically active nonsodium solute, partially explaining the "pseudohyponatremia" component of the picture.

Why furosemide is avoided in the unstable patient specifically: a volume-overloaded but hemodynamically stable patient benefits from diuresis to offload the absorbed fluid. A patient who is already hypotensive or in cardiovascular collapse from the same syndrome doesn't have volume to spare — removing more preload in that setting worsens the hemodynamic problem you're simultaneously trying to fix.

Why the saline-irrigant (TURis) variant needs a different antidote entirely: switching to isotonic saline solves the hyponatremia/glycine-toxicity problem, but large-volume saline absorption introduces a new mechanism — dilutional hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis — that doesn't respond to the usual vasoactive-agent approach to hypotension. Recognizing which irrigant was used changes what "correcting" the picture actually requires.

08

Workup & Disposition

Serial labs during correction: recheck serum sodium every 2–4 hours during active correction to confirm you're tracking the target rate, not over- or under-correcting. Check osmolality, and consider ammonia level if a large volume of glycine irrigant was used, since hyperammonemia can independently contribute to altered mental status.

Cardiac monitoring: continuous ECG given the described T-wave changes and troponin elevation pattern with glycine absorption — these findings can persist up to 24 hours and don't necessarily indicate a primary cardiac event.

Ophthalmology follow-up if visual disturbance or transient blindness occurred and doesn't resolve promptly with correction.

Disposition: ICU admission for any symptomatic case, particularly one requiring hypertonic saline or presenting with seizure/altered consciousness — this is not a routine PACU-to-floor recovery.

Documentation and future planning: record the irrigant type, total volume used, resection duration, and the patient's clinical course — this directly informs whether future procedures should use a saline-based bipolar system, shorter staged resections, or neuraxial rather than general anesthesia for earlier symptom detection.
