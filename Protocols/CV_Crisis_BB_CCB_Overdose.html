Beta-Blocker & Calcium Channel Blocker Overdose

Conventional therapy fails more often than it succeeds — high-dose insulin is the therapy that actually turns the tide, and it works by feeding a starving heart, not by reversing the drug directly.

01

Emergency Management & Immediate Actions

Start conventional therapy, but don't wait for it to fail before considering HDI — early initiation outperforms rescue-only use.
1
IV fluid resuscitation first
Corrects the vasodilation and low cardiac filling pressures both drug classes produce — the essential first step before layering on other therapies.
2
Calcium salts
Calcium gluconate or calcium chloride — can help hypotensive patients from either agent alone or in combination, more reliably in CCB toxicity than beta-blocker toxicity specifically. Reasonable as an early adjunct regardless of exact agent.
3
Glucagon and atropine — reasonable first attempts, often disappointing
Glucagon bypasses the blocked beta-receptor to activate the same downstream intracellular pathway directly. Atropine for bradycardia. Both are worth trying, but conventional therapies (fluids, atropine, calcium, glucagon) frequently fail to restore hemodynamics in a severely poisoned patient — don't persist with escalating doses of a failing therapy at the expense of moving to HDI.
4
High-dose insulin euglycemic therapy (HDI/HIET) — start early, not as a last resort
Bolus 1 U/kg regular insulin, followed by an infusion starting at 0.5–1 U/kg/hr, titrated upward as needed (case series document rates as high as 10 U/kg/hr or more). Give 1–2 ampules of D50W (50–100 mL) when starting, unless glucose is already >250 mg/dL, and run a continuous dextrose infusion alongside. Effect can be delayed 15–60 minutes, so continue vasopressor/glucagon support in parallel while HDI takes hold — don't stop other support the moment HDI starts.
5
Monitor potassium and glucose closely once HDI is running
Check potassium and glucose before starting, then glucose every 10 minutes initially (less frequently once stable), given the real risk of profound, predictable hypoglycemia and hypokalemia at high infusion rates.
6
Vasopressors as needed, without a ceiling
There is no maximal dose of catecholamine vasopressors in this setting — some patients genuinely require unusually high doses. That said, HDI is generally preferable to escalating vasopressors alone, since high-dose catecholamines increase myocardial oxygen demand and systemic vascular resistance in ways that can worsen perfusion. Consider isoproterenol specifically for beta-blocker-driven refractory bradycardia — as a pure beta-agonist it targets the blocked receptor directly without unintended alpha-mediated vasoconstriction, though it also causes hypokalemia and lactic acidosis.
7
Lipid emulsion therapy for lipophilic agents (propranolol, verapamil, diltiazem)
Same regimen used for LAST — see that crisis file for dosing. Case reports describe successful combined use with HDI in refractory, cardiovascular-collapse-level overdose, particularly with lipophilic agents that respond to the "lipid sink" mechanism.
8
Refractory cases — methylene blue, ECMO, extracorporeal removal
Methylene blue has case-report-level evidence for hemodynamic improvement in refractory CCB overdose (vasoplegia rescue, similar rationale to its use in refractory anaphylaxis). ECMO is a genuine bridge option in truly refractory poison-induced cardiogenic shock. The EXTRIP workgroup's systematic reviews provide agent-specific recommendations on extracorporeal treatment (hemodialysis) for both beta-blocker and CCB poisoning — consult these for agent-specific guidance rather than assuming all agents in either class behave identically.
Assess mechanism before choosing your next drug

Bedside echocardiography to distinguish vasodilatory shock (needs more vasopressor) from cardiogenic shock (needs inotropic support — HDI, lipid emulsion) changes which therapy is actually going to help. Escalating the wrong category of drug wastes time in a patient who doesn't have much to spare.

02

Brief Review

Beta-blockers and calcium channel blockers are among the most commonly prescribed cardiovascular drugs, and overdose of either is common and potentially fatal — the National Poison Data System logged over 11,000 beta-blocker and 6,000 calcium-channel-blocker overdoses in a single recent year. Both classes converge on a similar clinical picture: profound bradycardia, depressed myocardial contractility, and vasodilation, producing hemodynamic instability that is frequently refractory to standard first-line therapy — a combined syndrome often called poison-induced cardiogenic shock.

CCBs block the L-type calcium channel directly, preventing the calcium influx that normally triggers myocyte contraction and, in the pancreas, insulin release — meaning CCB toxicity itself produces relative hypoinsulinemia and hyperglycemia, shifting cardiac metabolism away from its preferred glucose substrate toward less efficient fatty acid metabolism. Beta-blockers act further upstream, blunting the entire adrenergic signaling cascade that would otherwise drive heart rate and contractility. Both pathways converge on the same downstream problem: a heart that can't generate adequate cardiac output.

Prevention & Set-Up (In Place of "Room Setup")

  • Have an institutional HDI guideline/protocol in place before it's needed — guideline-driven implementation (concentrated insulin infusions, structured monitoring, clear escalation criteria) has been shown to improve safety of this therapy relative to ad hoc dosing.
  • Stock concentrated insulin (e.g., 20 units/mL) specifically for this indication — standard insulin concentrations become impractical at the infusion rates HDI sometimes requires, risking dangerous volume overload if concentrated formulations aren't available.
  • Call your regional poison center early — 1-800-222-1222 (US) — for real-time guidance on HDI titration, lipid emulsion timing, and when to escalate to ECMO; this is exactly the kind of case where expert consultation changes outcomes.
  • Use bedside echocardiography to distinguish the cardiogenic-shock component from the vasodilatory component — this directly determines whether the next escalation should be inotropic support (HDI) or additional vasopressor.
  • Know that HDI initiated early, before full refractory collapse, outperforms rescue-only use — this should shift practice toward earlier consideration in a deteriorating patient rather than waiting to exhaust every conventional option first.

Rationale for BB/CCB Overdose Management

Why HDI works despite not being a specific "antidote" to either drug class: the poisoned heart, whether from beta-blockade or calcium-channel blockade, is metabolically starved — it can't efficiently use glucose the way it normally would, and shifts toward less efficient fatty-acid metabolism under stress. Insulin at supraphysiologic doses forces glucose utilization back into the cardiac myocyte, restores lactate metabolism, and directly increases contractility through calcium-handling pathways independent of the adrenergic or calcium-channel receptors that are actually blocked. This is why HDI is described as metabolic support rather than a receptor-level reversal agent — it works around the blockade rather than displacing the offending drug.

Why HDI outperforms escalating vasopressors alone: catecholamine vasopressors raise blood pressure partly by increasing systemic vascular resistance, which increases the workload (and oxygen demand) of a heart that's already struggling to contract — potentially worsening perfusion even as the blood pressure number improves. HDI's vasodilatory and inotropic effects work in the opposite direction, improving cardiac output without the same afterload penalty, which is the physiologic basis for preferring it over simply pushing vasopressor doses higher.

Why the effect is delayed 15–60 minutes and other support must continue in parallel: insulin's inotropic mechanism involves several intracellular signaling steps (PI3-kinase activation, calcium-handling changes) rather than an immediate receptor-blocking reversal — it takes real time for those downstream effects to translate into measurable hemodynamic improvement. Stopping vasopressor or glucagon support the moment HDI starts, expecting instant benefit, risks a dangerous gap in support during that lag window.

Why hypoglycemia and hypokalemia are predictable, not incidental, complications: at the doses used in HDI — sometimes hundreds of units per hour — insulin's normal glucose- and potassium-lowering effects scale proportionally. This isn't a rare idiosyncratic reaction; it's the expected pharmacologic consequence of the dose being used, which is exactly why frequent glucose monitoring is built into every HDI protocol from the start rather than added reactively.

Why lipid emulsion specifically helps the more lipophilic agents: propranolol, verapamil, and diltiazem are more lipid-soluble than their hydrophilic counterparts (e.g., atenolol, which is largely renally cleared and less lipid-soluble) — the same "lipid sink/shuttle" mechanism used in LAST has a more plausible mechanistic basis for these specific agents, which is why lipid emulsion is considered more strongly for lipophilic BB/CCB overdose rather than applied uniformly across the entire drug class.

08

Workup & Disposition

Labs and imaging: glucose and potassium before and throughout HDI, ECG (QRS/QTc, conduction abnormalities), specific drug level if available and clinically useful, digoxin level if co-ingestion is possible. Bedside echocardiography to characterize the shock mechanism (cardiogenic vs. vasodilatory) and guide ongoing therapy choice.

ICU-level monitoring is standard for any patient requiring HDI, vasopressors, or lipid emulsion — this is not a floor-level management scenario given the intensity of monitoring HDI alone requires.

Weaning: once hemodynamically stable, wean the HDI infusion gradually (commonly halving the dose and observing for 2 hours before further reduction) rather than stopping abruptly — watch specifically for rebound hyperkalemia once the infusion is fully discontinued.

Consult toxicology/poison control early and throughout — this is a genuinely high-acuity, evidence-limited area (most HDI data is case-series level, with no completed RCTs) where real-time expert input meaningfully changes decision-making around escalation thresholds and agent-specific nuances.
