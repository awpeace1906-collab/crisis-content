Tricyclic Antidepressant Overdose

Sodium bicarbonate is the cornerstone — and it works through two separate mechanisms at once. Most patients presenting with this exact pattern today aren't actually on a TCA anymore, which matters for how you think about the differential.

01

Emergency Management & Immediate Actions

Bicarbonate for QRS widening comes before charcoal, before a quantitative level, before almost anything else — treat the ECG in front of you.
1
Secure the airway early if indicated, get an ECG immediately
Intubate for respiratory depression, altered mental status, or inability to protect the airway — before giving activated charcoal. Get a 12-lead or rhythm strip on arrival; QRS duration drives the entire next several steps.
2
Sodium bicarbonate for QRS >100 ms or any life-threatening cardiotoxicity
1–2 mEq/kg IV bolus (1–2 amps), repeated every 5–10 minutes as needed. Target arterial pH 7.45–7.55. If the QRS narrows and later widens again, repeat the bolus — this can happen multiple times over a prolonged course.
3
Never let respiratory acidosis develop — even transiently
If intubating, hyperventilate to help achieve alkalinization quickly, then reduce minute ventilation as serum bicarbonate rises to avoid excess alkalemia. A brief period of hypoventilation during a difficult intubation can measurably worsen an already unstable TCA-poisoned patient.
4
Benzodiazepines for seizures — avoid phenytoin
First-line for TCA-associated seizures, which tend to be single and short-lived. For refractory seizures: barbiturates, propofol, or general anesthesia. Avoid phenytoin — it's itself a sodium-channel blocker and will worsen the cardiotoxicity you're simultaneously trying to treat.
5
Hypotension — fluids and bicarbonate first, then direct-acting vasopressors
IV isotonic fluid boluses plus bicarbonate correct much early hypotension. Persistent hypotension: norepinephrine or phenylephrine (direct-acting agents) — avoid agents relying on indirect catecholamine release, since TCAs already deplete that mechanism through norepinephrine reuptake blockade.
6
QRS still wide after 4 amps of bicarbonate — consider hypertonic saline
100 mL of 3% saline as a next step when bicarbonate alone has reached its practical/safe ceiling (pH already near 7.5) but the QRS remains wide. Human evidence is limited but supportive; monitor serum sodium (keep <155) alongside pH.
7
Refractory ventricular arrhythmia — lidocaine
Reasonable when arrhythmia persists despite adequate bicarbonate therapy, expected to produce QRS narrowing and hemodynamic improvement. Magnesium can also be considered for persistent arrhythmias.
8
Refractory hemodynamic collapse or cardiac arrest — lipid emulsion, then ECMO
20% lipid emulsion 1.5 mL/kg (or 100 mL adult) bolus over 1 minute, repeatable for cardiac arrest or recurrent toxicity, followed by an infusion if response is achieved — the "lipid sink" mechanism sequestering lipophilic TCA molecules, same principle as LAST. VA-ECMO for cardiac arrest or refractory cardiogenic shock unresponsive to vasopressors, bicarbonate, lidocaine, and lipid emulsion; early consultation, before prolonged arrest, improves outcomes.
Activated charcoal is not a resuscitation priority

Give activated charcoal (1 g/kg, max 50 g, PO or via NG) only after the airway is secured and resuscitation priorities are addressed — typically reserved for ingestions >10 mg/kg presenting early. Charcoal never takes priority over stabilizing an unstable patient.

02

Brief Review

TCAs act through multiple receptor mechanisms simultaneously: cardiac sodium-channel blockade (producing QRS widening and depressed contractility — the dominant life-threatening mechanism), potassium-channel blockade (QT prolongation), GABA-A receptor inhibition (contributing to seizures), peripheral alpha-1 adrenergic blockade (vasodilation/hypotension), muscarinic acetylcholine antagonism (the anticholinergic toxidrome), and H1 histamine blockade (sedation). TCAs have a narrow therapeutic index — single-tablet fatalities have been historically reported — and prescribing has declined significantly with the rise of SSRIs, though TCA overdose-associated hospitalization has been trending upward in some recent data.

Onset is usually within an hour, with most symptoms appearing within 6 hours, but absorption can be unpredictable and delayed due to the anticholinergic effect slowing GI transit — a deceptively reassuring early presentation doesn't rule out later deterioration. Ingestions above roughly 10 mg/kg can produce coma, hypotension, seizures, and dysrhythmias; above 30 mg/kg, severe pH-dependent cardiotoxicity and coma lasting more than 24 hours should be expected.

Prevention & Set-Up (In Place of "Room Setup")

  • Get an ECG immediately on arrival for any suspected TCA ingestion, regardless of how the patient currently looks — the ECG, not symptoms, drives the decision to start bicarbonate.
  • Continuous cardiac monitoring for a minimum of 6 hours from ingestion, even in an initially asymptomatic patient with a normal early ECG — delayed absorption from anticholinergic-slowed GI transit makes early reassurance unreliable.
  • Have sodium bicarbonate and a lipid emulsion kit immediately accessible wherever overdose patients are managed — this isn't a rare-event kit to locate under pressure.
  • Avoid other sodium-channel-blocking or QT-prolonging drugs in a patient with confirmed or suspected TCA overdose — this includes phenytoin (as noted above) and Class Ia/Ic antiarrhythmics (procainamide, flecainide), which stack directly onto the existing blockade rather than treating it.
  • Call poison control early (1-800-222-1222 in the US) — TCA overdose management, especially around hypertonic saline timing and prolonged bicarbonate infusion duration, benefits from real-time toxicology input.

Rationale for TCA Overdose Management

Why sodium bicarbonate works through two mechanisms at once: the literature identifies both an increased extracellular sodium concentration (which can partially overcome the drug's competitive-like blockade at the sodium channel through simple mass action) and serum alkalinization (which reduces the ionized, protein-unbound fraction of TCA available to bind the channel in the first place) as contributing mechanisms — the exact proportional contribution of each remains debated, but current consensus is that both matter, which is why sodium loading and alkalinization are pursued together rather than treated as interchangeable.

Why even brief respiratory acidosis is treated as a genuine emergency within the emergency: acidemia increases the free, protein-unbound fraction of TCA in the blood, directly amplifying the drug's ability to bind and block cardiac sodium channels. This means a technically difficult intubation with even a short period of inadequate ventilation can measurably worsen cardiotoxicity in real time — the airway management itself becomes a moment of real cardiovascular risk, not just a routine procedural step.

Why phenytoin is specifically excluded from the seizure algorithm: phenytoin is itself a sodium-channel blocker, working through a mechanism that directly compounds the TCA's own channel blockade rather than treating the seizure through an unrelated pathway (as benzodiazepines, which act on GABA-A receptors, do instead). Reaching for a "standard" anticonvulsant reflexively here can make the primary cardiotoxicity worse while treating a secondary symptom.

Why direct-acting vasopressors are preferred over agents like dopamine: TCAs block presynaptic norepinephrine reuptake, which over time saturates and depletes the readily-releasable catecholamine pool at nerve terminals — conceptually similar to the catecholamine-depletion problem seen in chronic cocaine use (see that crisis file). Vasopressors that depend partly on triggering endogenous catecholamine release may be less effective against an already-depleted system, while direct alpha-agonists (norepinephrine, phenylephrine) act independent of that mechanism and remain reliably effective.

Why observation extends to a full 6 hours regardless of how the patient looks early on: the same anticholinergic properties driving the toxidrome also slow gastric emptying and GI transit, which can delay absorption of the ingested dose well beyond what a "typical" overdose timeline would predict. A patient with a reassuring ECG and no symptoms at 1–2 hours can still deteriorate later as delayed absorption catches up — which is why the discharge/medical-clearance criteria require the full 6-hour window with a normal ECG and mental status throughout, not just a single reassuring snapshot.

08

Workup & Disposition

ECG immediately and serially — this is the primary monitoring tool throughout the course, tracked far more closely than any drug level.

Quantitative TCA level is not clinically useful acutely — turnaround time is too slow to guide real-time decisions. A qualitative urine screen can support the diagnosis, and in prolonged cases, tracking urine screen positivity alongside QRS duration has been used to help guide when to wean a bicarbonate infusion, given that TCA elimination kinetics can be markedly unpredictable (delayed, saturable, and enterohepatically recirculated) at toxic doses.

Continuous cardiac monitoring for a minimum of 6 hours post-ingestion; patients with a normal ECG, normal mental status, and no cardiovascular instability or seizures throughout that window are medically clear for discharge or psychiatric evaluation. Patients with any abnormality need prolonged observation, and those requiring inotropic/vasopressor support or intubation need ICU-level care.

Mental health evaluation for any intentional overdose, once medically stable.

Disposition: ICU admission for any hemodynamically significant, seizure-complicated, or ECG-abnormal presentation; general medical/observation unit for minor, resolving abnormalities under continued cardiac monitoring.
