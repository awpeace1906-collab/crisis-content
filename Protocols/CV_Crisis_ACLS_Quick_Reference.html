01

The Circular Algorithm

Compressions and defibrillation are the highest-value actions in the whole algorithm — everything else is built around minimizing interruptions to them.
1
Start high-quality CPR immediately, attach the monitor/defibrillator
Begin compressions before the monitor is even on. Check rhythm as soon as available.
2
Shockable (VF/pVT) — shock immediately, then resume CPR with minimal pause
Single-shock approach preferred over stacked shocks. Prefer biphasic waveform. Use the device's recommended energy, escalating for later shocks if the defibrillator supports it. CPR for 2 minutes, then recheck rhythm.
3
Non-shockable (asystole/PEA) — epinephrine as soon as feasible
Give the first dose of epinephrine as early as possible in non-shockable rhythms — this is a specific 2025 emphasis point, distinct from the shockable pathway.
4
Shockable — give epinephrine after initial defibrillation attempts fail
The updated 2025 sequencing: prioritize rapid defibrillation over medication for shockable rhythms — epinephrine follows once shocks alone haven't converted the rhythm, commonly after the second shock.
5
Continue epinephrine every 3–5 minutes throughout
1 mg IV/IO, regardless of rhythm, once the pathway-specific timing above has been triggered.
6
Refractory VF/pVT after the third shock — add an antiarrhythmic
Amiodarone or lidocaine — see Numbers to Know for dosing. Neither is clearly favored over the other in current guidance; choose based on availability and institutional protocol.
7
Actively search for and treat reversible causes throughout — don't wait for a lull
The H's and T's aren't a checklist to run once things stall; they're a live differential to be working through continuously, especially in PEA and asystole where there's no shockable rhythm to anchor on. See the dedicated table below with links to full crisis files.
8
Consider advanced airway and capnography without delaying compressions
Airway placement should never come at the cost of compression quality or timing. Waveform capnography helps confirm placement, monitor CPR quality, and detect ROSC (a sustained PETCO₂ jump >25 mmHg, or >40 mmHg, suggests ROSC).
The "don't give this routinely" list — 2025 emphasis

Do not substitute or add vasopressin to epinephrine (no benefit). Do not use high-dose epinephrine routinely. Do not give calcium, sodium bicarbonate, magnesium, or steroids routinely during undifferentiated arrest — each has a role, but only for a specific indication (see Numbers to Know), not as a default add-on.

02

Brief Review

ACLS is the general-purpose algorithm for adult cardiac arrest — a cycle-based approach combining high-quality CPR, timely defibrillation for shockable rhythms, epinephrine, and a continuous search for reversible causes. The 2025 AHA update kept the overall structure stable while sharpening specifics: earlier epinephrine in non-shockable rhythms, epinephrine deliberately delayed until after initial defibrillation in shockable rhythms, and a clearer "don't add extras without a specific indication" stance on calcium, bicarbonate, magnesium, and steroids.

This file is the algorithm skeleton the rest of this crisis series plugs into. Where a trigger-specific pathway diverges meaningfully from standard ACLS — LAST, MH, hyperkalemia, tension pneumothorax, tamponade, PE, traumatic arrest, refractory VF/ECPR — that divergence is covered in its own dedicated file, cross-linked from the H's and T's table below rather than duplicated here.

Shockable vs. Non-Shockable — The First Branch Point

RhythmPathway
VF / pulseless VTShockable — immediate defibrillation, epinephrine after initial shocks fail, antiarrhythmic after 3 shocks if still refractory.
AsystoleNon-shockable — no defibrillation; epinephrine as early as possible; CPR and reversible-cause search are the entire treatment.
Pulseless electrical activity (PEA)Non-shockable — organized rhythm without a pulse; treatment is entirely dependent on identifying and fixing the underlying cause (see H's and T's below) — echo is invaluable here.
Refractory VF/pVT (persists after ≥3 shocks)See the dedicated Refractory VF/pVT & ECPR crisis file for vector-change defibrillation, DSED, and early ECPR candidacy assessment.
Asystole with P-wavesA distinct entity from flatline asystole — consider emergency transcutaneous/transvenous pacing rather than the standard non-shockable pathway alone.
04

CPR Quality Standards (In Place of "Room Setup")

  • Rate: 100–120 compressions per minute.
  • Depth: at least 2 inches (5 cm), up to 2.4 inches (6 cm).
  • Full chest recoil between compressions — leaning on the chest between compressions measurably reduces effectiveness.
  • Minimize interruptions — every pause reduces coronary and cerebral perfusion pressure, which then takes multiple subsequent compressions to rebuild.
  • Change compressor every 2 minutes, or sooner if fatigued — compression quality degrades with fatigue well before the compressor consciously feels tired.
  • Ventilation ratio: 30:2 without an advanced airway; 1 breath every 6 seconds (10/min) with continuous compressions once an advanced airway is placed. Avoid excessive ventilation — hyperventilation raises intrathoracic pressure and reduces venous return.
  • IV access preferred over IO for drug delivery, though IO is reasonable if IV can't be obtained quickly without delaying other key interventions.

06

Rationale for the 2025 Algorithm Changes

Why epinephrine timing now diverges by rhythm: in non-shockable rhythms, there's no competing intervention (no shock to prioritize) — early epinephrine gives the best chance of restoring perfusion, and pediatric data specifically shows shorter time-to-first-dose correlates with better outcomes. In shockable rhythms, the highest-value action is defibrillation itself; giving epinephrine first (or simultaneously) doesn't improve on rapid, uninterrupted shock delivery, and current evidence supports letting defibrillation attempts run their course first before adding the drug.

Why vasopressin was formally dropped: multiple systematic reviews and meta-analyses consistently found no survival advantage for vasopressin alone or in combination with epinephrine — a rare case of a once-common ACLS drug being explicitly removed rather than just deprioritized, based on accumulated null results rather than new harm data.

Why calcium, bicarbonate, magnesium, and steroids are "not routine" rather than "never": each has a real, evidence-supported role in a specific reversible-cause scenario (hyperkalemia for calcium, TCA toxicity for bicarbonate, torsades for magnesium) but no demonstrated benefit — and some plausible harm — when given reflexively in undifferentiated arrest. This is the clearest expression of the "diagnosis-driven resuscitation" theme running through this whole crisis series: the same drug is right or wrong depending entirely on whether the specific indication is actually present.

Why the H's and T's search happens continuously, not as a fallback: in PEA and asystole specifically, there's no rhythm-based intervention (no shock) doing any of the work — the entire treatment is finding and fixing the actual cause. Treating the differential search as something to start only after "standard measures fail" wastes the exact window where a reversible cause is still reversible.

08

Post-ROSC Care & Disposition

Immediate targets: MAP ≥65 mmHg (fluids first, then vasopressor infusion if needed), PaCO₂ 35–40 mmHg avoiding both hyper- and hypoventilation, SpO₂ 90–98% using the lowest FiO₂ that achieves it.

12-lead ECG and continuous monitoring to assess for STEMI; urgent coronary angiography with transfer to a cath-capable facility if indicated.

Continue searching for and treating reversible causes in the post-ROSC window — hypovolemia, hypoxia, acidosis, electrolyte derangement, hypothermia, tension pneumothorax, tamponade, toxins, and thrombosis all remain relevant after circulation returns, not just during the arrest.

Targeted temperature management per standard post-arrest protocol.

Disposition: ICU admission is standard for any resuscitated arrest, with close coordination between the resuscitation team and ICU on ongoing management. Institutions should have a standard of care in place to evaluate cardiac arrest survivors — including those unlikely to have a favorable outcome — for organ donation potential, per the 2025 update's new Class 1 recommendation, and to provide ongoing disability support for survivors.
