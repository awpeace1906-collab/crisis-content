01

Emergency Management & Immediate Actions

The dosing calculus flips between peri-arrest and true arrest — know which one you're in before you draw up the drug.
1
Cardiac arrest with suspected/known PE as the cause — full-dose systemic thrombolysis
Continue high-quality CPR and give thrombolytics without delay. The most consistently applied regimen is alteplase (tPA) 50 mg IV bolus. Ongoing CPR is not a contraindication — international resuscitation guidance is explicit that compressions should continue through and after administration.
2
Peri-arrest (not yet arrested), high-risk PE with cardiogenic shock — half-dose thrombolysis preferred when no absolute contraindication
Half-dose alteplase: 0.5 mg/kg (max 50 mg) — 10–20 mg bolus, remainder infused over 2 hours. Half-dose tenecteplase: 0.25 mg/kg single bolus (max 25 mg). Systemic thrombolytics are favored over catheter-based options specifically in the peri-arrest patient with no absolute contraindication, given the time-critical nature.
3
Guide redosing with bedside echocardiography
If ROSC is achieved but right ventricular dysfunction persists on echo, a second bolus (case reports describe a repeat 50 mg alteplase dose roughly 20 minutes after the first) has been used successfully to address ongoing clot burden — let the echo, not a fixed protocol alone, guide whether more is needed.
4
Continue CPR for an extended period after thrombolysis
The drug needs time to work — hemodynamic improvement may not be immediate. There's no firm consensus duration in the literature, but extending resuscitation efforts well beyond a typical arrest timeline (commonly practiced in the 60–90 minute range post-lytic) before considering termination is standard practice specifically because of this delayed-effect pharmacology.
5
Consider nebulized pulmonary vasodilators as an adjunct
Nebulized nitroglycerin (50 mg/50 mL, 5 mL nebulized over 15 min) or nebulized milrinone (10 mg/mL, 5 mL over 15 min) have been used in cardiac arrest and peri-arrest PE based on limited data — reasonable adjuncts, not a substitute for thrombolysis or definitive therapy.
6
Escalate to mechanical/surgical options if thrombolysis fails or is contraindicated
Aspiration/mechanical thrombectomy, surgical embolectomy, or VA-ECMO as a bridge — all documented rescue options in refractory cases, particularly when a contraindication (e.g., recent major surgery, active bleeding) rules out lysis.
7
Activate a Pulmonary Embolism Response Team (PERT) if available
Now a Class 1 recommendation in the 2026 AHA/ACC guideline specifically to improve timeliness of care — involve them early rather than after initial stabilization attempts have already run their course.
The evidence is honestly weaker than the confidence it's given

ILCOR's 2020 consensus lists thrombolysis for suspected PE-caused arrest as a weak recommendation with very low-certainty evidence. A large blinded RCT of rescue thrombolysis in undifferentiated PEA arrest showed no benefit — but PE-specific registries and case series suggest real benefit when the diagnosis is genuinely likely. The practice is followed because the alternative, in a patient who is otherwise going to die, carries little additional downside — not because the evidence is strong.

02

Brief Review

Acute pulmonary embolism causes a sudden rise in pulmonary vascular resistance, producing right ventricular pressure overload, dilation, and impaired systolic function. The failing right ventricle reduces left ventricular filling (via septal shift and reduced venous return through the pulmonary circuit), causing a fall in cardiac output that can progress to cardiogenic/obstructive shock and cardiac arrest — most commonly presenting as pulseless electrical activity.

The first dedicated AHA/ACC clinical practice guideline for acute PE (2026) replaces the older, blunt "massive/submassive" terminology with a new A-through-E severity classification based on the most severe clinical, laboratory, and imaging finding — Category E1 (cardiopulmonary failure with recurrent or persistent hypotension/cardiogenic shock) corresponds roughly to the old "massive" category and carries a Class 2a recommendation for systemic thrombolysis; Category D represents incipient cardiopulmonary failure. PE is estimated to be a contributing cause in a meaningful share of cardiac arrests overall, alongside acute MI.

Set-Up & Readiness (In Place of "Room Setup")

  • Have a thrombolytic immediately accessible in any resuscitation area — the time to locate and reconstitute the drug is time the patient doesn't have once arrest occurs.
  • Bedside echo capability for real-time diagnostic support and to guide redosing decisions after an initial bolus.
  • Know your institution's PERT activation pathway before you need it — a Class 1 recommendation now, meaning this should be a routine reflex for high-risk PE, not a rare escalation.
  • Label thrombolytic doses clearly for indication — institutions using tenecteplase across stroke, STEMI, and PE indications benefit from clear dose labeling on the drug itself, since the correct dose varies meaningfully by indication and getting it wrong under pressure is a real risk.
  • Identify high-risk patients proactively — recent surgery, malignancy, prolonged immobilization — so that a sudden collapse in this population triggers PE-specific suspicion immediately rather than defaulting to a generic arrest workup.

Rationale for Peri-Arrest PE Management

Why the dose flips from half to full between peri-arrest and true arrest: half-dose regimens exist specifically to reduce bleeding risk in a patient who is still perfusing and has time for a more conservative approach. Once circulation has actually stopped, the calculus changes entirely — the patient is going to die imminently without rapid, maximal clot dissolution, and the marginal bleeding risk of a full dose becomes a secondary concern relative to the near-certainty of death without aggressive intervention.

Why ongoing CPR isn't a contraindication to giving the drug: a natural instinct is to worry that chest compressions during or after thrombolysis increase bleeding or trauma risk. Current resuscitation guidance explicitly rejects this concern for PE-suspected arrest — compressions may even mechanically help fragment and distribute the thrombus, and stopping CPR to "protect" against a theoretical bleeding risk guarantees the more immediate risk of death from ongoing circulatory arrest.

Why extended CPR duration after thrombolysis is standard practice despite no formal consensus number: thrombolytic drugs take time to actually dissolve the obstructing clot and restore pulmonary blood flow — a resuscitation that looks unsuccessful in the first several minutes after the bolus may simply not have had enough time yet. This is conceptually similar to hyperkalemic arrest (see that crisis file): the underlying cause is being actively treated in real time, which justifies continuing well past when a typical arrest would prompt termination.

Why the negative TROICA trial result doesn't overturn the PE-specific approach: TROICA tested rescue thrombolysis in undifferentiated PEA arrest of unknown cause — a population where the large majority didn't actually have PE, diluting any true PE-specific benefit into statistical noise. Subsequent registries and case series specifically selecting patients with known or strongly suspected PE (like the French National Cardiac Arrest Registry) show a real survival signal. The lesson isn't "thrombolysis doesn't work for arrest" — it's that diagnostic specificity is what determines whether the intervention actually helps, which is exactly the diagnosis-driven framing this whole crisis series is built around.

Why bedside echo drives redosing rather than a fixed protocol: a single 50 mg bolus dissolves a variable amount of thrombus depending on clot burden and time since embolization — persistent RV dysfunction after apparent ROSC is direct evidence that meaningful obstruction remains, which is a more physiologically grounded trigger for a second dose than an arbitrary fixed-protocol cutoff.

08

Workup & Disposition

Confirm once stabilized: CT pulmonary angiography as soon as the patient is stable enough to transport, since thrombolysis in the acute setting is typically given on clinical/echo suspicion rather than confirmed imaging.

Anticoagulation after thrombolysis: transition to a heparin infusion for ongoing anticoagulation once the acute lytic phase is complete, per standard PE management principles.

Monitor for bleeding: close neurologic and hemodynamic monitoring for the bleeding complications inherent to thrombolytic therapy, particularly intracranial hemorrhage.

Escalation if incomplete response: catheter-directed therapy, mechanical thrombectomy, or surgical embolectomy for persistent right heart strain or hemodynamic compromise despite systemic lysis — increasingly available and structured under the 2026 guideline's risk-stratified framework.

Disposition: ICU admission for any patient post-arrest or post-thrombolysis for PE, with PERT/multidisciplinary involvement continuing through the acute and subacute phases, including decisions about IVC filter placement if anticoagulation is later contraindicated.
