Emergency Management & Immediate Actions

Escalate uterotonics in rapid succession while working the 4 T's in parallel — atony is the most common cause, but don't stop looking once you've treated it.
1
Activate the hemorrhage protocol, call for help
Start quantitative blood loss measurement (calibrated drapes/canisters) rather than relying on visual estimation — this single change, bundled with structured protocol response, has been shown to meaningfully reduce severe hemorrhage and death.
2
Uterine massage and bimanual compression
First mechanical step for atony — the most common cause of PPH by a wide margin. Bimanual compression is also a reasonable temporizing measure while other therapies are mobilized.
3
Oxytocin — first-line uterotonic
10–40 units in 500–1,000 mL IV as a continuous infusion, or 10 units IM if IV access isn't yet established.
4
Add a second-line uterotonic in rapid succession if bleeding continues
No evidence favors one over another — choice is driven by contraindication profile. Methylergonovine 0.2 mg IM q2–4h (avoid with hypertension). Carboprost (15-methyl PGF2α) 0.25 mg IM or intramyometrial q15min, up to 8 doses (avoid with asthma). Misoprostol — reserve for patients with both asthma and hypertension, given inconsistent efficacy and higher side-effect burden compared to the others.
5
Tranexamic acid — within 3 hours of birth
1 g IV over 10 minutes, second dose if bleeding continues after 30 minutes or restarts within 24 hours of the first dose. Give when initial uterotonic therapy fails to control bleeding — associated with roughly a 30% reduction in bleeding-related mortality when given within this window.
6
Work the 4 T's in parallel, not just after atony treatment fails
Examine for retained placental tissue, genital tract lacerations, and uterine rupture while uterotonics are being given — treating atony alone in a patient actually bleeding from trauma or retained tissue wastes critical time. See the dedicated differential table below.
7
Uterine tamponade for bleeding refractory to uterotonics
Balloon device (Bakri, Foley, condom catheter, or commercial system) filled with 250–500 mL saline, or gauze packing (often thrombin- or TXA-soaked). Placed before advanced shock develops, tamponade controls the large majority of otherwise-refractory bleeding with near-zero mortality — this is not a last-resort measure, it's most effective used early.
8
Activate Massive Transfusion Protocol and escalate surgically as needed
See the dedicated MTP crisis file for ratio-based resuscitation. Surgical escalation — compression sutures (B-Lynch), uterine artery ligation, uterine artery embolization (interventional radiology), and hysterectomy as a last resort — should not be delayed while waiting to correct coagulopathy if there's an ongoing surgical bleeding source.
Normal vital signs can be deceptively reassuring

Pregnancy's expanded blood volume and cardiac reserve let patients compensate for substantial blood loss while heart rate and blood pressure still look normal. By the time vitals clearly deteriorate, a dangerous volume may already be lost — track quantitative blood loss and escalate on that number, not just on the monitor.

02

Brief Review

Postpartum hemorrhage is traditionally defined as blood loss exceeding 500 mL after vaginal delivery or 1,000 mL after cesarean delivery; ACOG's 2017 redefinition instead uses cumulative blood loss ≥1,000 mL with signs/symptoms of hypovolemia within 24 hours of birth, regardless of delivery mode — reflecting how consistently blood loss at delivery is underestimated visually. PPH remains the leading cause of maternal mortality worldwide, though it's substantially preventable with timely recognition and a coordinated, protocolized response.

The most common cause by far is uterine atony (roughly 70–80% of cases), followed by genital tract trauma, retained placental tissue, and coagulopathy — commonly organized under the "4 T's" mnemonic (Tone, Trauma, Tissue, Thrombin). A second uterotonic beyond oxytocin is required in an estimated 3–25% of PPH cases, underscoring that atony frequently doesn't resolve with a single intervention.

Prevention & Set-Up (In Place of "Room Setup")

  • Active management of the third stage of labor (AMTSL) — prophylactic uterotonic administration, controlled cord traction, and fundal massage — is the primary prevention strategy for every delivery, not just high-risk ones.
  • Identify risk factors in advance: prior PPH, multiple gestation, polyhydramnios, prolonged labor, chorioamnionitis, and macrosomia all raise risk and should prompt heightened readiness, though PPH also occurs unpredictably in low-risk patients.
  • Have organized, systematic hospital-wide response processes in place — ACOG specifically recommends this rather than leaving PPH response to individual provider judgment in the moment.
  • Stock uterine balloon tamponade and vacuum-induced hemorrhage-control devices and keep them immediately accessible to the delivery team, per ACOG clinical guidance.
  • Adopt calibrated, quantitative blood loss measurement as standard practice — the evidence supporting this as a bundle-level intervention (not just a documentation nicety) is strong enough that it's now considered a core prevention/early-detection strategy in its own right.
  • Ensure MTP readiness — see the dedicated Massive Transfusion Protocol crisis file for blood bank activation pathways and ratio-based resuscitation.

Rationale for PPH Management

Why quantitative blood loss measurement changed outcomes at a population level: the E-MOTIVE trial demonstrated that calibrated, objective blood-loss detection combined with a structured response bundle substantially reduces severe hemorrhage, laparotomy, and bleeding-related death after vaginal birth. Visual estimation is notoriously inaccurate — blood mixes with amniotic fluid and other fluids, and providers systematically underestimate loss — meaning a purely visual approach delays the exact moment escalation should happen. This is why quantitative measurement is treated as a core intervention in its own right, not just better documentation.

Why TXA's benefit is time-locked to a 3-hour window: this mirrors the same time-dependent antifibrinolytic principle seen in trauma resuscitation (see the MTP crisis file) — TXA works by inhibiting an actively occurring fibrinolytic process. The WOMAN trial's mortality benefit was specifically demonstrated within this window; later administration hasn't shown the same benefit, which is why "within 3 hours of birth" is written directly into the dosing guidance rather than treated as a soft suggestion.

Why no single second-line uterotonic is preferred over another: methylergonovine, carboprost, and misoprostol all work toward the same end (myometrial contraction) through different receptor pathways, and head-to-head trial data hasn't established clear superiority among them. This is why the actual decision is driven by each patient's specific contraindications (hypertension ruling out methylergonovine, asthma ruling out carboprost) rather than by any presumed efficacy ranking.

Why tamponade is framed as an early intervention, not a last resort: mechanical compression works best before the coagulopathy and vascular collapse of advanced shock have set in — placing a balloon while the patient is still physiologically stable gives it the best chance to actually control bleeding, which is exactly why the evidence shows such strong outcomes (near-zero mortality, over 80% success) specifically when it's used before shock develops rather than after exhausting every other option first.

Why pregnancy's physiologic reserve makes vital signs a lagging indicator here specifically: the roughly 30–50% expansion in blood volume during pregnancy, combined with a young, otherwise healthy patient population, means compensatory mechanisms can maintain a normal-looking heart rate and blood pressure well into what would be a dangerous blood loss in a non-pregnant patient. This is the same physiologic principle that makes quantitative blood loss tracking more valuable than vital-sign trending as the primary escalation trigger — by the time the vitals clearly show trouble, the patient has already used up much of the reserve that was masking the problem.

08

Workup & Disposition

Labs: CBC, coagulation panel with fibrinogen, type and crossmatch, and quantitative blood loss tracking throughout. Bedside ultrasound can help assess for retained products of conception or an evolving hematoma.

Escalation pathway: interventional radiology consultation for uterine artery embolization when hemodynamically feasible; surgical team involvement early rather than as a delayed last resort, since delaying definitive surgical control while attempting to first correct coagulopathy can itself worsen outcomes in an ongoing surgical bleed.

Disposition: high-acuity labor-and-delivery or ICU-level monitoring for any patient requiring more than first-line uterotonic therapy; continue close observation for delayed bleeding (uterotonic effect wearing off, retained tissue becoming apparent later) even after apparent initial control.

Debrief and documentation: given how rapidly PPH can escalate and how much depends on early, coordinated response, structured post-event debrief helps both the immediate care team and institutional protocol refinement — treat this the same way any other high-stakes, relatively infrequent crisis in this series gets reviewed afterward.
