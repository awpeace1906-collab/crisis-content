Local Anesthetic Systemic Toxicity

CNS and cardiovascular collapse from local anesthetic overdose or inadvertent intravascular injection — the lipid rescue protocol, why the code drugs change, and how to keep it from happening in the first place.

01

Emergency Management & Immediate Actions

You have roughly a 3-minute window from recognition to lipid emulsion running before hypoxia and acidosis make the cardiac blockade harder to reverse.
1
Stop injecting local anesthetic, call for help
Get the LAST rescue kit (lipid emulsion, syringes, checklist). Ask for a "designated reader" to run the ASRA checklist out loud — improves adherence under stress.
2
Manage the airway
100% O₂, prevent hypoxia and acidosis — both potentiate LAST. Low threshold for intubation if seizing or obtunded.
3
Suppress seizures with benzodiazepines
First-line. If only propofol is available, use small 20 mg increments — larger doses worsen cardiovascular depression on top of the LA-induced depression already present. Propofol is not a substitute for lipid emulsion; its lipid content is far too low to act as one.
4
Start 20% lipid emulsion — ASRA 2020 regimen
≥70 kg: bolus 100 mL over 2–3 min, then infuse 250 mL over 15–20 min.
<70 kg: bolus 1.5 mL/kg over 2–3 min, then infuse 0.25 mL/kg/min (consider a pump if <40 kg).
If unstable: repeat the bolus and double the infusion rate. Maximum total dose 12 mL/kg. Continue ≥15 minutes once stable. Order of bolus vs. infusion isn't critical — get it running.
5
Modify ACLS — this is not a standard arrest
Epinephrine, if needed for arrhythmia or hypotension, at a reduced dose <1 mcg/kg — standard 1 mg ACLS boluses can trigger lethal arrhythmias in an LA-poisoned heart. Avoid vasopressin, calcium channel blockers, beta-blockers, and additional local anesthetic as antiarrhythmics — all worsen outcomes in this specific arrest physiology.
6
If ACLS + lipid emulsion fail — alert for CPB/ECMO
Call the nearest facility capable of cardiopulmonary bypass early; prolonged resuscitation has a track record of good outcomes in LAST specifically, so don't terminate on the usual arrest timeline.
7
Monitor after stabilization
Continue lipid infusion ≥15 minutes past hemodynamic stability. Watch for recurrence — LA redistribution can cause delayed relapse.
Standard code doses of epinephrine can kill this patient

1 mg epinephrine boluses are appropriate for a normal arrest and dangerous in LAST. Reduced dosing (<1 mcg/kg) and avoidance of vasopressin/CCB/BB is a deliberate, LAST-specific deviation from standard ACLS — not an error.

02

Brief Review

LAST results from elevated plasma concentrations of local anesthetic — most often from inadvertent intravascular injection during a nerve block, less often from cumulative absorption after a large-volume block or infusion. Local anesthetics block sodium channels everywhere, not just at the injection site: at toxic plasma levels they disrupt CNS inhibitory/excitatory balance and cardiac conduction simultaneously.

Estimated incidence is roughly 1.8 per 1,000 peripheral nerve blocks by signs/symptoms, and closer to 1 in 384 surgeries with a block by lipid-emulsion-use as a surrogate marker in some populations — for a "rare" event, not actually rare. Lidocaine is the most commonly implicated agent overall (44% of reported cases, reflecting its frequency of use), but bupivacaine carries the narrowest safety margin and the greatest cardiotoxicity per dose; ropivacaine has a comparatively better safety profile.

Initial Signs and Symptoms

StageFindings
Early / prodromalCircumoral numbness, metallic taste, tinnitus, lightheadedness — often the first warning, easy to miss under drapes or in a sedated patient.
CNS excitationVisual disturbance, agitation, muscle twitching, progressing to generalized tonic-clonic seizure.
CNS depression (can follow rapidly)Drowsiness, obtundation — especially with bupivacaine, where cardiac and CNS toxicity can present nearly simultaneously.
CardiovascularBradycardia, conduction block on ECG, hypotension, ventricular arrhythmias, cardiac arrest.
Atypical presentationsOccur — CNS and CV signs don't always appear in the "expected" order, and severe cardiotoxicity can be the presenting sign with no preceding CNS symptoms, particularly with bupivacaine or in a patient under general anesthesia who can't report prodromal symptoms.
TimingMost cases begin within minutes of injection, but severe manifestations have been reported up to 6 hours later — keep suspicion up for delayed presentation, not just the immediate post-injection window.
04

Prevention & Set-Up (In Place of "Room Setup")

  • Every site using LA at toxic-capable doses stocks 20% lipid emulsion and basic resuscitation equipment — not just the OR. Outpatient and procedural settings are exactly where delayed rescue kills.
  • Aspirate before injecting, use incremental dosing rather than a single large bolus, and use ultrasound guidance for regional blocks — ultrasound guidance measurably reduces LAST risk versus landmark/nerve-stimulator technique.
  • Calculate the maximum dose before you draw it up, using ideal body weight, not actual weight, in patients where the two diverge significantly.
  • Reduce dose at the extremes of age (under 4 months or over 70 years) and in patients with cardiac conduction defects, ischemic heart disease, or hepatic dysfunction — generally 10–20% dose reduction.
  • Have the LAST checklist physically posted or in the kit — a printed/electronic checklist and a designated reader measurably improves adherence during an actual event.
  • Toxicity is additive across agents — account for cumulative dose if more than one local anesthetic is used in the same case or within the same 4-hour window.

Maximum Recommended Doses by Agent

There's no lab that predicts LAST before it happens — the equivalent "numbers to know" here are the weight-based ceilings that keep you from causing it.
AgentMax dose aloneMax dose with epinephrine
Bupivacaine / Levobupivacaine2 mg/kg3 mg/kg
Ropivacaine3 mg/kg~3.5 mg/kg (less consistently increased by epi than others)
Lidocaine4.5–5 mg/kg7 mg/kg
Mepivacaine5 mg/kg7 mg/kg
Prilocaine6 mg/kg8 mg/kg (methemoglobinemia risk above ~8 mg/kg regardless of toxicity)
Relative cardiotoxicity, high to low: bupivacaine > levobupivacaine ≈ ropivacaine > lidocaine ≈ mepivacaine. Lidocaine and mepivacaine tend to reduce cardiac contractility more; bupivacaine and ropivacaine tend to cause more malignant arrhythmias — different failure modes, both dangerous.
06

Rationale for LAST Management Steps

Why lipid emulsion works — the "lipid sink/shuttle" mechanism: local anesthetics are lipophilic. Infused lipid emulsion was originally thought to simply sequester ("sink") free drug out of plasma, pulling it away from the heart and brain. Current understanding favors a "shuttle" model instead: lipid redistributes local anesthetic away from high-perfusion, high-toxicity-risk organs (heart, brain) toward storage/metabolism sites (muscle, liver). Lipid also has an independent cardiotonic effect — increased contractility and blood pressure support — separate from its sequestration role, which is part of why it works even before drug redistribution is complete.

Why epinephrine dosing is reduced, not eliminated: epinephrine is still used for hemodynamic support, but LA-blocked myocardium responds differently to catecholamine surges than a normal ischemic arrest — full ACLS doses have been linked to worsened arrhythmia and reduced effectiveness of lipid rescue in animal and case-report data, which is the basis for the <1 mcg/kg recommendation.

Why vasopressin, CCBs, and beta-blockers are avoided: the LA-poisoned heart is already profoundly depressed at the sodium-channel level. Vasopressin has been associated with worsened outcomes (including pulmonary hemorrhage) in this specific setting; CCBs and beta-blockers add further conduction/contractility depression on top of an already-blocked heart, risking irreversible asystole.

Why benzodiazepines over propofol for seizures: propofol causes independent cardiovascular depression, which compounds the LA-induced depression already present — exactly the wrong direction when the heart is the organ you're trying to protect. It also doesn't carry enough lipid to function as rescue therapy, despite superficially "being a lipid."

Why resuscitation is prolonged beyond a normal arrest timeline: case reports and registry data show good neurologic outcomes after unusually long resuscitations in LAST arrests when lipid emulsion and modified ACLS are used correctly — the pathophysiology is reversible in a way a primary cardiac arrest often isn't, which justifies escalating to CPB/ECMO rather than terminating on standard timelines.

Differential Diagnosis

MimicKey distinguishing features
Vasovagal / syncopal reaction to the procedure itselfBradycardia and hypotension without seizure or the classic LAST prodrome; resolves quickly with positioning and reassurance, not lipid emulsion.
Epinephrine reaction (from LA-epi mixture)Tachycardia, hypertension, anxiety — opposite hemodynamic direction from LAST cardiotoxicity; timing immediately post-injection, self-limited.
Anaphylaxis to the local anesthetic or a co-administered drugTrue LA allergy is rare; look for urticaria, angioedema, bronchospasm rather than the CNS-first LAST pattern. Co-administered antibiotics are a more common culprit than the LA itself.
High or total spinal (after neuraxial technique)Rapidly ascending sensory/motor block, hypotension, bradycardia, potential respiratory arrest — mechanical spread of block rather than systemic toxicity; different treatment pathway (airway/hemodynamic support, not lipid emulsion).
Primary seizure disorder / eclampsia (obstetric setting)No temporal relationship to LA administration; other eclampsia features (hypertension, proteinuria) present.
Methemoglobinemia (prilocaine/benzocaine)Cyanosis and low SpO₂ that doesn't correct with O₂, pulse ox reading in the high-80s/low-90s regardless of FiO₂ — different mechanism, different antidote (methylene blue), not lipid emulsion.
08

Workup & Disposition

Acute phase: LAST is a clinical diagnosis based on temporal relationship to LA administration plus the CNS/CV pattern above — there's no confirmatory point-of-care test. Serum LA levels can be sent but rarely change acute management.

Monitoring: continuous ECG, blood pressure, and pulse oximetry through resuscitation and recovery. Repeat neuro checks once sedation/seizure activity clears.

Disposition: admit for monitoring after any LAST event with cardiovascular involvement or seizure — recurrence after apparent stabilization has been reported due to LA redistribution. Isolated, rapidly resolved prodromal symptoms without seizure or arrhythmia may be observed for a shorter period per institutional protocol, but err toward longer observation with any cardiac involvement.

After the event: document the total LA dose given and route, report the event per institutional/departmental safety process, and review the case for a modifiable cause (dose, technique, ultrasound use) before the next block.
