01

Emergency Management & Immediate Actions

Ventilate first, reverse second — naloxone treats respiratory depression, not unconsciousness, and bag-mask ventilation buys time regardless of dose or route available.
1
Support the airway and ventilation immediately
100% FiO₂ with bag-valve-mask ventilation before or alongside naloxone administration — this is the actual life-saving intervention while naloxone takes effect, and matters even if naloxone is delayed or unavailable.
2
Opioid-naive or unknown history — standard dosing
Naloxone 0.4 mg IV/IM/SC (or 2–4 mg intranasal), repeated every 2–3 minutes as needed. Higher intranasal formulations (8 mg) exist but aren't associated with better outcomes than 4 mg — only more severe withdrawal when it does occur.
3
Known or suspected chronic opioid use — go low and slow
0.04–0.1 mg IV initial dose, titrated upward every 1–3 minutes only as needed to restore adequate respiration. The goal is reversing respiratory depression, not fully waking the patient — overshooting risks severe, distressing precipitated withdrawal without added clinical benefit.
4
Cardiac arrest — standard resuscitation, naloxone if pulse status uncertain
Follow standard ACLS for pulseless patients; give naloxone if there's genuine uncertainty about pulse presence, but don't let naloxone delay chest compressions in a confirmed arrest — see the ACLS Quick Reference crisis file.
5
Suspected buprenorphine overdose — higher dose needed
Buprenorphine's high mu-receptor affinity and slow dissociation resist standard reversal. Bolus 2–3 mg IV, followed by a continuous infusion of 4 mg/hr — expect complete reversal within 40–60 minutes with this approach, and expect delayed onset compared to a typical full-agonist overdose.
6
Repeated re-sedation — start an infusion
A continuous naloxone infusion at roughly two-thirds the bolus dose that achieved arousal, per hour, is appropriate for patients requiring repeated boluses — common with longer-acting opioids (methadone, fentanyl patches, buprenorphine) whose duration outlasts naloxone's own half-life.
7
Manage precipitated withdrawal if it occurs
Uncomfortable but not typically life-threatening — supportive care (IV fluids for nausea/vomiting/diarrhea, benzodiazepines for anxiety, alpha-2 agonists like clonidine or dexmedetomidine, antiemetics). Rare severe cases (stress cardiomyopathy, significant dehydration) may need higher-acuity monitoring.
8
Watch for non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema
A recognized, uncommon complication of naloxone administration, possibly related to a sympathetic surge/catecholamine release from abrupt reversal — supportive management (oxygen, positioning, diuresis if genuinely fluid-overloaded) rather than withholding further naloxone if respiratory depression recurs.
The goal is breathing, not a fully alert patient

Titrating naloxone until someone is wide awake and irritated overshoots the actual clinical target. Enough naloxone to restore adequate respiration and airway protection is the endpoint — additional dose beyond that only raises the risk and severity of precipitated withdrawal without added safety benefit.

02

Brief Review

Opioid overdose produces a classic triad of CNS depression, respiratory depression, and miosis (though some opioids — meperidine, propoxyphene — don't reliably cause pinpoint pupils). Death results from respiratory depression progressing to apnea and hypoxic arrest, not from a direct cardiotoxic effect in most cases. Naloxone is a competitive mu-, kappa-, and delta-opioid receptor antagonist with no intrinsic opioid activity of its own — it displaces the causative opioid from receptors rather than metabolically clearing it, which is why it can be overwhelmed by ongoing opioid presence once naloxone itself wears off.

Naloxone's own half-life (roughly 30–80 minutes in adults) is shorter than many of the opioids it's used to reverse — methadone, fentanyl (including transdermal formulations), and buprenorphine in particular can all outlast a single naloxone dose, producing re-sedation once the antagonist effect fades. This mismatch is the single most important pharmacokinetic fact governing post-reversal monitoring.

Prevention & Set-Up (In Place of "Room Setup")

  • Have naloxone immediately accessible in any setting where opioids are administered or where overdose presentations are plausible — IV, IM, and intranasal formulations all have a role depending on access and setting.
  • Default to low-dose titration when chronic opioid use is likely — a quick history (pain management, known OUD, visible fentanyl patch) should shift the starting dose before the first bolus, not after a bad reaction to a standard dose.
  • Nalmefene is not a routine substitute for naloxone — the American College of Medical Toxicology and American Academy of Clinical Toxicology have taken a position statement against replacing naloxone as the primary antidote given its longer half-life (4–8 hours) and less-established safety profile in synthetic-opioid overdose, despite recent post-marketing data suggesting the precipitated-withdrawal signal may be lower than initially feared.
  • Plan monitoring duration around the causative opioid's half-life, not naloxone's — a patient reversed after a long-acting opioid needs a substantially longer observation window than naloxone's own duration of action would suggest.
  • Offer buprenorphine initiation after reversal where systems support it (e.g., the "ODNaloxoneBup" protocol model) — early medication-assisted treatment engagement immediately post-overdose has shown promise for reducing recurrence, using a mixed-agonist transition strategy rather than waiting for a separate visit.

06

Rationale for Opioid Overdose Management

Why the endpoint is respiration, not consciousness: naloxone's job is displacing opioid from mu-receptors enough to restore adequate ventilatory drive — full reversal of all opioid binding isn't necessary and isn't the target. Pushing dose further than that specifically to "wake the patient up" only increases receptor displacement further, which is exactly what triggers precipitated withdrawal in someone physically dependent on opioids.

Why low-and-slow titration matters specifically in chronic users: a patient with ongoing physical dependence has receptor and downstream adaptations calibrated to continuous opioid exposure. Abrupt, complete receptor displacement removes that exposure instantly rather than gradually, producing an acute withdrawal syndrome the body hasn't had time to buffer — titrating slowly gives just enough antagonism to restore breathing while minimizing the abruptness of that displacement.

Why the naloxone-outlasted-by-the-opioid problem drives monitoring duration: because naloxone is a competitive antagonist with its own pharmacokinetic clearance, once naloxone's blood level falls below the causative opioid's, that opioid re-occupies receptors and respiratory depression can recur — a phenomenon called re-narcotization. This is a pharmacokinetic mismatch problem, not a treatment failure, and it's the entire basis for extended observation after reversal with long-acting agents.

Why buprenorphine needs a fundamentally different dosing strategy: buprenorphine binds mu-receptors with unusually high affinity and dissociates slowly — a standard naloxone dose simply can't out-compete it at the receptor the way it can displace a typical full agonist. The higher bolus-plus-infusion approach is specifically calibrated to overcome that binding characteristic rather than being an arbitrary "give more because it's a stronger drug" escalation.

Why higher-dose naloxone/nalmefene products aren't simply "better": real-world data comparing naloxone doses hasn't shown improved reversal outcomes with higher doses — what it has shown is more frequent and more severe precipitated withdrawal. This is why current expert consensus (reflected in "compassionate overdose response" guidance) favors the lowest effective dose rather than defaulting to the highest available formulation, even as synthetic opioids have grown more potent.

Differential Diagnosis

MimicKey distinguishing features
Sedative-hypnotic overdose (benzodiazepines, barbiturates, GHB)Respiratory depression without reliable pupillary miosis; no response to naloxone since these act on different receptor systems — flumazenil is a distinct and higher-risk consideration for benzodiazepines specifically, not routinely recommended in undifferentiated overdose.
Alcohol intoxicationCharacteristic odor, no pinpoint pupils typically, no response to naloxone alone — though co-ingestion with opioids is common and naloxone should still be given if opioid use is plausible.
Traumatic brain injury / intracranial eventFocal neurologic findings, unequal pupils, or a clear trauma mechanism should raise this alternative, especially if naloxone produces no response despite adequate dosing.
HypoglycemiaCheck a point-of-care glucose in any depressed-consciousness presentation regardless of suspected opioid involvement — cheap, fast, and treatable.
Clonidine or other alpha-2 agonist overdoseCan mimic opioid toxicity closely (sedation, respiratory depression, sometimes miosis) — history and lack of full naloxone response are the distinguishing clues.
Carbon monoxide poisoningAltered mental status without the reliable respiratory-depression-plus-miosis pattern; check carboxyhemoglobin if suspected, especially with multiple simultaneous patients from the same environment.
08

Workup & Disposition

Confirm and characterize: point-of-care glucose, pulse oximetry/ABG for oxygenation status, ECG if a specific agent (e.g., methadone with QT effects) is suspected. Urine toxicology can support the diagnosis but shouldn't delay treatment and often doesn't change acute management.

Observation duration: minimum 6–12 hours post-reversal, extended further for known long-acting opioid exposure (methadone, fentanyl patches, buprenorphine) given the re-narcotization risk described above.

Harm reduction and treatment engagement: every reversed overdose is an opportunity — take-home naloxone kits, discussion of medication-assisted treatment (buprenorphine, methadone), and connection to substance use treatment resources meaningfully reduce future overdose risk and are increasingly considered standard of care at the point of reversal, not a separate referral to arrange later.

Disposition: ED/observation-level monitoring is standard for any reversed overdose; ICU-level care for those requiring a naloxone infusion, experiencing significant complications (aspiration, pulmonary edema), or with a co-ingestion complicating the picture.
