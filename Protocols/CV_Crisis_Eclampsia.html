Eclampsia & Severe Preeclampsia

Magnesium sulfate is neuroprotective, not an antihypertensive — the two goals need different drugs, and the toxicity monitoring is bedside clinical exam, not a lab value you wait on.

01

Emergency Management & Immediate Actions

Two parallel tracks, not one: stop/prevent seizures with magnesium, and treat severe hypertension as its own time-critical emergency with a different drug entirely.
1
Active seizure — magnesium sulfate first-line
4–6 g IV loading dose over 20–30 minutes (or 4 g over 5–10 minutes specifically for active seizure per some protocols), then maintenance infusion 1–2 g/hr. If IV access can't be obtained/maintained: 10 g IM (5 g in each buttock), then 5 g IM q4h. Magnesium is dramatically more effective than benzodiazepines or phenytoin for this specific indication — see Rationale.
2
Protect the airway during the seizure itself
Position to prevent aspiration, supplemental O₂. Most eclamptic seizures are self-limited — don't reach for a benzodiazepine reflexively if magnesium is already running and the seizure is resolving on its own.
3
Severe hypertension (SBP ≥160 or DBP ≥110) — treat as a separate, time-critical emergency
This is not the same problem magnesium is solving. Labetalol 20 mg IV bolus, then 40 mg at 10 min if inadequate, then 80 mg q10min (max total 220 mg). Alternative: hydralazine 5–10 mg IV, repeated until target reached. Goal is prompt, controlled reduction — not a specific target number achieved instantly, and not overcorrection.
4
Monitor for magnesium toxicity clinically, at the bedside
Check deep tendon reflexes, respiratory rate, and urine output at regular intervals — this is a clinical exam, not something to wait on a lab level for. Absent patellar reflex, respiratory rate <12/min, or urine output <25–30 mL/hr are hold/toxicity criteria.
5
Calcium gluconate immediately available as the magnesium antidote
10 mL of 10% calcium gluconate IV (1 g total) over 3 minutes if toxicity develops — have this drawn up and at the bedside for any patient on a magnesium infusion, not something to locate after signs appear.
6
Do not delay delivery to "complete" magnesium administration
Delivery is the only definitive treatment for preeclampsia/eclampsia. Continue the magnesium infusion through delivery — including cesarean, despite theoretical interactions with muscle relaxants used in general anesthesia — rather than pausing it to finish a course first.
7
Foley catheter, strict input/output
Hourly urine output monitoring is both a magnesium-toxicity safety check and a marker of renal perfusion/severity — place early rather than reactively.
8
Continue magnesium 12–24 hours postpartum
Most institutions continue for 24 hours after delivery or after the last seizure, though evidence suggests 12 hours performs comparably for seizure prevention, reflex depression, respiratory depression, and pulmonary edema — reasonable practice variation exists here.
Magnesium and antihypertensives are two different jobs

Magnesium sulfate does not reliably lower blood pressure and is not an antihypertensive agent. A patient can be appropriately loaded on magnesium and still be in a hypertensive emergency requiring a completely separate drug — treat both tracks in parallel, not sequentially.

02

Brief Review

Preeclampsia is new-onset hypertension after 20 weeks' gestation with proteinuria, or — in the absence of proteinuria — new-onset thrombocytopenia, renal insufficiency, impaired liver function with right-upper-quadrant/epigastric pain, pulmonary edema, new headache unresponsive to treatment, or visual disturbance. Severe features (SBP ≥160 or DBP ≥110 mmHg on two occasions ≥4 hours apart, thrombocytopenia <100,000/mm³, impaired LFTs, renal insufficiency, pulmonary edema, or new cerebral/visual symptoms) mark a meaningfully higher-risk category. Eclampsia is a new-onset generalized seizure in this setting not attributable to another cause — one of preeclampsia's most severe manifestations.

Magnesium sulfate is the first-line agent for both preventing and treating eclamptic seizures — a Cochrane review found it reduces eclampsia risk by roughly 92% compared to phenytoin (relative risk 0.08), with significantly reduced pneumonia, mechanical ventilation, and ICU admission compared to that alternative. ACOG restricts routine magnesium use to preeclampsia with severe features or eclampsia; use in milder disease remains a genuine area of practice variation, discussed further below.

Prevention & Set-Up (In Place of "Room Setup")

  • Magnesium sulfate is recommended for preeclampsia with severe features or eclampsia — for milder disease (gestational hypertension or preeclampsia without severe features), the decision is genuinely unsettled. An estimated 100–129 women need treatment to prevent one eclampsia case in this lower-risk group, and ACOG explicitly leaves the decision to physician/institution judgment and patient preference rather than mandating it.
  • Have calcium gluconate immediately at the bedside for any patient on a magnesium infusion — not something to send for once toxicity signs appear.
  • Antihypertensive threshold for treatment varies by society — ACOG initiates treatment at severe-range hypertension, while NICE, SOGC, ISSHP, and SOMANZ recommend treating milder hypertension, several targeting 135/85 mmHg. Know your institution's specific threshold rather than assuming a single universal number.
  • Don't withhold or interrupt magnesium for operative delivery — continuing through cesarean, including with general anesthesia, is the current recommendation despite interaction considerations with neuromuscular blocking agents.
  • Place a Foley early in any patient started on magnesium — hourly urine output is both a safety monitor and a severity marker.
  • Contraindications to magnesium: myasthenia gravis, hypocalcemia, moderate-to-severe renal failure, cardiac ischemia, heart block, myocarditis — know these before reflexively loading every hypertensive obstetric patient.

Rationale for Eclampsia/Preeclampsia Management

Why magnesium outperforms benzodiazepines and phenytoin for this specific indication: unlike phenytoin or diazepam, which act as general anticonvulsants via unrelated mechanisms, magnesium's benefit in eclampsia is thought to work through NMDA receptor blockade, calcium channel modulation, and — based on animal model data — protection of the blood-brain barrier against the neuroinflammation that lowers seizure threshold in preeclampsia specifically. This mechanistic specificity is likely why a Cochrane review found such a dramatic relative risk reduction (0.08) against phenytoin, alongside significantly less respiratory depression, pneumonia, and ICU admission — benzodiazepines and phenytoin work reasonably well for generic seizures but aren't targeting the actual pathophysiology at play here.

Why magnesium and antihypertensive therapy are treated as two separate problems, not one: magnesium's mechanism (NMDA/calcium channel modulation, primarily central) doesn't reliably or predictably lower systemic blood pressure — it's not pharmacologically built to be an antihypertensive, despite both being given in the same patient for the same underlying disease. Severe hypertension carries its own acute stroke/hemorrhage risk independent of seizure risk, which is why it gets a dedicated, parallel treatment track (labetalol or hydralazine) rather than being assumed to resolve once magnesium is running.

Why toxicity monitoring is clinical, not lab-based, in real time: serum magnesium levels take time to result, and the toxicity progression (areflexia at lower levels, respiratory paralysis at higher levels, cardiac arrest beyond that) is well-characterized enough that bedside deep tendon reflex, respiratory rate, and urine output checks catch impending toxicity faster and more practically than waiting on a lab draw. This is why the standard protocol is built around a physical exam checklist rather than serial magnesium levels as the primary safety mechanism.

Why calcium gluconate works as the specific antidote: magnesium's toxic effects are largely mediated through its action at the same neuromuscular junction and calcium-channel-related pathways that produce its therapeutic anticonvulsant effect — calcium directly competes with and antagonizes magnesium's physiologic action at these sites, which is why it reverses toxicity relatively quickly when given, rather than simply providing generic supportive correction.

Why delivery isn't delayed to "finish" a magnesium course: magnesium is neuroprotective and seizure-preventive, but it doesn't address the underlying placental/vascular pathophysiology driving preeclampsia — delivery is the only intervention that does. Treating magnesium administration as a prerequisite to complete before proceeding to delivery inverts the actual priority; the drug is meant to support the patient safely through the process of delivery, not to be finished before delivery can happen.

08

Workup & Disposition

Labs: CBC with platelets, LFTs, creatinine/renal function, uric acid, urine protein-to-creatinine ratio or 24-hour collection, coagulation panel if HELLP is a concern. Fetal monitoring (NST, growth assessment) as clinically indicated.

Neuroimaging for atypical presentation — focal deficits, failure to improve as expected, or diagnostic uncertainty about the seizure etiology.

Delivery planning: delivery is recommended at or beyond 34 0/7 weeks for preeclampsia with severe features, after maternal stabilization; delivery should not be delayed for steroid administration in the late preterm period given the acuity. Expectant management before 34 weeks is reserved for carefully selected candidates under close monitoring.

Postpartum continuation: continue magnesium 12–24 hours postpartum per institutional protocol; monitor for late-postpartum severe hypertension, which can present up to several days after delivery and warrants its own evaluation rather than being assumed resolved once delivery has occurred.

Disposition: ICU or high-acuity obstetric monitoring for any patient with eclampsia or severe-feature preeclampsia requiring magnesium and/or IV antihypertensive therapy; continued close monitoring through the postpartum window given ongoing seizure and hypertensive risk.
