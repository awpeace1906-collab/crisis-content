Emergency Management — The Escalation Ladder

Most laryngospasm resolves with the first two steps. Escalate quickly if it doesn't — hypoxia plus a paralytic is a dangerous combination if the airway still isn't secured.
1
Stop the stimulus, call for help
Remove the triggering stimulus if one is identifiable (suction catheter, surgical stimulation, extubation timing). Get another set of hands — this escalates fast if it doesn't break quickly.
2
Jaw thrust / Larson's maneuver + CPAP with 100% O₂
Firm jaw thrust at the "laryngospasm notch" — bilateral pressure behind the ramus of the mandible, anterior to the mastoid process. Simultaneously apply CPAP via tight-seal BVM with a PEEP valve. Avoid vigorous positive-pressure ventilation attempts — forceful bagging against a closed glottis risks gastric insufflation. Larson's maneuver has limited controlled evidence behind it, but it's low-risk and widely used as first response.
3
Clear the airway, deepen anesthesia
Suction the pharynx if blood or secretions are contributing. If not already breaking: propofol 0.5–0.8 mg/kg IV (subhypnotic doses as low as 0.25 mg/kg have been effective) — deepens anesthesia and relaxes upper airway tissue without the full apnea burden of a paralytic.
4
Desaturating or no response — succinylcholine
Low dose often sufficient to break the spasm: 0.1–0.2 mg/kg IV (full paralysis in 30–45 sec, breaks spasm in 45 sec–1 min). Severe/refractory: full dose 1–1.5 mg/kg IV and proceed to intubation. No IV access: succinylcholine 4 mg/kg IM (onset slower, roughly 3–4 minutes) or 2 mg/kg intralingual (IM into the tongue body — retains perfusion better than peripheral muscle during hypoxia, but carries a high rate of transient arrhythmia). Rocuronium 1.2 mg/kg IV is a reasonable alternative if succinylcholine is contraindicated.
5
Treat bradycardia if it develops
Airway manipulation and succinylcholine can both provoke bradycardia/arrest in a hypoxic patient, especially children. Correct hypoxia urgently and give atropine 0.02 mg/kg for symptomatic bradycardia.
6
Intubate if the spasm doesn't break
Forced orotracheal intubation, even through partially closed cords if necessary, rescues the airway — some vocal cord trauma is an acceptable trade for hypoxic arrest. Laryngospasm is often followed by a brief gasp; that moment can be your window to pass the tube.
7
Surgical airway — last resort
Cricothyrotomy/tracheostomy only if intubation also fails and oxygenation cannot otherwise be restored — same CICO logic as any other failed airway.
Not always obvious

Roughly 23% of laryngospasm cases don't present with the classic high-pitched crowing sound — some present only as increasing work of breathing, paradoxical chest movement, or desaturation without any audible warning. Suspect it whenever airway obstruction appears without an obvious supraglottic cause.

02

Brief Review

Laryngospasm is an exaggeration of the normal protective glottic-closure reflex — sustained closure of the true (and often false) vocal cords that partially or completely obstructs the airway. Sensory input runs through the internal branch of the superior laryngeal nerve; the motor response is mediated by the recurrent laryngeal nerve driving the intrinsic laryngeal muscles. It occurs most often during induction and emergence from general anesthesia, though it's also described during procedural sedation and, rarely, in the awake state.

It's a relatively common perioperative event, more frequent in children than adults — pediatric procedural sedation series report roughly 3.9 per 1,000 cases versus about 1.1 per 1,000 in adults, and the large majority of reported cases involve ketamine sedation specifically.

Risk Factors & Prevention (In Place of "Room Setup")

  • Patient factors: young age (children, especially infants), airway hypersensitivity, recent upper respiratory tract infection, passive or active smoke exposure, obesity with obstructive sleep apnea, GERD, structural airway anomaly.
  • Anesthetic/procedural factors: light plane of anesthesia during airway manipulation, blood/secretions/gastric contents irritating the cords, laryngoscopy or suction catheter stimulation, extubation during a light ("stage 2") plane of anesthesia — a well-recognized higher-risk moment.
  • Prevention strategies: IV propofol induction rather than inhalational induction in children reduces risk; IV lidocaine or magnesium sulfate given prophylactically has supporting evidence in at-risk cases; extubating either fully awake or fully deep (not in between) avoids the highest-risk light plane; suctioning and clearing the airway thoroughly before extubation reduces stimulus load.
  • Have the ladder ready before extubating a higher-risk patient: propofol drawn up, succinylcholine dose calculated and available, difficult airway equipment nearby — treat any extubation in a known at-risk patient the way you'd treat a planned difficult airway.

06

Rationale for Laryngospasm Management Steps

Why deepen anesthesia before reaching for a paralytic: laryngospasm is fundamentally a reflex problem — the cords are closing in response to a stimulus interpreted as threatening. Deepening anesthesia with propofol reduces the reflex arc's sensitivity directly, often resolving the spasm without the apnea and full recovery time a paralytic requires. It's the lower-risk intervention and is tried first whenever the patient isn't yet critically hypoxic.

Why low-dose succinylcholine is often enough: the goal isn't full surgical paralysis — it's relaxing the intrinsic laryngeal muscles enough to break the spasm. A fractional dose (0.1–0.2 mg/kg) frequently accomplishes this while preserving some respiratory drive and shortening total recovery time compared to a full RSI dose, which is why experienced anesthesia providers often reach for the low dose before escalating.

Why avoid vigorous bag-mask ventilation attempts: forcing positive pressure against a closed or partially closed glottis pushes air into the stomach instead of the lungs, distending it and increasing aspiration risk without improving oxygenation — CPAP (steady pressure, not forceful bagging) is the correct technique while waiting for pharmacologic treatment to work.

Why the tongue is used for IM injection when there's no IV: peripheral skeletal muscle perfusion drops significantly during hypoxia and shock, slowing drug uptake from a standard IM injection exactly when speed matters most. The tongue retains relatively better blood flow under those conditions, which is the physiologic basis for intralingual succinylcholine as a faster-acting alternative to a deltoid or thigh injection — at the cost of a higher reported arrhythmia rate.

Why Larson's maneuver persists in every algorithm despite weak evidence: the proposed mechanism is that firm, painful pressure at the laryngospasm notch triggers vocal cord relaxation via a pain-mediated autonomic reflex — plausible, widely taught, and low-risk to attempt, but it has never been validated in a controlled trial. It's kept in the sequence because it costs nothing to try while CPAP and repositioning are already underway, not because the evidence is strong.

Differential Diagnosis

MimicKey distinguishing features
Simple supraglottic obstruction (tongue falling back)Resolves promptly with jaw thrust and an oral/nasal airway alone — true laryngospasm does not resolve with positioning adjuncts.
BronchospasmExpiratory wheeze rather than inspiratory stridor; obstruction is lower-airway, not glottic; treated with bronchodilators rather than the laryngospasm ladder.
Foreign body / secretions occluding the airwaySuctioning or removal resolves it directly; laryngospasm can co-occur if the material itself is the triggering stimulus.
Anaphylaxis with airway involvementAccompanied by hypotension, urticaria, or other systemic features; treated with epinephrine, not the airway-deepening ladder.
Epiglottitis (if awake/pre-induction)Hours-to-days onset, drooling/dysphagia/distress triad — an entirely different, infectious process; do not examine the throat.
Vocal cord dysfunction / paradoxical vocal fold motionCan mimic laryngospasm in awake or lightly sedated patients; often has a psychogenic or irritant-triggered component and doesn't follow the anesthesia-induction/emergence timing pattern.
08

After the Spasm — Complications & Disposition

Watch for negative pressure pulmonary edema: forceful inspiratory effort against a closed glottis generates markedly negative intrathoracic pressure, which can pull fluid into the alveoli once the obstruction clears — this can present minutes to a couple of hours after the laryngospasm itself resolves, so don't consider the episode "closed" the moment oxygenation normalizes.

Reintubation/airway support: once broken, support with 100% O₂ and reassess. If the patient was intubated to break the spasm, continued intubation may be appropriate if airway soiling or pulmonary edema occurred, allowing suctioning and lung recruitment before extubation is reattempted.

Disposition: extended PACU or ICU-level observation after any laryngospasm requiring pharmacologic intervention, specifically to catch delayed negative pressure pulmonary edema and confirm full recovery from any paralytic given.

Documentation and future anesthetics: record the trigger, the intervention that worked, and the total drug doses given. Recurrent laryngospasm (especially post-tonsillectomy/adenoidectomy patients) may warrant a modified extubation plan or subhypnotic propofol prophylaxis on future anesthetics; genuinely recurrent cases occasionally prompt consideration of superior laryngeal nerve block as an extreme preventive measure.
