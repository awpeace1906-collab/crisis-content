Tension Pneumothorax

A clinical diagnosis, not a radiographic one — the site-selection debate between 2nd ICS midclavicular and 5th ICS midaxillary, why needle decompression alone is never the last step, and why nitrous oxide makes it worse.

01

Emergency Management & Immediate Actions

Decompress based on the clinical picture. Waiting for a chest X-ray to confirm what the exam already told you is how this becomes a fatal delay.
1
Recognize it clinically and act immediately
Hemodynamic instability plus unilateral absent/diminished breath sounds and clinical suspicion is enough — do not delay decompression to obtain a chest X-ray in an unstable patient.
2
Needle decompression — choose your site deliberately
2nd intercostal space, midclavicular line (2nd ICS-MCL): the traditional, most widely taught site. 5th intercostal space, anterior/midaxillary line (5th ICS-MAL): increasingly favored when time and anatomy allow — chest wall is measurably thinner here across multiple studies, translating to a lower reported failure rate (~16.7% vs. ~42.5% for 2nd ICS-MCL with a short needle in one cadaveric comparison). Left-sided tension: the 2nd ICS-MCL is the safer choice given proximity of the heart to the 5th ICS-MAL on that side.
3
Use an adequately long needle/catheter
A catheter ≥7–8 cm is recommended to reliably reach the pleural space, especially in patients with a thicker chest wall — shorter needles (5 cm) have documented high failure rates on CT-based chest wall thickness studies.
4
Consider finger (simple/open) thoracostomy instead, especially in trauma/peri-arrest
Several EMS and trauma bodies now favor finger thoracostomy over needle decompression given needle decompression's high real-world failure rate (catheter kinking, inadequate length, clotting). Performed at the 4th–5th ICS, anterior axillary line — the same site used for the definitive chest tube that follows. Incise, blunt-dissect with forceps down to pleura, and sweep a finger through to confirm entry and break up adhesions.
5
Discontinue nitrous oxide immediately if it's running
N₂O diffuses into the gas-filled pleural space faster than nitrogen leaves, actively enlarging the pneumothorax and worsening tension physiology.
6
Reassess immediately after decompression
Expect a rush of air, improving breath sounds, and hemodynamic improvement. If there's no improvement, reconsider the diagnosis, check catheter position/patency, or escalate to finger thoracostomy.
7
Always follow with definitive tube thoracostomy
Needle or finger decompression is a temporizing bridge, not a cure — the lung remains collapsed and the air leak persists until a formal chest tube is placed and connected to a drainage/seal system.
8
Cardiac arrest / peri-arrest with suspected tension
Bilateral finger thoracostomy is reasonable in traumatic arrest with suspected bilateral tension physiology or when the affected side is uncertain — don't let uncertainty about laterality delay decompression in extremis.
Positive-pressure ventilation can create or worsen this

A ventilated patient who suddenly becomes hard to bag, desaturates, and develops rising peak airway pressures should be assumed to have tension pneumothorax until proven otherwise — barotrauma from positive-pressure ventilation is a well-recognized cause, not just a complication of trauma.

02

Brief Review

Tension pneumothorax results from a one-way valve mechanism: air enters the pleural space but cannot exit, progressively raising intrapleural pressure. The expanding air collapses the ipsilateral lung, shifts the mediastinum away from the affected side, and — critically — kinks the vena cava and reduces venous return, producing obstructive shock that progresses to PEA cardiac arrest if not decompressed.

Causes include blunt or penetrating chest trauma, iatrogenic injury (central line placement, thoracentesis, mechanical ventilation with barotrauma in a patient with an undetected simple pneumothorax or bronchopleural fistula), and rarely spontaneous pneumothorax progressing to tension physiology. Pneumothorax of some form is present in up to 20% of major trauma victims found alive; missed or delayed tension pneumothorax specifically is estimated to account for a meaningful share (roughly 5%) of preventable deaths from serious thoracic injury.

The "classic" exam is often incomplete. In one retrospective EMS series, diminished/absent breath sounds and hypoxia were the dominant documented indications for decompression — not the full textbook constellation of tracheal deviation and JVD. Build clinical suspicion from whatever combination of findings is actually present, not from waiting for every textbook sign to appear.
04

Prevention & Set-Up (In Place of "Room Setup")

  • Post-procedure imaging after central line placement — a chest X-ray specifically to rule out an iatrogenic pneumothorax before it has a chance to progress to tension physiology.
  • Point-of-care ultrasound for rapid bedside diagnosis (absence of lung sliding) in any patient with sudden desaturation or hemodynamic instability where pneumothorax is plausible — faster than waiting for a portable film.
  • Avoid unnecessarily high peak airway pressures during mechanical ventilation, and maintain a high index of suspicion for barotrauma in any ventilated patient with known or suspected lung pathology (COPD, asthma, prior pneumothorax).
  • Have decompression equipment immediately accessible in any setting where the risk is elevated (trauma bay, thoracic surgery, central line procedures) — long (≥8 cm) angiocatheters specifically, given the documented failure rate of shorter needles.
  • Individualize site selection to the patient rather than defaulting reflexively to one taught site — chest wall thickness varies significantly by age, sex, BMI, and COPD status, all of which affect which site is actually reachable with standard equipment.

Rationale for Tension Pneumothorax Management

Why 5th ICS-MAL is gaining favor over the traditional 2nd ICS-MCL site: CT-based chest wall thickness measurements consistently show the 5th ICS-MAL is thinner across most body habitus categories, which is why real-world failure rates run lower there — a needle simply has a better chance of actually reaching the pleural space. The tradeoff is proximity to the heart on the left side, which is why left-sided tension pneumothorax still favors the 2nd ICS-MCL specifically, even though it has a documented higher overall failure rate.

Why needle decompression is inherently a temporary fix, not a treatment: the catheter is thin, can kink, clot, or become dislodged, and does nothing to address the underlying air leak causing the tension in the first place. Every successful needle decompression still needs a formal chest tube to follow — treating the needle as "done" rather than as a bridge is a recognized failure mode.

Why finger thoracostomy is increasingly preferred in trauma/peri-arrest settings specifically: it creates a large, direct opening into the pleural space that a provider can physically confirm with a finger sweep — bypassing the failure modes (wrong depth, kinked catheter, inadequate length) that plague needle decompression. It also happens to be performed at the exact site the definitive chest tube will go, so it doubles as the first step of definitive management rather than a separate procedure.

Why clinical diagnosis takes priority over imaging: the physiology driving deterioration — progressive vena caval compression reducing venous return — worsens continuously and can progress from hemodynamic compromise to full arrest within minutes. Waiting for a portable chest X-ray in a hemodynamically unstable patient converts a survivable, rapidly treatable condition into a resuscitation.

Why nitrous oxide is a specific, avoidable aggravating factor: N₂O's high blood:gas solubility means it diffuses into any closed gas space (a pneumothorax, an air embolus, a bowel loop) faster than the nitrogen already there can diffuse out — the same principle that makes it dangerous in venous air embolism. In a patient with any suspected or confirmed pneumothorax, this converts a background anesthetic gas into an active contributor to the emergency.

Differential Diagnosis

MimicKey distinguishing features
Cardiac tamponadeBreath sounds typically symmetric bilaterally (unlike the unilateral finding in tension pneumothorax); JVD present in both, but percussion/auscultation of the chest is the key differentiator — echo confirms.
Massive pulmonary embolismNo unilateral breath sound change; look for a clear PE risk factor profile and, where available, echo findings of acute right heart strain rather than a mediastinal shift.
Myocardial infarction / cardiogenic shockECG ischemic changes, troponin elevation; breath sounds equal bilaterally, no hyperresonance.
Simple (non-tension) pneumothoraxHemodynamically stable — the defining distinction. Doesn't require emergency decompression; managed with observation or elective chest tube depending on size and symptoms.
HemothoraxDull, not hyperresonant, to percussion on the affected side — blood rather than air filling the pleural space; may coexist with pneumothorax as a hemopneumothorax.
Esophageal ruptureCan present with chest pain, subcutaneous emphysema, and hemodynamic instability; history (recent instrumentation, forceful vomiting) and mediastinal air on imaging point toward this rather than a primary pleural process.
08

Workup & Disposition

Confirm and formalize: chest X-ray or point-of-care ultrasound after decompression to confirm re-expansion and correct chest tube position; watch specifically for reexpansion pulmonary edema after rapid decompression of a significantly collapsed lung.

Persistent air leak: if the chest tube continues bubbling well beyond the expected window, evaluate for bronchopleural fistula or ongoing parenchymal injury — this may need surgical or interventional pulmonology involvement rather than continued observation alone.

Disposition: ICU or trauma monitoring for any patient who required emergency decompression, given both the underlying injury severity and the need to confirm re-expansion and rule out recurrence.

Documentation: record which site was used, needle/catheter length, and the clinical response — useful for the immediate chest tube placement that follows and for any subsequent procedures in the same patient.
