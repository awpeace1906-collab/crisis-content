01

Emergency Management & Immediate Actions

Start the ACLS backbone immediately, but pivot fast to the OR-specific cause — you likely already know, or can quickly find, why this patient just arrested.
1
Declare the arrest, call for help, start CPR
High-quality chest compressions immediately. Call for additional anesthesia/surgical/nursing help and the code cart. Note the exact time — OR arrests are almost always witnessed, which is an advantage over community arrest that should translate to faster intervention.
2
Shockable rhythm — defibrillate immediately
VF or pulseless VT: immediate defibrillation, no delay for further workup first. This is a strong recommendation independent of suspected cause.
3
Asystole with P-waves — consider emergency pacing
A distinct scenario from flatline asystole; temporary pacing is specifically indicated here rather than proceeding straight through the standard non-shockable pathway.
4
Immediately consider what was happening in the room right before the arrest
Recent drug given (NMBA, antibiotic, LA)? Blood product running? Position change or insufflation just started? Central line just placed? Succinylcholine given to a patient with occult neuromuscular disease? This context, unavailable in a community arrest, is the single biggest advantage the perioperative setting has — use it.
5
Empirically treat for hyperkalemia early if the cause is unclear
Calcium chloride/gluconate, insulin/dextrose, hyperventilation, bicarbonate — hyperkalemia is common enough and rapidly treatable enough in the OR (transfusion, succinylcholine, renal failure, crush injury) that empiric treatment while the workup continues is reasonable rather than waiting for a potassium level.
6
Use echocardiography (TTE/TEE) to work up PEA
Especially valuable for pulseless electrical activity — directly visualizing a distended right ventricle (PE, tension physiology), tamponade, hypovolemia, or a hypocontractile heart focuses resuscitation on the actual mechanism rather than working through a generic differential blindly.
7
Match the antidote to the trigger — don't default to standard ACLS dosing
LAST-triggered arrest: reduced epinephrine (≤1 mcg/kg, not standard 1 mg boluses) plus lipid emulsion — repeated full-dose epinephrine measurably worsens lipid emulsion's effectiveness. MH-triggered arrest: dantrolene is the priority alongside CPR. Anaphylaxis: epinephrine at anaphylaxis dosing. See each respective crisis file for full detail — the point here is recognizing that standard ACLS pharmacology can be actively wrong for several OR-specific triggers.
8
Refractory arrest — escalate to ECMO/CPB early, not late
If a reversible cause is identified or suspected and standard measures aren't working within several minutes, involve a cardiac surgery/ECMO team early. Perioperative ECPR series report survival to discharge around 57.6% — substantially better than traditional CPR outcomes — which supports earlier rather than later escalation when support is available. Intraoperative thrombolysis is a reasonable consideration specifically for suspected massive PE, weighed against surgical bleeding risk.
The reversible-causes list here isn't the standard H's and T's

Community/ward arrests draw on a generic differential. The OR has its own dominant causes — hemorrhage, gas embolism, hyperkalemia, LAST, MH, anaphylaxis, high spinal, vagal reflex from surgical stimulation — and the PeRLS 2025 guideline explicitly frames this as reason to move away from a one-size-fits-all ACLS protocol toward diagnosis-driven resuscitation.

02

Brief Review

Perioperative cardiac arrest has an incidence of roughly 2–13 per 10,000 anesthetics, with mortality before hospital discharge ranging from 32–75% depending on the population and cause. It differs fundamentally from out-of-hospital or ward cardiac arrest: OR arrests are almost always witnessed, occur under continuous invasive monitoring, involve trained anesthesia/surgical staff already at the bedside, and frequently have an identifiable, immediately treatable cause — because the team usually knows exactly what was happening in the room in the moments before the arrest.

The 2025 Perioperative Resuscitation and Life Support (PeRLS) guideline — the third iteration of this evolving guidance, published in Anesthesiology — explicitly reframes perioperative arrest as a diagnosis-driven discipline rather than a straightforward extension of community ACLS. Standard ACLS was developed primarily for out-of-hospital and general in-hospital arrest, and several of its default assumptions (standard epinephrine dosing, generic reversible-causes search) can be actively counterproductive for OR-specific triggers like LAST or MH.

04

Readiness & Set-Up (In Place of "Room Setup")

  • Continuous invasive monitoring is already the OR's built-in advantage — leverage it. A sudden EtCO₂ drop, arterial line waveform change, or arrhythmia on the monitor often gives earlier warning than a community arrest ever could.
  • Have a cognitive aid immediately accessible (e.g., Stanford Emergency Manual / Cognitive Aids for Perioperative Crisis) — under the stress of a real arrest, a structured checklist measurably improves adherence to the right sequence of steps.
  • Know your institution's ECMO/CPB activation pathway before you need it — who to call, how fast a team can mobilize, and what criteria trigger the call. This is not something to figure out mid-arrest.
  • Stock and know the location of trigger-specific rescue kits — MH cart with dantrolene, LAST lipid emulsion kit — matched to the case-specific risk profile before a high-risk case even starts.
  • For known higher-risk patients (severe cardiomyopathy, planned high-hemorrhage-risk surgery), pre-arrange blood availability, consider standby perfusion/ECMO readiness, and involve cardiac anesthesia/ICU early rather than reactively.
  • Debrief structurally after every event — immediate or delayed, either is better than none, and structured post-event reflection has been shown to improve team performance on the next event.

06

Rationale for Perioperative-Specific Management

Why standard ACLS isn't simply "good enough" here: ACLS was built primarily around common cardiac causes of arrhythmic arrest in the community and general hospital ward, with pharmacology (especially epinephrine dosing) optimized for that context. Several dominant OR-specific causes — LAST, MH, anaphylaxis, gas embolism, vagal reflex — either need different drugs entirely or need the same drugs at meaningfully different doses. Applying unmodified ACLS to a LAST-triggered arrest, for instance, can actively worsen outcomes through repeated full-dose epinephrine undermining lipid emulsion's effectiveness — which is exactly the gap PeRLS 2025 was written to close.

Why the team's situational knowledge matters as much as any algorithm: in a witnessed OR arrest, the team usually knows the drugs given, the procedure step underway, and the patient's baseline physiology in real time — information a code team responding to an unwitnessed ward or community arrest simply doesn't have. Building the resuscitation around "what was just happening" rather than working mechanically through a generic differential is what "diagnosis-driven" resuscitation actually means in practice.

Why echocardiography is weighted so heavily for PEA specifically: PEA is a description of the rhythm, not a diagnosis — it can result from tamponade, massive PE, severe hypovolemia, tension pneumothorax, or profound myocardial dysfunction, each needing a completely different immediate intervention. Bedside echo turns "PEA, differential unclear" into a specific, actionable finding (a collapsed, empty left ventricle vs. a distended, hypocontractile right ventricle vs. a pericardial effusion with tamponade physiology) far faster than working through the possibilities empirically.

Why earlier ECMO/CPB escalation is increasingly favored over waiting: traditional CPR outcome data for prolonged in-hospital arrest is poor — one large multicenter dataset found under 2.5% favorable outcomes after more than 40 minutes of CPR even in shockable-rhythm scenarios. Perioperative ECPR series showing survival to discharge above 50%, often with good neurologic outcomes, reflect a population where the underlying cause is frequently reversible and mechanical support can bridge the patient through it — the earlier that bridge starts relative to arrest duration, the better the odds, which is the basis for escalating early rather than only as a last resort.

08

Post-ROSC Care & Disposition

Immediate post-ROSC targets: MAP ≥65 mmHg (crystalloid boluses first, then vasopressor infusion if needed), PaCO₂ 35–40 mmHg avoiding both hyper- and hypoventilation, SpO₂ 90–98% using the lowest FiO₂ that achieves it, continuous cardiac monitoring with a 12-lead ECG to assess for STEMI.

Identify and treat reversible causes in the post-ROSC window too — hypovolemia, hypoxia, acidosis, electrolyte derangement, hypothermia, tension pneumothorax, tamponade, toxins, and pulmonary/coronary thrombosis all remain relevant after return of circulation, not just during the arrest itself.

Coronary evaluation: urgent coronary angiography with transfer to a cath-capable facility if STEMI or a strong suspicion of acute coronary occlusion is present.

Disposition: ICU-level care is standard for any perioperative arrest, with close coordination between the anesthesia/surgical team and ICU on medication adjustments, device optimization, and relevant subspecialty consultation.

Debrief: structured post-event debriefing — immediate or delayed — improves subsequent team performance and is worth doing after every event, not just the ones that go poorly.
