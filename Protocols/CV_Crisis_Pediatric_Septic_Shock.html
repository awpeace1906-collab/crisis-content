01

Emergency Management & Immediate Actions

Fluid dosing depends heavily on whether ICU-level support is actually available — reassess after every bolus rather than pushing volume reflexively.
1
Recognize using the Phoenix Sepsis Score
A score ≥2 in a child with suspected infection defines sepsis; septic shock requires that plus cardiovascular dysfunction (cardiovascular sub-score ≥1). This 2024 SCCM-developed criterion replaced older, less data-driven definitions.
2
Empiric antimicrobial therapy — within 1 hour for septic shock
A strong recommendation despite very-low-certainty evidence, reflecting the severity of delay in this population. For probable sepsis without shock: a time-limited period of rapid evaluation is reasonable, with antimicrobials ideally within 3 hours once concern is substantiated — balancing urgency against the real risk of unnecessary antibiotics in a population with substantial symptom overlap with other conditions.
3
Fluid resuscitation — ICU-available setting
Up to 40–60 mL/kg in bolus fluid (10–20 mL/kg per bolus) over the first hour, titrated to clinical markers of cardiac output, and discontinued if signs of fluid overload develop. Reassess hemodynamic status after every single bolus rather than running a fixed total volume on autopilot.
4
Fluid resuscitation — no ICU availability, no hypotension
A strong recommendation against fluid bolus therapy (high certainty) — start maintenance fluids instead. This is a direct consequence of the FEAST trial (see Rationale) and represents a genuine, evidence-driven divergence from high-resource practice, not an oversight.
5
Fluid resuscitation — no ICU availability, with hypotension
Up to 40 mL/kg in bolus fluid (10–20 mL/kg per bolus) over the first hour is suggested over no bolus therapy — a more permissive threshold than the "no hypotension" scenario above, but still more conservative than the ICU-available pathway.
6
Fluid choice
Balanced/buffered crystalloids (lactated Ringer's, Hartmann's) preferred over 0.9% saline, though saline is a suitable alternative if balanced solutions aren't available. Prefer saline specifically for hyponatremia or concern for elevated intracranial pressure (e.g., CNS infection). Crystalloid preferred over albumin for initial resuscitation.
7
Vasoactive support if shock persists despite fluids
Epinephrine or norepinephrine are both reasonable first-line choices — current evidence doesn't clearly favor one over the other, and this remains an active area of ongoing trial investigation. Titrate to clinical perfusion endpoints and blood pressure targets rather than a fixed dose.
8
Hydrocortisone — situational, not routine
Suggested against if fluid resuscitation and vasopressor therapy have already restored hemodynamic stability. Either hydrocortisone or no hydrocortisone is reasonable specifically when fluids and vasopressors have not restored stability — both options carry low-quality evidence, reflecting genuine clinical equipoise rather than a clear answer.
Glucose control: avoid tight targets

A moderate-certainty recommendation exists against targeting blood glucose ≤140 mg/dL in pediatric septic shock — the guideline panel was unable to specify an alternative target range given insufficient evidence, but is clear that tight glycemic control specifically is not the answer here.

02

Brief Review

Pediatric sepsis and septic shock account for an estimated 3 million+ child deaths annually worldwide, with the highest burden falling on children under 5 and disproportionately in low-resource settings. The 2026 Surviving Sepsis Campaign pediatric guidelines — 61 recommendation statements, 20 new and 13 updated from the 2020 edition — adopt the Phoenix Sepsis Score as the current data-driven definition, replacing prior consensus-based criteria, and explicitly acknowledge that most aspects of pediatric sepsis care still rest on relatively low-quality evidence despite meaningful expansion of the evidence base since 2020.

A defining feature of this guideline cycle is that fluid resuscitation recommendations diverge by healthcare system resource level — a distinction rarely made this explicitly in other resuscitation guidelines in this series. This isn't hedging; it reflects a genuine, trial-demonstrated difference in the risk-benefit calculus between settings with and without intensive care and mechanical ventilation capability.

Signs — Warm vs. Cold Shock

PhenotypeFindings
Cold shockDelayed capillary refill (>3 sec), weak/thready pulses, cool mottled extremities, narrow pulse pressure — reflects predominant vasoconstriction with reduced cardiac output. More common in younger children.
Warm shockFlash capillary refill, bounding pulses, warm extremities, wide pulse pressure — reflects predominant vasodilation, more closely resembling the classic adult septic shock picture.
HypotensionA late finding in pediatric shock — children compensate remarkably well via tachycardia and vasoconstriction, meaning normal blood pressure does not rule out significant, ongoing shock. "Compensated shock" (severely impaired perfusion without hypotension) is the more clinically relevant early category.
Altered mental statusLethargy, irritability, poor interaction — an important and sometimes earlier clue than hemodynamic numbers alone.
Age-adjusted shock index (HR/SBP)A 0-hour score performed better than conventional vital signs at predicting need for vasoactive support/mechanical ventilation and early mortality in one observational study — though evidence certainty was judged too low to formally recommend for routine monitoring.
04

Set-Up & Readiness (In Place of "Room Setup")

  • Have a clear institutional threshold for ICU/mechanical ventilation availability — the entire fluid strategy above depends on knowing which resource category your setting falls into, not defaulting to the higher-resource protocol regardless of actual capability.
  • Empiric antibiotic protocols pre-identified by likely source — given the 1-hour target for septic shock, having a standing broad-spectrum regimen ready to start immediately (rather than deliberating in the moment) directly supports meeting that timeline.
  • POCUS availability — a new conditional 2026 recommendation supports cardiac and lung point-of-care ultrasound to guide resuscitation where resources allow, though its clinical-outcome impact is still being evaluated.
  • Blood culture collection technique matters — attention to proper collection limits contamination and unnecessary downstream diagnostic/therapeutic cascades; in low-resource settings, a single culture set has shown similar yield to multiple sets per recent evidence.
  • Plan for post-sepsis follow-up before discharge — 30–40% of children surviving PICU-level sepsis treatment may face lasting health issues, a newly emphasized focus area in the current guideline cycle.

Rationale for Pediatric Septic Shock Management

Why fluid strategy genuinely diverges by resource setting rather than being a single universal recommendation: the FEAST trial, conducted specifically in resource-poor African hospitals without routine access to mechanical ventilation or inotropic support, was stopped early after finding significantly excess mortality in children randomized to a fluid bolus compared to controls (relative risk 1.45) — a result consistent across sepsis, malaria, coma, acidosis, and severe anemia subgroups. This is a genuinely different finding from the higher-resource-setting trials (Santhanam, Sankar, Inwald) that found no mortality difference between restrictive and liberal fluid strategies when ICU-level rescue was available. The guideline's resource-stratified recommendations reflect this real trial evidence, not institutional hedging — a child who develops fluid-overload-related respiratory failure from aggressive bolusing has a fundamentally different prognosis in a setting with mechanical ventilation available versus one without it.

Why the FEAST result still generates methodological debate: some critics have argued incorrect statistical methods were used in the original analysis, and the study's applicability outside its specific low-resource context has been questioned. This tension is worth holding honestly — the trial's core message (fluid boluses aren't automatically safe, especially without rescue capability) has been broadly influential, even as some of its specific statistical conclusions remain debated in the literature.

Why "discontinue if signs of fluid overload develop" is written directly into the ICU-available recommendation: even in a resource-rich setting where FEAST's harm signal doesn't directly apply, fluid boluses aren't free of downstream risk — pulmonary edema, worsening oxygenation, and tissue edema are recognized complications of aggressive volume therapy. This is why reassessment after every bolus, rather than a fixed total volume delivered regardless of response, is built into current guidance — the goal is titrated, monitored resuscitation, not a protocol-driven volume target pursued mechanically.

Why hypotension is treated as a late, unreliable early warning sign: children have robust compensatory mechanisms (tachycardia, peripheral vasoconstriction) that maintain blood pressure well into a state of significantly impaired perfusion — this is exactly why "compensated shock" is the more clinically emphasized pediatric category, and why relying on blood pressure alone as a trigger for escalation systematically identifies sepsis later than perfusion-based assessment would.

Why the antibiotic timing recommendation explicitly names the tension with overuse: the guideline is unusually direct that the urgency to treat septic shock quickly has to be balanced against the real risk of unnecessary antimicrobial therapy in children whose presentation overlaps substantially with numerous non-infectious or less severe conditions. Naming this tension explicitly, rather than simply stating "treat fast," reflects an honest acknowledgment that pediatric sepsis recognition is genuinely harder than the urgency of the 1-hour target might suggest in isolation.

Differential Diagnosis

MimicKey distinguishing features
Cardiogenic shock (congenital heart disease, myocarditis)Look for a cardiac history, murmur, or hepatomegaly; POCUS can help distinguish poor cardiac contractility from a primarily vasodilated/septic picture.
Hypovolemic shock (dehydration, hemorrhage)Clear volume-loss history (vomiting/diarrhea, trauma, GI bleed) without the same degree of systemic inflammatory response; still responds to fluid resuscitation, but without the parallel antimicrobial urgency.
AnaphylaxisClear exposure history, skin findings, rapid onset — treated with epinephrine per the dedicated Anaphylaxis crisis file rather than the sepsis pathway.
Diabetic ketoacidosisCan present with tachycardia, altered mental status, and dehydration; point-of-care glucose and ketones distinguish quickly.
Adrenal crisisKnown adrenal insufficiency or recent steroid withdrawal; profound hypotension refractory to fluids and vasopressors should raise this consideration alongside the situational hydrocortisone guidance above.
Toxin/medication ingestionSee the relevant Toxicologic crisis files (BB/CCB overdose, TCA overdose) — can mimic shock physiology without an infectious source; history and specific toxidrome features distinguish.
08

Workup & Disposition

Labs and cultures: blood cultures (aerobic bottles alone are generally sufficient given similar yield to combined aerobic/anaerobic sets in most cases, unless anaerobic infection is specifically suspected), CBC, lactate, coagulation panel, renal/hepatic function, glucose. Don't let culture collection delay antimicrobial administration in septic shock.

Reassessment cadence: hemodynamic status after every fluid bolus, ongoing perfusion assessment (capillary refill, mental status, urine output) throughout resuscitation rather than a single initial assessment driving the whole course.

POCUS where available: cardiac and lung ultrasound can help distinguish cardiogenic from distributive shock and guide ongoing fluid/vasopressor decisions, per the new conditional 2026 recommendation.

Disposition: PICU admission is standard for confirmed septic shock, given the need for continuous monitoring, potential vasopressor titration, and reassessment cadence described above.

Follow-up planning: given that 30–40% of PICU sepsis survivors face lasting health issues, arrange structured post-discharge follow-up rather than treating survival to discharge as the endpoint of care.
