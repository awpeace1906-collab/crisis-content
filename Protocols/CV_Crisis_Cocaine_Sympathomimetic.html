Cocaine & Sympathomimetic Toxicity

Benzodiazepines first, beta-blockers avoided — but the "unopposed alpha" dogma behind that avoidance is more contested than most training implies. Cocaine's sodium-channel blockade means it plays by LAST's rules too, not just an adrenergic surge's.

01

Emergency Management & Immediate Actions

Benzodiazepines address the CNS sympathetic outflow driving nearly everything else — start there before reaching for a cardiovascular-specific drug.
1
Benzodiazepines first-line for agitation, tachycardia, and hypertension
Lorazepam 2–3 mg IV or diazepam 5–10 mg IV, titrated every 5 minutes to effect — small, incremental doses that reduce central sympathetic outflow, treating the CNS driver rather than just the downstream vitals. High cumulative doses are often needed; don't hesitate to escalate.
2
Add an antipsychotic for severe agitation refractory to benzodiazepines
Haloperidol or olanzapine; combination benzodiazepine-plus-antipsychotic therapy has shown better efficacy than either alone in this population. Avoid phenothiazines specifically — they lower seizure threshold and their anticholinergic effect impairs heat dissipation, working against you in a hyperthermic patient.
3
Persistent hypertension despite adequate benzodiazepine dosing
Nitroglycerin or nitroprusside (both also help reverse cocaine-induced coronary vasoconstriction), or phentolamine 1–5 mg IV slowly. Non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers (diltiazem, verapamil) reliably lower BP but don't reliably help the tachycardia; avoid dihydropyridines (nifedipine) — reflex tachycardia can worsen the picture.
4
Avoid beta-blockers during active intoxication
Current guidance still recommends avoiding all beta-blockers (including labetalol) while a patient shows active signs of intoxication (euphoria, tachycardia, hypertension) — see Rationale below for the real nuance behind this rule and why it's more contested than it's often taught.
5
Aggressive, immediate cooling for hyperthermia
Evaporative cooling and ice packs — hyperthermia is a leading driver of mortality and must be treated emergently. Antipyretics don't work here (this isn't a hypothalamic-set-point fever) and shouldn't be relied on.
6
Widened QRS / ventricular dysrhythmia — treat as sodium-channel blockade
Sodium bicarbonate is first-line for QRS widening, the same logic used for TCA overdose (see that crisis file) — cocaine has genuine local-anesthetic, sodium-channel-blocking properties. Lidocaine is a reasonable option if bicarbonate fails, despite lidocaine itself being another sodium-channel blocker — used cautiously and specifically for refractory ventricular dysrhythmia.
7
IV volume resuscitation and rhabdomyolysis prevention
20–30 mL/kg isotonic fluid bolus for dehydration and to reduce rhabdomyolysis risk — agitation, hyperthermia, and seizures all predispose to significant muscle breakdown.
8
Chest pain — evaluate as any potential ACS, avoid beta-blockers, give aspirin and nitroglycerin
Cocaine-associated MI risk is markedly elevated in the first hour after use (up to 24-fold) but can also present with delayed coronary vasoconstriction from active metabolites persisting up to 24 hours — a normal-appearing early presentation doesn't rule this out. Thrombolytics are contraindicated with severe hypertension, seizures, intracranial hemorrhage, or aortic dissection.
Naloxone can unmask sympathomimetic toxicity

If a mixed opioid-stimulant ingestion is reversed with naloxone, the opioid's sedating effect disappears and the underlying sympathomimetic toxicity can become suddenly apparent — don't be caught off guard by a patient who "wakes up agitated" after reversal.

02

Brief Review

Cocaine blocks presynaptic reuptake of norepinephrine, dopamine, and serotonin, producing prolonged, amplified postsynaptic stimulation across alpha, beta, and central adrenergic receptors — the sympathomimetic toxidrome shared with amphetamines, phencyclidine, synthetic cannabinoids, and ketamine. Cocaine has a second, distinct mechanism: at higher concentrations, it blocks voltage-gated sodium channels directly, the same property that makes it useful as a topical local anesthetic and that produces QRS widening and malignant ventricular arrhythmia in overdose.

Life-threatening complications span hyperthermia, hypertension, tachydysrhythmias, myocardial ischemia/infarction, seizures, intracranial hemorrhage, and aortic dissection. A dose above roughly 1 gram is potentially lethal, though individual tolerance varies enormously with chronic use — a dose non-toxic to a novice user can be fatal to a tolerant one due to sensitization, not tolerance in the usual protective direction.

Prevention & Set-Up (In Place of "Room Setup")

  • Consider cocaine use in any young-to-middle-aged patient with atypical chest pain — many patients won't volunteer recent use, making a low threshold for toxicology screening reasonable in this presentation.
  • Know that a negative screen doesn't exclude sympathomimetic exposure — numerous "designer" sympathomimetics aren't detected by standard immunoassay panels; treat the clinical toxidrome, not just the lab result.
  • If RSI is needed, use a non-depolarizing agent (e.g., rocuronium) rather than succinylcholine — hyperkalemia risk from hyperthermia and rhabdomyolysis makes depolarizing blockade a real concern in this population.
  • Avoid physical restraints when possible — struggling against restraint worsens hyperthermia, acidosis, and rhabdomyolysis; adequate chemical sedation is both more humane and physiologically safer.
  • Perform pregnancy testing routinely in reproductive-age patients — cocaine can precipitate miscarriage, premature labor, or placental abruption, and unrecognized pregnancy prevalence in this ED population runs up to 6%.
  • Have cooling equipment (ice packs, evaporative cooling setup) staged and ready rather than assembled reactively once hyperthermia is already severe.

Rationale for Sympathomimetic Toxicity Management

Why benzodiazepines are first-line rather than a cardiovascular-specific drug: the tachycardia, hypertension, hyperthermia, and agitation of cocaine toxicity all trace back to excessive central sympathetic outflow. Benzodiazepines reduce that outflow at the source rather than blocking one downstream receptor at a time — which is why a systematic review found benzodiazepine-first management effectively treats both the cardiovascular and CNS effects together, rather than requiring separate drugs for each symptom.

Why "unopposed alpha stimulation" is real physiology but has become an overapplied dogma: the classical concern is that beta-blockade removes beta-2-mediated vasodilation while leaving alpha-1-mediated vasoconstriction unopposed, worsening coronary vasospasm and hypertension. This is a legitimate concern specific to active, ongoing sympathomimetic intoxication. But the rule has been applied far more broadly than the evidence supports — it does not mean beta-blockers are dangerous for all stimulant-related tachycardia in all contexts (e.g., mild caffeine-driven tachycardia in a hemodynamically stable patient), and some recent data on mixed alpha/beta agents (labetalol, carvedilol) suggests the absolute contraindication may not hold as strongly as historically taught. Current guidelines still recommend avoiding beta-blockers during active cocaine intoxication specifically — the point is understanding *why* (a real but narrower physiologic concern), not just following the rule reflexively in every stimulant-adjacent scenario.

Why cocaine gets treated like a sodium-channel blocker overdose when the QRS widens: cocaine's local-anesthetic mechanism — blocking voltage-gated sodium channels — is pharmacologically the same class of problem as TCA overdose, even though cocaine is conceptually filed under "stimulant." Sodium bicarbonate raises extracellular sodium concentration and alkalinizes serum, both of which help overcome sodium-channel blockade — the same physiologic logic applies regardless of which drug produced the blockade.

Why hyperthermia gets treated as an independent emergency, not just a vital sign to note: elevated core temperature in this setting reflects a hypermetabolic state driven by excess catecholamines and muscular hyperactivity — it is not a hypothalamic set-point fever, which is exactly why antipyretics (which work by resetting the hypothalamic set point) do nothing here. Only active, external cooling addresses the actual mechanism, and prompt correction matters because mortality rises sharply with degree and duration of hyperthermia.

Why dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers are specifically avoided despite CCBs generally being reasonable: nifedipine and similar dihydropyridines cause peripheral vasodilation that can trigger reflex tachycardia — adding fuel to a fire in a patient whose primary problem may already include dangerous tachycardia. Non-dihydropyridines (diltiazem, verapamil) don't share this reflex mechanism to the same degree, which is why they're the reasonable CCB choice here while dihydropyridines aren't.

Thyroid stormNo temporal link to drug use; look for known thyroid disease and characteristic labs (suppressed TSH, elevated free T4).

Anticholinergic toxicityDry, flushed skin and absent bowel sounds distinguish this from the diaphoretic, hyperactive-bowel-sounds picture more typical of sympathomimetic toxicity ("hot as a hare, dry as a bone" vs. a wet, agitated stimulant picture).
08

Workup & Disposition

Labs and imaging: ECG (QRS width, ischemic changes), troponin and serial ECGs for chest pain, CK for rhabdomyolysis, CBC/BMP, CXR if fever or hypoxemia raises concern for aspiration or "crack lung." CT head ± CT angiography for altered mental status, focal neurologic findings, or head trauma.

Monitor until clinically resolved: continue observation until the patient is no longer tachycardic or hypertensive and is calm and cooperative — cocaine's effects are generally short-lived, so mild intoxication often needs no specific treatment beyond monitoring.

Watch for medication interactions worsening the picture: some agents given for other indications can compound cocaine's adverse effects — review the full medication list rather than treating each new symptom with an additional drug in isolation.

Disposition: ICU-level care for hemodynamic instability, refractory hyperthermia, significant rhabdomyolysis, or any evidence of ACS, ICH, or aortic dissection. Stable patients who normalize with supportive care can often be managed on a general floor or observation unit and connected to substance use resources before discharge.
