Massive Transfusion Protocol

Ratio-based resuscitation built to mimic whole blood, the lethal triad it's designed to break, and why TEG/ROTEM complements the protocol instead of replacing it.

01

Emergency Management & Immediate Actions

Transfusion buys time — it doesn't stop the bleeding. Activate the protocol and pursue source control in parallel, not sequentially.
1
Activate the MTP, call for help
Notify the blood bank immediately once criteria are met (see Activation Criteria below) — don't wait for confirmatory labs. Activation itself triggers pre-packaged, ratio-matched coolers to start moving.
2
Start with universal products, transition to type-specific
O-negative PRBCs with AB plasma (or low-titer group A plasma if AB is unavailable) until type-specific or crossmatched products are ready.
3
Give products in a balanced ratio
Empiric 1:1:1 (PRBC : FFP : platelets), extending to 1:1:1:1 with cryoprecipitate for prolonged/repeated rounds. Platelets ideally run through a separate IV, not through a rapid infuser — infuser shear can damage platelet function. RBCs and plasma can run simultaneously through the same line.
4
Tranexamic acid — early, or not at all
1 g IV bolus over 10 minutes, then 1 g over 8 hours. Give within 3 hours of injury — CRASH-2 data shows benefit specifically in this window; later administration hasn't shown the same mortality benefit and isn't the default move.
5
Replace calcium proactively
Citrate in stored blood products chelates ionized calcium — check it and replace early and often rather than waiting for it to become critically low; hypocalcemia independently worsens coagulopathy on top of dilution.
6
Actively prevent hypothermia and acidosis
Warm fluids/blood, use active external warming, minimize exposure. Avoid excess crystalloid — it dilutes clotting factors and doesn't carry oxygen. Permissive hypotension (accepting a lower-than-normal blood pressure target) is appropriate in the actively hemorrhaging trauma patient before definitive hemostasis, to avoid disrupting early clot formation.
7
Reassess with labs and, where available, viscoelastic testing
Check CBC, PT/PTT, fibrinogen after every ~5 units of PRBCs. TEG/ROTEM, when available, helps target specific deficiencies — hyperfibrinolysis needing more TXA, or a low fibrinogen needing cryoprecipitate — rather than continuing to transfuse blindly on ratio alone.
8
Pursue source control in parallel, not after
Surgery, interventional radiology, or endoscopic hemostasis — whatever stops the bleeding definitively. REBOA or resuscitative thoracotomy are options in extremis. No transfusion ratio substitutes for actually stopping the hemorrhage.
Hemoglobin is a lagging indicator

Hemoglobin concentration doesn't fall immediately with acute blood loss — relying on it as an early marker of severity underestimates how much blood has actually been lost. Treat the clinical picture and activation criteria, not a normal-looking hemoglobin on an early sample.

02

Brief Review

Massive transfusion protocols exist to deliver blood components rapidly, in a pre-determined balanced ratio, to a patient in life-threatening hemorrhage — replacing the older practice of resuscitating primarily with crystalloid and PRBCs alone, which produces a dilutional coagulopathy that makes bleeding worse, not better. Modern MTPs grew out of military damage control resuscitation: rapid hemorrhage control, permissive hypotension, minimized crystalloid, and early balanced blood product use, rather than treating bleeding and coagulopathy as separate, sequential problems.

The classic definition of massive transfusion — 10 or more units of PRBCs in 24 hours — is arbitrary (chosen because it approximates one blood volume) and often too slow to guide real-time decisions. More clinically useful dynamic definitions include ≥4 units of PRBCs in 1 hour with ongoing need, ≥3 units in any 1-hour period in the first 24 hours, or replacing ≥50% of blood volume within 3 hours. Ultra-massive transfusion — more than 20 units of PRBCs within 24–48 hours — represents an even more extreme resuscitation tier with its own outcomes literature.

Activation Criteria (In Place of "Signs and Symptoms")

Tool / SignDetails
Assessment of Blood Consumption (ABC) scoreOne point each for: penetrating mechanism, positive FAST exam, systolic BP ≤90 mmHg, heart rate ≥120 bpm. A score ≥2 suggests likely need for massive transfusion — a simple, fast bedside tool usable before labs return.
Dynamic bleeding criteria≥4 units PRBC in 1 hour with ongoing need, or ≥3 units in any 1-hour period — more responsive to real-time deterioration than the classic 10-units-in-24-hours definition.
Clinical gestaltRapid, pattern-based recognition by the treating team remains a legitimate and commonly used activation trigger — don't wait for a formal score to confirm what's clinically obvious.
Physiologic markersPersistent hypotension, tachycardia, rising lactate, and worsening base deficit despite initial resuscitation all support activation, independent of any single scoring tool.
04

Set-Up & Institutional Readiness (In Place of "Room Setup")

  • Pre-packaged, ratio-matched coolers ready for immediate release on activation — most level 1 trauma centers maintain these specifically to avoid the delay of assembling individual components under pressure.
  • Rapid infuser and large-bore access ready — but keep platelets off the rapid infuser specifically, running them through a separate line to preserve platelet function.
  • Clear communication pathway with the blood bank established in advance — who calls, what's said, how products get delivered — this is exactly the kind of process that fails under stress if it hasn't been rehearsed.
  • Whole blood availability where possible — increasingly preferred over reconstructed component ratios where logistics allow, since it more closely mimics what was actually lost; over 80% of surveyed level 1 trauma centers currently still approximate this with component ratios rather than true whole blood.
  • Viscoelastic testing capability (TEG/ROTEM) available within the first hour of admission where resources allow, to complement — not replace — the initial empiric ratio-based approach.
  • Regular MTP simulation/drills for the full team (trauma, anesthesia, blood bank, nursing) — activation is rare enough at most institutions that unpracticed teams lose critical time on process alone.

Rationale for MTP Management Steps

Why the 1:1:1 ratio replaced RBC-heavy resuscitation: transfusing PRBCs alone (or with disproportionate crystalloid) restores oxygen-carrying capacity but dilutes clotting factors and platelets that were never replaced — actively worsening the coagulopathy driving ongoing hemorrhage. A balanced ratio approximates whole blood's composition, treating the anemia and the coagulopathy simultaneously rather than sequentially.

Why TXA's benefit is time-locked to the first 3 hours: TXA works by inhibiting fibrinolysis at a point in the injury response when hyperfibrinolysis is actively contributing to bleeding. Given later, after the coagulation system has shifted toward a more prothrombotic state, the same antifibrinolytic effect no longer targets an active problem and instead just adds thrombotic risk without the offsetting hemostatic benefit — which is exactly what CRASH-2's time-stratified results showed.

Why calcium replacement is proactive, not reactive: citrate — the anticoagulant in stored blood products — binds ionized calcium, and large-volume transfusion delivers citrate faster than the liver can metabolize it. Since calcium is a required cofactor in the coagulation cascade, citrate-induced hypocalcemia directly impairs clot formation on top of dilutional coagulopathy — checking and replacing it early prevents a second, iatrogenic driver of the same bleeding problem you're trying to treat.

Why permissive hypotension and minimized crystalloid matter before definitive hemostasis: a higher blood pressure target can disrupt a fragile early clot before surgical or interventional control is achieved, effectively "popping the clot" and restarting hemorrhage. Excess crystalloid compounds the dilutional coagulopathy problem the balanced-ratio strategy is specifically designed to avoid — both practices exist to protect the clot you're trying to help the body form, not just to raise a number on the monitor.

Why TEG/ROTEM complements rather than replaces the empiric protocol: trial evidence (iTACTIC) found no significant outcome difference between viscoelastic-guided and conventional-coagulation-test-guided transfusion when both used immediate empiric transfusion up front — meaning the real value of viscoelastic testing shows up in identifying specific deficits (hyperfibrinolysis needing more TXA, isolated fibrinogen depletion needing cryoprecipitate) in a patient who continues bleeding despite standard MTP, not in replacing the initial ratio-based approach entirely.

Differential Diagnosis — Why Isn't This Working?

ConsiderationKey clues / distinguishing features
Ongoing surgical/anatomic bleeding source not yet controlledPersistent transfusion requirement despite adequate ratio and normalizing labs — this is a source-control problem, not a transfusion-ratio problem; escalate to OR/IR rather than transfusing further.
Pre-existing anticoagulant useHistory of warfarin, DOACs, or antiplatelet therapy — needs specific reversal (vitamin K/PCC for warfarin, agent-specific reversal for DOACs) on top of the standard MTP, since balanced-ratio products don't reverse a pharmacologic anticoagulant.
Underlying hemophilia or von Willebrand diseaseDisproportionate bleeding relative to injury severity, prior bleeding history — needs factor-specific replacement, not just ratio-based component transfusion.
DIC from a non-traumatic cause (sepsis, AFE, malignancy)Bleeding out of proportion to a mechanical injury, with a clinical context (infection, peripartum collapse) pointing toward a primary consumptive coagulopathy rather than dilutional trauma-induced coagulopathy.
Isolated severe thrombocytopenia from another causePlatelet count disproportionately low relative to the degree of hemodilution — consider immune thrombocytopenia or another primary hematologic process if the pattern doesn't fit simple dilution.
08

Workup & Disposition

Ongoing labs: continue serial CBC, coagulation panel, fibrinogen, ionized calcium, blood gas/lactate/base deficit roughly every 30–60 minutes through active resuscitation, and after every ~5 units of PRBCs specifically.

Stand-down criteria: deactivate the MTP once bleeding is controlled and the patient is hemodynamically stable — continuing ratio-based transfusion past the point of active hemorrhage risks unnecessary product use and volume overload (see the Transfusion Reactions crisis file for TACO specifically).

VTE prophylaxis timing: massive transfusion independently raises VTE risk, but prophylaxis has to be balanced against ongoing bleeding risk — this is a genuine timing judgment call made once hemostasis is achieved, not a fixed protocol step.

Disposition: ICU admission is standard for any patient who required massive transfusion, given the need for continued monitoring of coagulation status, electrolytes, and end-organ function.

Documentation and debrief: record total products given, ratio achieved, TXA timing, and viscoelastic results if obtained — both for this patient's ongoing care and for institutional review of MTP performance and blood product stewardship.
