01

Emergency Management — The HOTT Framework

Treat all reversible causes simultaneously, not sequentially — this is context-sensitive prioritization, not a fixed step order.
1
Control catastrophic external hemorrhage first
Tourniquet, hemostatic dressing, or direct pressure — before airway if the bleeding is severe enough. A patient exsanguinating dies faster than one with a compromised but still-functioning airway.
2
Secure the airway and maximize oxygenation
Address hypoxia — the "O" in HOTT — as a parallel priority alongside hemorrhage control, not strictly after it.
3
Bilateral chest decompression
Needle or finger thoracostomy bilaterally to exclude tension pneumothorax — see the dedicated Tension Pneumothorax crisis file for site selection and technique. Do this even without clear lateralizing signs in the traumatic arrest setting, since the affected side may not be obvious under time pressure.
4
Restore circulating volume
IV/IO fluid and blood product replacement — see the Massive Transfusion Protocol crisis file for ratio-based resuscitation once products are available.
5
Suspected tamponade — resuscitative thoracotomy
Primarily indicated for penetrating chest or abdominal trauma resulting in arrest, particularly when an organized rhythm is present or arrest duration has been short — even in asystole. Allows direct tamponade relief and, via clamping the descending aorta, proximal hemorrhage control for infradiaphragmatic bleeding. Requires appropriate Expertise, Equipment, Environment, and Elapsed time (the "4 E's").
6
REBOA — consider selectively, not as a default
May be reasonable for exsanguinating hemorrhage below the diaphragm, prior to full cardiovascular collapse, only by appropriately trained personnel. Contraindicated when penetrating chest trauma is the suspected cause. The evidence is genuinely mixed — see Rationale below before reaching for this reflexively.
7
Run conventional CPR in parallel if resources allow
Chest compressions, defibrillation, and epinephrine can run simultaneously with reversible-cause treatment when staffing allows — but they do not take priority over addressing the actual mechanical/volume problem, and shouldn't delay it.
8
Reassess for a medical cause preceding the trauma
If a medical event (arrhythmia, MI, seizure) is reasonably suspected to have caused the trauma (e.g., a single-vehicle crash with no clear mechanism), switch to standard medical cardiac arrest management (full ACLS) rather than the trauma-specific pathway.
Time-critical, not open-ended

A favorable outcome from resuscitative thoracotomy is rarely possible — even in penetrating trauma — if initiated more than 10 minutes after the onset of arrest. Prolonged CPR (>10 minutes) after reversible causes have been addressed is almost never associated with a good outcome. This isn't a reason to give up early; it's a reason to move fast on the causes that are actually reversible.

02

Brief Review

Traumatic cardiac arrest (TCA) results predominantly from severe hemorrhage, hypoxia, or a mechanical cause of obstructive shock (tension pneumothorax, cardiac tamponade) — mechanisms that are frequently, at least in principle, reversible if identified and treated fast enough. This distinguishes it from most out-of-hospital cardiac arrest, where a primary arrhythmic event is the dominant cause and standard ACLS pharmacology and compressions are the mainstay.

TCA predominantly affects young males and, globally, most commonly results from blunt mechanisms, though penetrating trauma carries meaningfully better resuscitative outcomes. Reported survival is dismal overall — global rates persistently below roughly 7.5% — though individual series range from 4% to 40% depending heavily on patient selection and how survival is measured. Despite the historically grim outlook, contemporary evidence indicates that survival with good or moderate neurological recovery is genuinely possible when reversible causes are treated fast and correctly.

Decision Surrogates (In Place of "Signs and Symptoms")

SurrogateHow it's used
Injury locationA practical stand-in for the likely cause of arrest when the true mechanism is uncertain — a chest or upper-abdominal wound raises suspicion for tamponade and points toward thoracotomy; a pelvic or lower-body injury pattern points toward exsanguination and favors transfusion/hemorrhage control over immediate thoracotomy.
Presenting ECG rhythmA rapid marker of physiological viability when exact arrest duration is unclear — an organized rhythm supports proceeding with thoracotomy even when timing is uncertain; asystole with an unclear or prolonged down-time argues against it, though a genuinely short asystolic arrest can still warrant intervention.
POCUS findingsReal-time identification of tamponade, pneumothorax, and hypovolemia directly guides which HOTT component to prioritize first rather than working through all four blindly.
Mechanism (blunt vs. penetrating)Penetrating trauma has consistently better resuscitative outcomes than blunt — this shapes both the threshold for aggressive intervention (thoracotomy) and the honest conversation about expected outcome.
04

Set-Up & Readiness (In Place of "Room Setup")

  • Tourniquets and hemostatic dressings immediately accessible and staff trained to apply them without hesitation for catastrophic external hemorrhage.
  • Bilateral thoracostomy as a default step in traumatic arrest protocols, not something reserved for confirmed lateralizing findings — adoption of this practice has increased significantly over time in systems that track it.
  • Pelvic binder immediately available for suspected pelvic fracture with hemorrhagic shock — in a patient with a stable airway, rapid binder application and transfer may be more valuable than airway intervention first.
  • Thoracotomy and REBOA capability matched to actual training level — these interventions should only be performed by teams with genuine expertise, not attempted opportunistically by providers without specific training and a system built to support the intervention.
  • Prehospital blood product availability where the system supports it — early blood, not just crystalloid, meaningfully changes outcomes in hemorrhagic traumatic arrest.
  • Clear futility and withdrawal criteria established in advance — resuscitation of TCA is not futile by default and should generally be attempted, but injuries obviously incompatible with life are a recognized exception worth having explicit protocol guidance on before the moment arrives.

Rationale for Trauma-Specific Prioritization

Why reversible causes are prioritized over conventional CPR here: chest compressions on a heart that's mechanically obstructed (tamponade, tension pneumothorax) or has nothing left to pump (severe hypovolemia) generate minimal or no meaningful cardiac output — the substrate compressions need to work simply isn't present. Treating the mechanical or volume problem restores that substrate; compressions alone on an unaddressed cause are largely futile, which is why the trauma-specific algorithm inverts the usual medical-arrest priority.

Why external hemorrhage control comes before airway: a patient actively exsanguinating from a compressible source dies from blood loss faster than from a still-functioning, if imperfect, airway. This mirrors the MARCH sequence used in prehospital/military trauma care (see the Crush Syndrome crisis file for a related discussion) — massive hemorrhage control is treated as the first, not the last, priority.

Why resuscitative thoracotomy is concentrated on penetrating chest/abdominal trauma: this is where the intervention has the clearest mechanistic match to the problem — direct tamponade relief and aortic cross-clamping for infradiaphragmatic hemorrhage control are specific, targeted fixes for specific injury patterns. Outcomes in blunt trauma are consistently worse; one series found tamponade on thoracotomy in 15% of blunt TCA cases, yet none of those specific patients survived, underscoring that finding a "reversible" cause doesn't guarantee reversibility once combined with the broader physiological insult of blunt trauma.

Why REBOA's own pivotal trial is a cautionary tale, not an endorsement: REBOA can raise proximal blood pressure by occluding the aorta, but that's a physiologic number, not a cure — if inflating the balloon delays the actual definitive hemorrhage control (surgery, embolization) that stops the bleeding, the net effect can be harm despite an improving monitor reading. The UK-REBOA trial's early termination for harm, with increased mortality and delayed time to definitive control, is exactly this mechanism playing out in a randomized trial — which is why REBOA is described here as selectively considered rather than a default escalation step.

Why injury location and rhythm are used as decision surrogates despite real uncertainty: in the field, the actual cause of arrest and the precise duration of no-flow time are frequently unknowable in real time. These two proxies are pragmatic, fast substitutes that let a team make a directionally reasonable decision under genuine uncertainty rather than waiting for information that isn't going to arrive in time to matter.

Differential Diagnosis — What Kind of Arrest Is This?

ConsiderationKey distinguishing features
Primary medical arrest preceding the traumaNo clear mechanism explaining the traumatic event itself (e.g., unexplained single-vehicle crash) — treat as standard medical cardiac arrest with full ACLS rather than the trauma-specific HOTT pathway.
Hemorrhagic (hypovolemic) arrestVisible or highly suspected massive external/internal bleeding as the dominant driver — prioritize hemorrhage control and transfusion over thoracic intervention unless tamponade/tension physiology is also present.
Tension pneumothorax as the dominant causeConfirmed or strongly suspected via exam/POCUS — bilateral decompression may resolve the arrest without needing thoracotomy at all.
Cardiac tamponade as the dominant causePenetrating chest wound, especially medial to the nipples/scapulae — the clearest indication for immediate thoracotomy; see the dedicated Tamponade crisis file.
Traumatic asphyxia / airway compromiseDirect airway injury or obstruction as the primary driver — securing the airway may be the single decisive intervention rather than a thoracic procedure.
Severe hypothermia (environmental trauma overlap)Prolonged exposure/cold-water immersion changes the resuscitation calculus entirely ("not dead until warm and dead") — see the Severe/Hypothermic Cardiac Arrest crisis file when this applies.
08

Post-ROSC Care & Disposition

Watch for new bleeding after thoracotomy: internal mammary and intercostal arteries severed by a clamshell incision can start bleeding once circulation is restored — be ready to clamp with small forceps as needed.

Facilitate rapid transfer without delay: every post-ROSC TCA patient needs prompt movement to the operating room, angiography suite, or definitive diagnostic imaging as appropriate — don't linger at the point of resuscitation once circulation returns.

Minimize REBOA inflation time if the balloon is in place, keeping occlusion as brief as feasible while facilitating transfer to definitive care — this directly addresses the "delayed definitive hemorrhage control" mechanism of harm identified in trial data.

Address metabolic derangement proactively: hyperkalemia and calcium imbalances are common in this population, particularly with massive transfusion or crush mechanism — see the Hyperkalemic Cardiac Arrest and Crush Syndrome crisis files for specific management.

Disposition: ICU/trauma team handoff with continued hemostatic resuscitation as needed. In austere or prolonged-evacuation settings, post-ROSC care of a TCA patient is recognized as extremely challenging and should prioritize evacuation to higher-level care whenever feasible rather than attempting extended stabilization in place.
