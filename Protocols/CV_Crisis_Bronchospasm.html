Severe Intraoperative Bronchospasm

Rule out the mechanical mimics first, deepen anesthesia, escalate through inhaled to IV bronchodilators — and watch for the dynamic hyperinflation that can quietly turn a respiratory problem into a hemodynamic one.

01

Emergency Management & Immediate Actions

Rule out the mimics before you commit to "bronchospasm" — treating a kinked tube with albuterol wastes the time you don't have.
1
100% O₂, disconnect from the ventilator, hand-ventilate
Squeezing the bag yourself lets you feel resistance directly and assess whether this is truly high-resistance bronchospasm versus a mechanical problem.
2
Rule out mechanical and other causes fast
Check for ETT kink or mucus obstruction, endobronchial (mainstem) intubation, tension pneumothorax, aspiration, pulmonary edema, and pulmonary embolism — all can mimic bronchospasm and none respond to bronchodilators. Reposition/pull back the tube and confirm placement by auscultation if endobronchial intubation is possible. If anaphylaxis is on the differential, check for rash and BP and stop any newly-given agent.
3
Deepen anesthesia
The first pharmacologic step. Increase inhaled volatile concentration — sevoflurane is a potent direct bronchodilator and a good choice here — or bolus/increase an IV agent (propofol, or ketamine specifically for its bronchodilating properties).
4
Inhaled beta-2 agonist
Albuterol 8–10 puffs via an MDI adapter in the circuit (a larger-than-usual dose is needed because a large fraction deposits on the ETT and circuit rather than reaching the lungs) or nebulized 2.5 mg. Add ipratropium, especially if a recent viral URI is a likely contributor.
5
Severe or unresponsive — IV epinephrine
1–2 mcg/kg IV, or titrated boluses of 10–100 mcg. Reach for this early rather than persisting with inhaled therapy alone if SpO₂ is critically low or the chest is silent — inhaled drug often can't reach the alveoli through a severely obstructed airway, while IV epinephrine works systemically regardless.
6
Refractory — add multimodal adjuncts
IV magnesium sulfate, ketamine bolus/infusion, IV lidocaine, or dexmedetomidine. Corticosteroids (methylprednisolone) help reduce recurrence/prolonged course but take 4–8 hours to act — give early anyway, but don't expect it to break the acute event.
7
Watch for dynamic hyperinflation ("breath stacking")
Severe obstruction can prevent full exhalation before the next breath is delivered, progressively raising intrathoracic pressure, reducing venous return, and causing hypotension or even PEA. If hypotension develops during severe bronchospasm, briefly disconnect the circuit to allow full passive exhalation — both diagnostic and therapeutic. Reduce respiratory rate and extend expiratory time; permissive hypercapnia is an acceptable trade-off.
8
If reversing neuromuscular blockade
Use sugammadex rather than neostigmine if the patient is actively wheezing — neostigmine's muscarinic activity can worsen bronchoconstriction.
Small share of claims, disproportionate severity

Bronchospasm accounts for only about 2% of anesthesia closed-claims cases, but roughly 90% of those specific claims involve severe brain injury or death — this is a low-incidence, high-lethality event when it goes wrong.

02

Brief Review

Bronchospasm is smooth-muscle-mediated narrowing of the bronchioles, producing airflow obstruction concentrated in the expiratory phase. Perioperative causes include reactive airway disease (asthma), COPD, anaphylaxis, direct mechanical/chemical airway irritation (ETT stimulating the carina or a mainstem bronchus, aspiration of gastric acid), and drug-related histamine release.

A large retrospective review found combined intraoperative bronchospasm and laryngospasm incidence around 1.7%, more frequent in older patients and those with active asthma. Even though it's a fairly infrequent event overall, its severity profile in closed-claims data (above) makes rapid, correct management disproportionately important relative to its incidence.

04

Prevention & Set-Up (In Place of "Room Setup")

  • Preoperative optimization for known reactive airway disease: confirm the patient is on their routine inhaled beta-agonist/steroid regimen, consider having them use their inhaler the morning of surgery, and postpone elective cases with poorly-controlled asthma when feasible.
  • Risk history matters more than exam findings the day of surgery — a history of frequent exacerbations, hospital visits, or prior intubation/mechanical ventilation for a severe asthma attack signals real risk even in a patient who looks and sounds fine at preop assessment.
  • Consider a supraglottic airway instead of an ETT when the case allows it, to reduce direct tracheal/carinal stimulation in a hyperreactive airway.
  • Ensure an adequate depth of anesthesia before airway manipulation — laryngoscopy and intubation performed too lightly are a well-recognized trigger.
  • Be cautious with histamine-releasing drugs (e.g., older-generation neuromuscular blockers like atracurium, morphine) in higher-risk patients.
  • Plan extubation deliberately — either fully awake or under a genuinely deep plane, avoiding the intermediate ("stage 2") depth that raises airway reactivity risk; IV lidocaine before extubation can help blunt reflex bronchoconstriction.
  • Reoccurrence on emergence is common once volatile anesthetic is turned off — keep an albuterol nebulizer and IV bronchodilator options accessible through emergence and early recovery, not just during the case itself.

06

Rationale for Bronchospasm Management Steps

Why ruling out mechanical causes comes before treating "bronchospasm": a kinked tube, mainstem intubation, or tension pneumothorax can produce an identical clinical picture — high airway pressure, desaturation, difficulty ventilating — without any bronchodilator having a chance of helping. Time spent nebulizing albuterol into a mechanically obstructed circuit is time not spent fixing the actual problem.

Why deepening anesthesia is the first pharmacologic step: a light anesthetic plane during airway stimulation is one of the most common precipitants of reflex bronchoconstriction, and volatile agents (sevoflurane in particular) are themselves direct bronchodilators — this single intervention can address both the trigger and the treatment simultaneously.

Why IV epinephrine gets used even though inhaled therapy is first-line in outpatient asthma care: inhaled drug delivery depends on the drug actually reaching distal airways, which becomes progressively less reliable as obstruction worsens — in a genuinely severe or "silent chest" presentation, systemic (IV) epinephrine bypasses that delivery problem entirely and acts through the same beta-2 mechanism regardless of how obstructed the airways are.

Why neostigmine is avoided when the patient is wheezing: neostigmine inhibits acetylcholinesterase non-selectively, increasing muscarinic as well as nicotinic cholinergic tone — the muscarinic effect promotes bronchoconstriction, directly working against what you're trying to treat. Sugammadex reverses rocuronium/vecuronium by direct encapsulation rather than through the cholinergic system, so it doesn't carry this risk.

Why dynamic hyperinflation causes hypotension that looks cardiac but isn't: when expiratory time is inadequate relative to the degree of obstruction, each subsequent breath adds volume on top of air that hasn't fully left the lungs. The resulting rise in intrathoracic pressure compresses the great veins and reduces venous return to the heart — a mechanical, not a primary cardiac, cause of hypotension. This is exactly why briefly disconnecting the circuit (allowing full passive exhalation) is both the diagnostic test and the treatment, rather than reaching first for fluids or vasopressors.

Differential Diagnosis

MimicKey distinguishing features
ETT obstruction (kink, mucus plug, cuff herniation)Passing a suction catheter fails to advance freely, or direct visualization/fiberoptic check reveals the obstruction — doesn't respond to bronchodilators.
Endobronchial (mainstem) intubationUnilateral breath sounds, asymmetric chest rise; resolves immediately with pulling the tube back to correct depth.
Tension pneumothoraxUnilateral absent breath sounds, tracheal deviation, hypotension, distended neck veins — a distinct mechanical emergency, not bronchospasm.
Pulmonary aspirationOften a known event (witnessed regurgitation) or occurs in a high-risk patient; gastric acid causes irritant-mediated bronchoconstriction that responds less reliably to epinephrine than immune-mediated bronchospasm — focus on suctioning and supportive care alongside bronchodilator therapy.
Pulmonary edemaPink frothy secretions, diffuse crackles rather than wheeze predominating, different hemodynamic picture (often volume-overloaded rather than obstructed).
Pulmonary embolismSudden desaturation and hypotension without a clear airway-pressure/wheeze pattern; consider especially with new hypotension out of proportion to apparent respiratory findings.
AnaphylaxisBronchospasm can be a component of anaphylaxis itself — check for rash, angioedema, and hypotension out of proportion to a pure airway problem, and treat as anaphylaxis (epinephrine, stop the trigger) if those features are present.
08

Workup & Disposition

Confirm resolution before proceeding: reassess airway pressures, breath sounds, and capnography waveform once treatment is given — don't assume resolution just because the immediate crisis feels over.

Chest imaging if there's any residual concern: a chest X-ray can rule out a missed pneumothorax or confirm tube position if endobronchial intubation was in the differential.

Emergence and extubation planning: anticipate recurrence as volatile anesthetic is discontinued; have bronchodilator therapy immediately available through extubation and early recovery, and plan a deep or fully-awake extubation deliberately rather than defaulting to routine timing.

Disposition: a severe or refractory episode — especially one involving dynamic hyperinflation with hemodynamic compromise, or requiring IV epinephrine/multimodal rescue — warrants postoperative ICU-level monitoring rather than routine PACU-to-floor progression.

Documentation and future planning: record the trigger if identifiable, treatments given and response, and flag the chart for anesthesia technique adjustments (SGA over ETT, avoidance of a specific drug, more aggressive preoperative optimization) on future anesthetics.
