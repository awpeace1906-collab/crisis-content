Crisis Management — Envenomation — Middle East

Middle East

Three species drive the overwhelming majority of medically significant envenomation in this region. All have narrow, geography-specific antivenom requirements — a product that works for one population of the same species elsewhere is not a safe assumption here.

Middle East Snake — Viperidae Hemotoxic (VICC)
Saw-Scaled Viper
Echis coloratus (Arabian/Levant saw-scaled viper — Israel, Jordan, Arabian Peninsula) and E. carinatus (Persian Gulf, Iran, further east). Second most common cause of snake envenomation in Israel; one of "the Big Four" venomous snakes across the broader Middle East/South Asia range
Onset / Timeline
Local pain and swelling within minutes of the bite. Hemostatic failure follows, though timeline and severity vary meaningfully between species/populations — E. coloratus in Israel is associated with lower mortality (rare, isolated fatalities from renal failure) than E. carinatus further east, where regional mortality estimates run higher.
Clinical Syndrome
Local swelling plus venom-induced consumptive coagulopathy. Roughly one-third of victims bleed or develop anemia. Severe outcomes (hemorrhage, acute kidney injury) are the exception rather than the rule with E. coloratus specifically, but should still be anticipated and screened for in every case.
Field / Prehospital Management
Standard viper approach: immobilize the limb, remove constricting items, transport. No tourniquet, incision, or suction.
ED / Resuscitation Management
Baseline and serial coagulation studies, renal function, and urinalysis (proteinuria is a treatment-threshold marker, see below). A bedside whole blood clotting test is a reasonable adjunct where formal coags are slow to return.
Antivenom
A regional monovalent equine IgG antivenom is used for E. coloratus envenomation, dosed by symptoms rather than a fixed protocol: an initial 20 mL IV dose for systemic involvement, with repeat 10 mL doses for relapse. In outcome data, 49% of patients showed no systemic symptoms and received no antivenom at all; of those treated, 63% resolved with a single 20 mL dose. A published decision model favors treating when bleeding risk is elevated — specifically with proteinuria, BUN >7 mmol/L, platelet count <100,000/µL, or hemoglobin <13 g/dL — rather than treating every confirmed bite empirically.
Disposition
Admit any suspected bite for coagulation monitoring regardless of initial appearance. Antivenom decision follows the symptom/lab-based threshold above rather than universal empiric dosing.
Pearls / Pitfalls
Echis antivenoms have documented narrow cross-reactivity — a product developed against one regional population (e.g., West African E. ocellatus, or Indian E. carinatus) is not reliably interchangeable with what's needed here, and mismatched antivenom has caused real treatment failures. Confirm the specific regional product your facility stocks. Serum sickness occurs in a notable share of antivenom-treated patients (reported as high as 44% in one series) — this is a real cost of treatment that factors into the symptom-triggered approach rather than treating everyone empirically.
Middle East / North Africa Scorpion Neurotoxic
Deathstalker Scorpion
Leiurus quinquestriatus. Also called: Palestine yellow scorpion, Omdurman scorpion, Israeli desert scorpion, Naqab desert scorpion. Rarely fatal in healthy adults — dangerous mainly to children, the elderly, and those with cardiac disease
Onset / Timeline
Sting pain is immediate and severe. In vulnerable patients — overwhelmingly children — systemic signs can progress over hours to a catecholamine-driven autonomic storm.
Clinical Syndrome
Venom neurotoxins act on sodium and potassium channels, driving a massive catecholamine surge. In severe pediatric envenomation this produces hypertension, tachycardia, cardiogenic shock, and pulmonary edema, alongside CNS findings — lethargy, confusion, agitation, and reduced consciousness. A high rate of pancreatitis has been reported in Israeli case series following envenomation. Nausea, vomiting, and abdominal cramps are common even in milder cases. Healthy adults typically experience severe local pain without progressing to this systemic picture.
Field / Prehospital Management
Analgesia and urgent transport, with a markedly lower threshold for urgency in pediatric, elderly, or cardiac-history patients — this is where the rare fatalities occur.
ED / Resuscitation Management
Local infiltration (1% lidocaine, digital block for a digit sting) is an effective, underused option for pain control. For hypertension and the catecholamine-driven cardiovascular picture, alpha-blockade (prazosin) or hydralazine have shown benefit in case series and small trials; beta-blockers, cardiac glycosides, and high-dose corticosteroids are not recommended. Mechanical ventilation and full ICU support for pulmonary edema or respiratory failure in severe pediatric cases.
Antivenom
Multiple regional products exist — the National Antivenom and Vaccine Production Center (Riyadh, Saudi Arabia), Sanofi Pasteur (France), and Twyford (Germany) all manufacture Leiurus-directed antivenom. None are FDA-approved in the US; access there is investigational-only (the US Armed Forces and the Florida Antivenin Bank hold IND applications for military/emergency use). Exact dosing for non-Centruroides scorpion antivenoms remains poorly established — treatment is guided by clinical severity and response rather than a fixed protocol, unlike the well-defined Anascorp dosing for North American Centruroides.
Disposition
Any pediatric patient with systemic signs needs ICU-capable care given the cardiogenic and CNS risk. Healthy adults with isolated severe local pain can typically be managed with analgesia and observation.
Pearls / Pitfalls
The core danger here is age-dependent, not dose-dependent in the way many envenomations are — a sting that's "just very painful" in an adult can be genuinely life-threatening in a child from the same scorpion. Don't extrapolate reassurance from an adult presentation to a pediatric one. Confirm which regional antivenom product (if any) is actually accessible before it's needed — availability varies significantly by country.
Middle East, Cyprus, Central Asia Snake — Viperidae Hemotoxic (VICC)
Levant Viper
Macrovipera lebetina. Also called: blunt-nosed viper. One of the largest and most dangerous vipers across its range, spanning the Levant, parts of the Arabian Peninsula, Cyprus, and into Central Asia
Onset / Timeline
Significant local pain and swelling develop rapidly given the species' large size and substantial venom yield; systemic coagulopathy follows over the subsequent hours.
Clinical Syndrome
Severe local tissue effects — swelling, blistering, and necrosis risk — combined with venom-induced consumption coagulopathy and systemic hemorrhage. Bites from this species are considered among the most medically significant viper envenomations in its range given the combination of large body size, substantial venom yield, and potent hemotoxic activity.
Field / Prehospital Management
Standard viper approach: immobilize, remove constricting items, transport. No tourniquet, incision, or suction.
ED / Resuscitation Management
Serial coagulation studies and limb assessment as for other major regional vipers. Aggressive wound care given real necrosis risk from the substantial local tissue destruction this species can cause.
Antivenom
Regional coverage is genuinely fragmented across this species' wide range. The Razi Vaccine and Serum Research Institute (Iran) produces a pentavalent/hexavalent product specifically covering M. lebetina obtusa alongside several other regional vipers. Egyptian VACSERA polyvalent antivenom has also demonstrated cross-neutralization against Macrovipera lebetina species in laboratory studies. Confirm which specific regional product is stocked — this is not a species with one simple continent-wide antivenom answer.
Disposition
Admit for coagulation monitoring and aggressive wound surveillance given the necrosis risk from this species' substantial local tissue destruction potential.
Pearls / Pitfalls
Don't underestimate this species relative to the smaller, more frequently discussed saw-scaled viper on this page — Levant viper bites carry real severity from sheer venom volume alone, and antivenom sourcing needs to be confirmed for the specific country/region rather than assumed.
Non-Critical — Supportive Care Only
Not life-threatening and doesn't require antivenom or critical-care intervention. Not a full workup — just enough to recognize and reassure.
  • Camel Spider (Solifugae — not a true spider) — large, fast-moving, and alarming in appearance with powerful jaws that can cause a mechanically painful bite, but not venomous at all. Care: clean the wound, standard laceration/puncture care; no antivenom exists because none is needed.
  • Field/Common Scorpions (Buthus spp. and similar, excluding deathstalker) — painful sting, local swelling; not typically dangerous to healthy adults. Care: ice, analgesia, reassurance; treat pediatric or elderly patients with the same caution as any scorpion sting until deathstalker exposure is excluded.
