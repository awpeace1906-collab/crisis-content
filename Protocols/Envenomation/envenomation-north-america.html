North America

North America Snake — Crotalinae Hemotoxic / Cytotoxic
Pit Vipers (Rattlesnake, Copperhead, Cottonmouth)
Crotalinae — ~98% of US venomous snakebites. Also called: rattler, diamondback, sidewinder (rattlesnake) · highland moccasin, chunk head (copperhead) · water moccasin, gapper (cottonmouth)
Onset / Timeline
Pain and local swelling begin within minutes. Copperhead and cottonmouth bites are dominated by local tissue effects; rattlesnake bites more often produce early, late, and recurrent coagulopathy layered on top of local injury. Coagulopathy can lag the local exam by hours and can recur after initial control.
Clinical Syndrome
Progressive edema, ecchymosis, and hemorrhagic bullae at the bite site. Hematologic effects: thrombocytopenia, hypofibrinogenemia, elevated PT/PTT. Systemic signs when significant: hypotension, perioral or extremity paresthesias, metallic or rubbery taste, nausea.
Field / Prehospital Management
Remove rings, watches, and constricting clothing before swelling progresses. Immobilize the extremity at heart level. Mark and time the leading edge of swelling/erythema for serial comparison. Do not use tourniquets, ice, incision, suction, or electrical shock — none are proven and several cause harm. Avoid NSAIDs given bleeding risk. Rapid transport; no field antivenom.
ED / Resuscitation Management
Baseline CBC, platelets, PT/PTT, fibrinogen. Re-mark leading edge every 15–30 minutes until progression stops. True compartment syndrome from crotalid envenomation is rare — most tense swelling is subcutaneous, not compartmental; prophylactic fasciotomy is not recommended and compartment pressure measurement should be reserved for exam findings inconsistent with venom effect alone.
Antivenom
Two FDA-approved products, both effective for all three US pit viper genera. CroFab (FabAV): initial dose 4–6 vials (4 vials for isolated copperhead bites), often needs scheduled maintenance dosing (2 vials q6h × 3 doses after initial control) to prevent recurrence. Anavip (Fab2AV): fixed initial dose of 10 vials regardless of age, longer half-life associated with lower rates of late/recurrent coagulopathy, no standard maintenance dose but more patients need repeat initial-control dosing (registry data: median 18 vials Anavip vs 10 vials CroFab to reach control). Reassess q1h against exam and labs; redose either product until initial control achieved — arrest of local progression, improving systemic signs, correcting coagulopathy.
Disposition
Observe a minimum of 18–24 hours after initial control regardless of antivenom used. Recheck coagulation labs 6–12 hours after control, again prior to discharge, and twice after discharge given recurrence risk — this applies to both products but especially CroFab.
Pearls / Pitfalls
No prehospital intervention has ever been proven to change outcome — the job in the field is removing constricting items and getting the patient to antivenom, nothing else. Watch for alpha-gal (galactose-α-1,3-galactose) sensitization causing anaphylactoid reactions to CroFab, which is sheep-derived — regionally more relevant in the southeastern US where tick-associated alpha-gal allergy is common. Recurrent coagulopathy after apparent control is a real phenomenon, not a monitoring failure — this is why the discharge lab schedule exists.
North America Snake — Elapidae Neurotoxic
Coral Snake
Micrurus fulvius (Eastern), M. tener (Texas). Also called: candy stick, candy snake. Delayed neurotoxicity — distinct course from pit vipers
Onset / Timeline
Deceptive. Local findings at the bite are often minimal or absent — no significant pain or swelling like a pit viper bite. Neurologic signs can be delayed several hours before appearing, and once they start, progression can be rapid and does not reliably reverse with antivenom once established.
Clinical Syndrome
Bulbar findings first: ptosis, dysarthria, dysphagia, diplopia. Progresses to generalized skeletal muscle weakness and paralysis, then respiratory failure if untreated. The mismatch between an unimpressive bite site and the eventual severity is the core danger.
Field / Prehospital Management
Same general principles as pit viper — immobilize, remove constricting items, transport. The critical field task is not under-triaging a bite just because it looks unremarkable.
ED / Resuscitation Management
Extended observation is mandatory even in asymptomatic patients, given delayed onset — historically 24 hours. Frequent neurologic and respiratory checks (cranial nerves, vital capacity/NIF if any bulbar signs appear). Have airway equipment ready; these patients can progress to needing prolonged ventilator support.
Antivenom
North American Coral Snake Antivenin (NACSA, Wyeth/Pfizer) is the only FDA-approved product and production stopped in 2003. Remaining stock has run on serial FDA expiration-date extensions of a single lot (CL6814) for years; check current lot status with poison control (1-800-222-1222) since availability is not guaranteed and changes. Dose 3–5 vials in adults, decreased in small children per response. There is no other FDA-approved alternative in the US; Mexican Coralmyn (Bioclon) has been used via special arrangement in some shortage situations. Historic practice was empiric treatment of any suspected bite given irreversible neurotoxicity risk; more recent data suggest a symptom-triggered approach doesn't worsen outcomes and avoids adverse events from treating asymptomatic patients — but this is a genuine access-driven tradeoff, not just a clinical preference, given how scarce the product is.
Disposition
Minimum 12–24 hour observation even if asymptomatic at presentation, given delayed onset. Any neurologic sign warrants ICU-level monitoring.
Pearls / Pitfalls
"Red touch yellow, kill a fellow; red touch black, venom lack" distinguishes coral snake from mimics like the scarlet kingsnake in the US — this rhyme does not reliably apply outside North America and should never be used to clear a bite in another region. Minimal local findings are not reassurance — they are the expected presentation. Antivenom scarcity is a real operational constraint here, not a hypothetical; know your hospital's and region's current supply before you need it.
North America Spider — Theridiidae Neurotoxic
Black Widow
Latrodectus mactans and related spp. Also called: hourglass spider, shoe button spider. Syndrome: latrodectism
Onset / Timeline
Sharp pinprick pain at the bite site, then local cramping that spreads over 15 minutes to a few hours. Untreated, the syndrome typically runs 1–3 days.
Clinical Syndrome
Severe muscle cramping and rigidity — abdominal wall can become boardlike and mimic a surgical abdomen, but without true peritoneal tenderness. Autonomic findings: diaphoresis, tachycardia, hypertension. Bite site itself is often unremarkable, so the exam and history carry more weight than the wound.
Field / Prehospital Management
Supportive — analgesia as tolerated, transport. No field-specific antidote or intervention beyond comfort measures.
ED / Resuscitation Management
Opioids and benzodiazepines are the mainstay for pain and rigidity control, though often only partially effective. Calcium gluconate has been used historically for muscle cramping but evidence is weak and it is not a substitute for antivenom in severe cases.
Antivenom
Merck's Lyovac Antivenin (Latrodectus mactans) — whole equine IgG — remains the product available in North America, though manufacturer low-inventory periods occur and current stock should be confirmed with poison control. Reserved for severe or refractory envenomation: seizures, hypertensive crisis, respiratory compromise, or intractable pain unresponsive to opioids and benzodiazepines. Dose: 1 vial diluted in 50 mL NS, IV over 15–30 minutes. Skin or conjunctival sensitivity testing is required first given real anaphylaxis risk (reported up to ~5%); history of asthma or atopy is a relative contraindication. Fear of this reaction is the main reason antivenom is underused in the US relative to how effective it is.
Disposition
Most cases are managed with supportive care and observation without antivenom. Admit for antivenom administration or refractory symptoms.
Pearls / Pitfalls
Consider latrodectism in unexplained abdominal rigidity without true peritoneal signs, especially with autonomic findings and a plausible exposure — garages, woodpiles, outdoor storage. The bite site will often not help you; the history and autonomic picture will. Antivenom underuse in US practice reflects genuine reluctance around the anaphylaxis rate, not a lack of efficacy — know the skin-test protocol before you need it.
North America Scorpion Neurotoxic
Bark Scorpion
Centruroides sculpturatus. Clinically significant scorpion in the US, concentrated in the Southwest (Arizona and neighboring states)
Onset / Timeline
Pain at the sting site is immediate and often out of proportion to visible findings — there's usually no significant local lesion. Systemic neurotoxic signs, when they occur, can develop within 30 minutes to a few hours, especially in young children.
Clinical Syndrome
Graded severity. Grade I–II: local pain and paresthesias only. Grade III–IV: either cranial nerve dysfunction (roving/abnormal eye movements, blurred vision, slurred speech, dysphagia) or somatic skeletal neuromuscular dysfunction (jerking, restlessness) — or both. The somatic findings in kids can look like seizure activity but are not epileptic.
Field / Prehospital Management
Analgesia and transport, with lower threshold for urgency in pediatric patients or anyone with early cranial nerve or neuromuscular signs.
ED / Resuscitation Management
Grade the envenomation on cranial nerve and somatic findings to decide on antivenom. Prior to antivenom availability, severe cases were managed with benzodiazepines, sedation, and occasionally intubation for supportive control — that approach is now reserved for cases where antivenom is unavailable or as an adjunct.
Antivenom
Anascorp (equine F(ab')2) — initial dose is a fixed 3 vials IV over 10 minutes regardless of age or weight, for any patient with Grade III–IV signs. Additional 1-vial doses may be given every 30–60 minutes based on symptom resolution. Mean time to symptom resolution after treatment is roughly 1.4 hours. Distribution is exclusive through AnovoRx rather than standard pharmacy wholesale — a stocking/logistics point worth knowing before a shortage-of-time situation, not during one.
Disposition
Patients typically can be discharged home once symptoms resolve after antivenom, often within a few hours of treatment.
Pearls / Pitfalls
The "tap test" — light tapping over the sting site reproducing severe pain with no visible lesion — is a useful bedside clue that distinguishes this from other bites/stings. There's active debate over whether the fixed 3-vial initial dose is necessary versus a 1-vial-then-reassess strategy for Grade III–IV envenomation; this is evolving practice, not settled. Pediatric patients remain the population most likely to progress to severe neurotoxicity.
SW United States, N. Mexico Snake — Crotalinae Neurotoxic (variable)
Mojave Rattlesnake
Crotalus scutulatus. Also called: Mojave green (though color varies — green, brown, yellow, gray). The pit viper that breaks the "pit vipers are hemotoxic" rule taught for every other species on this page
Onset / Timeline
Local pain and swelling on bite, similar to other pit vipers initially — but this species carries a real risk of a very different second act, depending on which venom phenotype delivered the bite.
Clinical Syndrome
This species has at least two distinct venom phenotypes, and telling them apart matters clinically. Type A venom is dominated by Mojave toxin (MTX), a potent presynaptic β-neurotoxin with little hemorrhagic activity — bites can progress to weakness, ptosis, and respiratory compromise with deceptively unimpressive local swelling, genuinely unusual for a North American pit viper. Type B venom lacks MTX and instead is rich in hemorrhagic metalloproteinases — a more typical crotalid picture of significant local tissue damage and coagulopathy. Mixed Type A+B individuals also exist, and some populations additionally carry a distinct myotoxin (Type M). Geography offers only a rough guide: Type A predominates in California, southern Nevada, southwest Utah, and parts of Arizona/New Mexico/Texas, while Type B is concentrated in central Arizona — but populations overlap and mix, so the venom type of any individual snake can't be assumed from location alone.
Field / Prehospital Management
Standard pit viper approach: immobilize, remove constricting items, transport. Given the neurotoxic risk, monitor for early bulbar or respiratory signs even if local swelling looks unimpressive — don't let a mild-looking bite site delay transport.
ED / Resuscitation Management
Assess for both pictures — coagulation studies as for any pit viper bite, AND close neurologic/respiratory monitoring given the real possibility of a Type A envenomation. A bite with minimal local swelling but early neurologic complaints should raise this species specifically, especially in its range.
Antivenom
CroFab and Anavip are both derived from venom pools that include Mojave rattlesnake venom and are considered effective against this species, including the Type A neurotoxic component — this is not a species requiring a separate antivenom, unlike several other regional-specificity cases on this site. Standard pit viper dosing and initial-control principles apply.
Disposition
Extend the standard pit-viper observation window with a lower threshold for prolonged neurologic monitoring, given that significant neurotoxicity can develop despite modest local findings.
Pearls / Pitfalls
This is the one North American pit viper where "mild local swelling" is not reassuring on its own — a Type A envenomation can be doing its real damage neurologically while the bite site looks unremarkable. Don't apply the general crotalid teaching point (local findings track severity) uncritically to a confirmed or suspected Mojave rattlesnake bite.
Non-Critical — Supportive Care Only
These North American species cause real pain/reaction but are not typically life-threatening and do not require antivenom or critical-care level intervention. Not a full workup — just enough to recognize and reassure.
  • Brown Recluse (fiddleback, violin spider) — most bites self-limited; minority progress to local necrotic lesion over days. Care: conservative wound care, no early debridement; rare pediatric systemic hemolysis is the exception — admit if it occurs.
  • Hymenoptera (bee/wasp/hornet/fire ant) — local pain and, with fire ants, a sterile pustule. Care: ice, analgesia; systemic/allergic reaction routes to Anaphylaxis, not this page.
  • Gila Monster — tenacious chewing bite, painful, occasional hypotension/anaphylactoid reaction; no US deaths reported in 70+ years. Care: wound irrigation, remove retained teeth, supportive care; observe ~6h.
  • Puss Caterpillar (asp, tree asp) — severe burning contact pain from hidden spines, resolves within hours. Care: tape-strip spine removal (don't scrape), ice, antihistamines.
  • Velvet Ant ("cow killer") — dramatic pain, minimal actual severity. Care: reassurance, ice, analgesia.
