01

Recognition

  • The head delivers and the shoulders do not follow with gentle downward traction and the next contraction
  • Turtle sign — the head delivers, then retracts tightly back against the perineum as the anterior shoulder catches on the symphysis
  • The head fails to restitute, sitting stuck facing directly down or up rather than rotating to one side
  • A head-to-body interval beyond about 60 seconds, or any delivery requiring maneuvers beyond gentle axial traction — this is the working definition, and it is deliberately broad

You cannot predict it. Most shoulder dystocias occur in normal-weight infants of women with no risk factors, and the recognized associations — macrosomia, diabetes, prior dystocia, instrumental delivery, prolonged second stage — have poor predictive value individually. Every delivery is a delivery where this can happen, which is why the response has to be drilled rather than reasoned out on the spot.

The clock is the whole problem

Once the head is out, the chest cannot expand and the cord is compressed against the pelvis. Fetal pH falls by roughly 0.04 units per minute from that moment. Beyond about 5 minutes, the risk of hypoxic-ischemic injury rises steeply. Announce the time the head delivered and have someone call it out every 30 seconds. Under pressure, teams consistently believe far less time has elapsed than actually has — a called clock is the only reliable correction for that.

02

The Three Things That Cause the Injuries

Most permanent harm from shoulder dystocia comes not from the impaction but from the response to it.

  • Never apply fundal pressure. It drives the anterior shoulder harder onto the symphysis, worsening the impaction it is meant to relieve, and it causes uterine rupture. Suprapubic pressure is the correct external maneuver, and the two get confused under stress precisely because both involve a hand on the abdomen. Say which one you want, and watch where the hand goes.
  • Never apply excessive or lateral traction to the head. Brachial plexus injury is caused by stretching the neck away from the shoulder. Traction is gentle, axial — in the line of the fetal spine, not downward-and-sideways — and it is applied only in coordination with a maneuver that is actually creating space.
  • Never twist or rotate the head. Rotation is done internally, on the shoulders, with a hand in the vagina. Turning the head turns nothing but the cervical spine.

Shoulder dystocia is among the most litigated events in obstetrics, and the record of what was and was not done matters. That is a secondary reason to get it right; the primary one is that these three errors are the ones that leave a child with a permanent deficit.

03

What You Need

  • People. The single most useful resource. Call for obstetric, anesthetic and neonatal help the moment you recognize it. You will need at least two for the legs, one for suprapubic pressure, one scribe, and a neonatal team.
  • A scribe and a visible clock. Every maneuver, with its time. This is both a clinical aid and the record.
  • A bed that flattens, and the ability to drop the foot of it and remove the end.
  • A stool — suprapubic pressure is delivered with body weight from above, which needs height.
  • Neonatal resuscitation equipment, warm and ready.
  • Cord gas syringes. Paired arterial and venous samples after delivery.
  • Episiotomy scissors and local anesthetic — not routinely required, but available.
  • Postpartum hemorrhage supplies. Prolonged dystocia is followed by atony often enough to anticipate it.
  • For symphysiotomy only (see §06): scalpel, urinary catheter, local anesthetic, and two assistants.

The Escalation Sequence

Roughly 30 seconds per maneuver. Move on. Do not repeat what has already failed.

1
Call for help. Note the time. Stop pushing.
Say the words “shoulder dystocia” out loud — it summons a specific response that “I need a hand” does not. Ask the mother to stop pushing for the moment; maternal effort against an impacted shoulder achieves nothing and makes the maneuvers harder. Flatten the bed and bring her buttocks to its edge.
2
McRoberts — hyperflex the hips
One person per leg, hips sharply flexed onto the abdomen and slightly abducted. This does not enlarge the pelvis. It rotates the symphysis cephalad and flattens the sacral promontory, which frees the anterior shoulder from behind the pubic bone. Simple, non-invasive, and it resolves a large share of cases on its own.
3
Suprapubic pressure — downward and lateral, from the fetal back
Stand on a stool. Heel of the hand just above the symphysis, over the anterior shoulder, pressing downward and toward the fetal chest — that is, from the side the baby's back is on. The aim is to adduct the shoulder, narrowing the bisacromial diameter, and nudge it into the oblique. Steady pressure first; a rocking CPR-like motion if steady fails. McRoberts plus suprapubic pressure resolves the majority of dystocias.
4
Consider an episiotomy — for access, not for space
The obstruction is bone against bone, and cutting the perineum does not relieve it. What it can do is give your hand room for the internal maneuvers that follow. Not routine; a judgment call when you cannot get a hand in.
5
Internal rotation — get a hand in posteriorly
Enter alongside the posterior shoulder, where the sacral hollow gives you room. Then either: Rubin II — fingers behind the anterior shoulder, pushing it toward the fetal chest to adduct and rotate it; or Woods screw — fingers on the front of the posterior shoulder, rotating the opposite way. Combining Rubin II with Woods on opposite shoulders, turning them like a screw, is more effective than either alone. The aim is to move the shoulders out of the anteroposterior plane into the oblique. If one direction fails, try the reverse.
6
Deliver the posterior arm — the highest-yield internal maneuver
Reach into the sacral hollow, follow the posterior humerus to the elbow, and press the antecubital fossa to flex the forearm. Grasp the forearm or hand — never the upper arm, which fractures — and sweep it across the chest and out over the face. Removing one arm reduces the shoulder diameter by around 20% and usually frees the impaction immediately. A humeral or clavicular fracture during this maneuver is an accepted cost; it heals, and hypoxic brain injury does not.
7
Gaskin — roll her onto all fours
Onto hands and knees. The change in pelvic geometry and the effect of gravity frees a proportion of dystocias on its own, and the shoulder that was posterior becomes the accessible one. Underused, because it looks disruptive mid-emergency. It is quick, it needs no equipment, and the internal maneuvers can all be repeated in this position. Impractical with an epidural or in an exhausted woman — but worth attempting when it is not.
8
Then the last resorts — and say so out loud
If the sequence has been worked through twice without success, you are in genuinely rare territory: deliberate clavicular fracture, the Zavanelli maneuver, abdominal rescue, or symphysiotomy (§06). All carry major morbidity, all are rarely performed, and the choice depends entirely on whether an operating room is reachable. Get the most senior help available and state clearly what you are about to do.

06

Symphysiotomy

A resource-limited last resort with a difficult history and real, permanent maternal cost.

Where it belongs. Symphysiotomy retains a narrow place in settings where cesarean delivery is not available in the time the fetus has. Where an operating room is reachable, Zavanelli and abdominal rescue are the correct last resorts and symphysiotomy is not. The procedure was historically performed on women without consent and without adequate analgesia, and it left many with lifelong disability — that history is a reason to hold the indication narrowly and to obtain consent wherever it is possible, not a reason to pretend the procedure does not exist.

1
Confirm there is no surgical option in time
State it aloud and record it. All standard maneuvers must have failed, and cesarean must be genuinely unreachable — not merely inconvenient or slow to organize.
2
Catheterize, and displace the urethra
Place an indwelling catheter — it both empties the bladder and lets you feel where the urethra is. An assistant's fingers in the vagina push the urethra laterally, away from the midline, and stay there throughout the cut. Urethral and bladder injury is the characteristic complication and this is what prevents it.
3
Two assistants on the legs — and a hard limit on abduction
One assistant per leg, supporting the thigh. Total abduction must not exceed about 80–90°. Once the symphysis is divided the pelvis is a hinge, and over-abduction tears the sacroiliac joints and the urethra. This limit is the difference between a recoverable injury and a permanently disabled woman, and it is the instruction most easily lost in the urgency.
4
Infiltrate local anesthetic
Over and into the joint. Seconds well spent, and she is awake.
5
Divide the fibrocartilage in the midline
A stab incision over the symphysis, then divide the joint from front to back with the blade under the guiding finger. The aim is partial division and roughly 2–2.5 cm of separation — measured, not maximal. Complete division does not help further and worsens the injury.
6
Deliver, then close and support the pelvis
Deliver immediately with the maneuvers that previously failed — they will now usually succeed. Then close the skin, keep the catheter in for several days, apply a firm pelvic binder or strapping, and arrange bed rest with gradual mobilization. She needs analgesia, follow-up, and an honest explanation of what was done and why.

Complications

Fetal / neonatal:

  • Brachial plexus injury — most commonly Erb palsy (C5–C6). The majority resolve within a year; a minority are permanent. Related to traction, but also occurs with none.
  • Clavicular and humeral fracture — heal well, and acceptable in exchange for delivery.
  • Hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy and death — the reason for the clock.

Maternal:

  • Postpartum hemorrhage from atony — common after a prolonged dystocia.
  • Third- and fourth-degree perineal tears, vaginal and cervical lacerations.
  • Symphyseal separation, with pain and difficulty walking, even without symphysiotomy.
  • Uterine rupture — from fundal pressure.
  • After symphysiotomy specifically: urethral and bladder injury, vesicovaginal fistula, stress incontinence, sacroiliac disruption, chronic pelvic pain, infection, and long-term gait disturbance.
  • Psychological trauma — a frightening, chaotic delivery with many hands and shouted instructions. Debrief her afterwards, with the sequence explained.
09

Afterwards

  • Paired cord gases, arterial and venous, from a doubly clamped segment.
  • Neonatal examination for plexus injury and fractures, and assessment for therapeutic hypothermia if the interval was long or the baby was depressed.
  • Document, in this order and with times: time of head delivery, which shoulder was anterior, each maneuver performed with its time and who performed it, the time of body delivery, who was present, cord gases, the neonatal condition and examination, and the maternal assessment. Which shoulder was anterior is the detail most often omitted and most often asked for later.
  • Examine the genital tract and quantify blood loss.
  • Explain it to the parents on the day, in plain language, and again later. They saw an emergency and understood almost none of it.
  • Debrief the team.
  • Drill it. Simulation training for shoulder dystocia has been shown to improve outcomes — one of the few obstetric interventions where that is demonstrable. The sequence must be automatic, because there is no time to reason it out.
10

Pearls

Say the words and start the clock. “Shoulder dystocia” summons a response; anything vaguer does not.

McRoberts plus suprapubic pressure solves most of them. Do both properly before moving on.

Suprapubic, never fundal. Name the landmark aloud and watch the hand.

Take your hands off the head. Increasing traction is the instinct that causes the permanent injury.

The posterior arm is the highest-yield internal maneuver. Flex at the elbow, grasp the forearm, sweep across the chest — never pull the upper arm.

All fours is quick and underused. No equipment, and every internal maneuver still works there.

A fracture is an acceptable price. Bone heals; brain does not.

Failing once through the sequence is not failure. Repeat it, ideally with fresh hands.

Expect the hemorrhage afterwards.

Record which shoulder was anterior. It is always asked for and rarely written.

Symphysiotomy only where there is no operating room. And then: catheter in, urethra held aside, and abduction capped at 90°.
