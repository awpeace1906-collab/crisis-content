Pelvic Binder Placement

Circumferential stabilization at the level of the greater trochanters — closing volume in an unstable pelvic ring fracture before hemorrhage becomes uncontrollable.

01

Indications

  • Any hemodynamically unstable blunt trauma patient with a mechanism that could break a pelvis. This is the important one — it is an empiric indication, applied before imaging and before examination confirms anything.
  • Clinical signs of pelvic ring disruption: instability felt on a single gentle examination, leg-length discrepancy, external rotation of a limb, perineal or scrotal hematoma, blood at the urethral meatus, an open perineal wound
  • Radiographic pelvic ring disruption on a trauma film or CT, if one is not already on
  • Traumatic arrest or peri-arrest from blunt mechanism — part of the same reflex as bilateral thoracostomies
  • During transfer or extrication of any patient with a suspected pelvic injury, whether or not they are currently unstable

A binder costs almost nothing to apply and almost nothing to be wrong about. The asymmetry is the whole argument: binding a pelvis that turns out to be intact is a non-event; failing to bind one that is disrupted is a preventable death.

02

Contraindications and Limits

  • None absolute in suspected pelvic disruption with shock.
  • It will not help an isolated hip dislocation, an isolated femoral neck fracture, or an acetabular fracture with an intact ring. A binder closes a ring; if the ring is not open, there is nothing to close.
  • Lateral compression (LC) patterns are already internally rotated, so a binder adds little and could theoretically over-compress. In practice this is not distinguishable at the bedside or in the field, and current guidance is to bind anyway — the theoretical harm has not materialized in the literature, and the cost of withholding in an open-book injury is very real.
  • Vertical shear patterns will not be reduced by circumferential compression alone; they need longitudinal traction and definitive fixation. Bind anyway, but know the binder is not the answer.
  • Skin already compromised at the level of the trochanters — degloving, burn, open fracture. Apply anyway if the patient is bleeding, and flag it loudly for early removal.
03

Equipment

  • A commercial binder — SAM Pelvic Sling, T-POD, Prometheus, or your service's equivalent. The advantage is not the fabric; it is that these devices self-limit tension at approximately 180 N, which is enough to close a ring and not enough to cause pressure injury quickly. A buckle that clicks and stops is doing something a sheet cannot.
  • A folded bed sheet as the improvised version — effective, universally available, and with one real drawback: you cannot control or reproduce the tension. Fold to about 20–25 cm wide, cross the ends, and secure with clamps or a tied knot.
  • Tape or a triangular bandage to bind the knees and ankles together — this is part of the procedure, not an optional extra (see §05).
  • Trauma shears. Everything comes off from under the binder first; see the warning below.
  • A marker for the application time.
Empty the pockets before you close the binder

A phone, a set of keys, a belt buckle, a holster, or a wallet trapped under a binder at the trochanter becomes a focal pressure point on a shocked, hypoperfused, immobile patient. Pressure necrosis over that object can begin within a couple of hours and will not be found until the binder comes off. Cut the clothing away and sweep the field with your hand before you tighten.

04

Why the Greater Trochanters

The most common application error, and it is not a small one.

A binder works by internally rotating both hemipelves around the femoral heads, which closes an opened symphysis, reduces pelvic volume, and lets a tamponading hematoma reach a pressure at which it stops growing. That rotation is generated by squeezing at the widest transverse point of the pelvis — the greater trochanters.

Placed at the iliac crests instead, the binder squeezes above the axis of rotation. It does not close the symphysis — and it can lever the ring further open. It also sits on bone with almost no soft-tissue cover, so it produces skin injury faster while achieving less. A binder at the belt line looks correct in a photograph and does nothing.

How to find the level without thinking: the greater trochanters sit roughly in line with the pubic symphysis, which is far lower than instinct suggests — about the level of the front trouser pocket, not the waistband. Palpate the bony prominence on the lateral hip and put the middle of the binder there.

05

Application

1
Examine the pelvis once, gently — or not at all
One careful assessment, and only if it will change what you do. Never spring the pelvis, and never re-check it. Repeated manipulation shears the clot that is the only thing containing a pelvic venous hemorrhage. If a binder is going on regardless — which for an unstable patient it is — the examination adds risk and no information. Assess once, bind, then leave it alone.
2
Strip and sweep the field
Cut clothing away from the hips. Sweep with your hand for phones, keys, buckles, and debris. Log-roll only if necessary and only once — or slide the binder under the natural hollow at the knees and walk it up beneath the buttocks, which avoids rolling entirely.
3
Position at the greater trochanters
The middle of the binder sits over the trochanters, level with the pubic symphysis. Check both sides — a binder that is correct on one hip and rides up on the other is applying an asymmetric force. If the device has a marked center, put it on the symphysis.
4
Internally rotate the legs and tie them together
Bring the legs into slight internal rotation — toes pointing at each other — and bind the knees and ankles with tape or a bandage before tightening. The femurs are levers on the femoral heads; internal rotation at the feet closes the ring from the other end. This step takes fifteen seconds, contributes real reduction, and is skipped almost every time.
5
Tighten to the device's stop — not to maximum force
Pull the straps evenly from both sides and let the buckle click. Snug and symmetric, not crushing. Over-tightening does not close the ring any further once it is reduced; it just injures skin and, in lateral compression patterns, risks over-reduction. With an improvised sheet you have no stop, so aim for firm and even, and re-check the skin more often.
6
Leave a window for the groin — deliberately
This patient will very likely need femoral arterial or venous access, and may need a REBOA sheath or angiography. Position the binder so the femoral triangles remain reachable, and say out loud that it is in place, because the next person to look will find the landmarks covered and lose time working out why.
7
Check distal perfusion, then write the time on it
Pulses, color, capillary refill in both legs. Then write the application time on the binder itself in marker. The dwell-time clock has started and nobody downstream will know when unless you record it now.
06

What the Binder Actually Stops — and What It Doesn't

Most pelvic bleeding is venous and cancellous bone — the presacral venous plexus and the raw fracture surfaces. That bleeding is low-pressure and self-tamponading if the pelvic volume stays small enough for a hematoma to build pressure. This is exactly what a binder restores, and it is why a binder works at all.

Arterial bleeding does not care about a binder. A torn internal iliac branch bleeds at systemic pressure into a space the binder has narrowed but not closed. If the patient remains unstable after the binder is on and other sources are excluded, the binder has done its job and reached its ceiling.

The escalation ladder from there:

  • Angiography and embolization — the definitive answer for arterial pelvic bleeding where it is available quickly
  • Preperitoneal pelvic packing — surgical, fast, and the better option where angiography is slow or the patient cannot survive the trip. The binder stays on for it.
  • External fixation — mechanical stability, usually alongside one of the above
  • REBOA Zone 3 — infrarenal occlusion as a bridge, sparing the kidneys and gut
  • Massive transfusion and TXA running throughout

The practical point: a binder that is on and correct, in a patient who is still unstable, is not a reason to reapply the binder. It is the signal to move to the next rung.

08

Complications

  • Pressure necrosis over the trochanters and sacrum — the dominant complication. In a shocked, hypothermic, immobile patient, skin injury can begin within 2–3 hours. Objects trapped underneath accelerate it dramatically.
  • Ineffective reduction from incorrect level — not a complication of the device so much as of its application, and the commonest reason a binder fails.
  • Over-compression of a lateral compression fracture — theoretical, rarely demonstrated, and not a reason to withhold.
  • Delayed recognition of ongoing arterial bleeding, because the binder is on and looks like an intervention that should have worked.
  • Obscured vascular access and delayed femoral cannulation.
  • Missed injuries under the binder — open perineal wounds and degloving are hidden the moment it goes on, which is an argument for a quick look before tightening.
  • Clot disruption from repeated pelvic examination — a complication of the assessment, not the binder, and entirely avoidable.
09

Dwell Time and Removal

  • It stays on until orthopedics or trauma surgery removes it — through the trauma bay, through CT, through transfer, and into the operating room. Nobody loosens it “to have a look.”
  • Target removal or conversion to definitive fixation within about 24 hours, and sooner in a patient who is hypoperfused, since that is the window in which skin injury accumulates.
  • Inspect the skin at the first safe opportunity, in a controlled setting, with orthopedics present and the ability to re-apply.
  • Handover the application time explicitly. Written on the binder, stated out loud, and in the note. A binder whose start time is unknown gets managed as if it has been on longer than it has, or worse, shorter.
  • Tell the receiving team it is on at all. Under a blanket, on a shocked patient in a busy resuscitation, a correctly applied binder is genuinely easy to miss.
10

Pearls

Greater trochanters, level with the symphysis. Lower than instinct says. This single fact accounts for most binder failures.

Bind empirically. Unstable, blunt, plausible mechanism — that is enough. Do not wait for a film.

Examine once, then never again. Springing a pelvis buys no information and shears the clot holding the patient together.

Tie the knees and ankles. Fifteen seconds, real reduction, almost always skipped.

Sweep the pockets. A phone under a binder is a pressure ulcer with a start time.

Snug, not crushing, and symmetric. The commercial devices stop at the right tension on purpose.

Leave the groin reachable. This patient is going to need femoral access, and probably angiography.

Do not take it off for the CT.

Still unstable with a correct binder means escalate, not retighten. Angiography, packing, external fixation, Zone 3.

Write the time on it.
