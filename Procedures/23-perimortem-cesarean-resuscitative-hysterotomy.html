Perimortem Cesarean Section / Resuscitative Hysterotomy

The 4-minute rule: a maternal resuscitation maneuver first, a delivery second. Speed and aortocaval decompression over sterility and anatomic precision.

01

Indications

The modern name is resuscitative hysterotomy, and the change is not cosmetic — it names what the procedure is for.

  • Maternal cardiac arrest with a uterus palpable at or above the umbilicus — roughly 20 weeks — without ROSC in the first few minutes of high-quality resuscitation
  • Peri-arrest with profound, refractory shock in late pregnancy, where aortocaval compression is plausibly the limiting factor
  • Fundal height is the trigger, not a documented gestational age. Nobody has dates in an arrest, and a stated LMP is not a reason to wait. Put a hand on the abdomen.

This is a maternal resuscitation maneuver. The gravid uterus at term can sequester a substantial fraction of cardiac output and compresses both the inferior vena cava and the aorta against the spine. Chest compressions already generate only a fraction of normal output; against aortocaval obstruction they may generate almost nothing. Emptying the uterus restores venous return, and it is frequently the intervention that makes the rest of the resuscitation work.

It is indicated even when the fetus is already dead, and even below the threshold of fetal viability, if the uterus is large enough to obstruct. Between about 20 and 24 weeks the procedure is performed for the mother alone. Framing this out loud to the team — “we are decompressing the aorta and vena cava” rather than “we are delivering a baby” — measurably reduces hesitation, and hesitation is the main failure mode.

The four-minute rule is a prompt, not a deadline

Katz's 1986 recommendation — incision at 4 minutes, delivery by 5 — was derived from case reports, and it remains the right thing to aim for. But its modern misuse is the opposite of its intent: teams look at a clock reading eleven minutes and conclude they have missed the window. They have not. Maternal ROSC and intact neonatal survival are both documented well beyond 5 minutes, including past 15. If the mother is still in arrest and the uterus is still obstructing, the procedure is still indicated. Aim for four minutes; never use having passed it as a reason not to act.

02

Contraindications

  • A uterus that does not reach the umbilicus. Below roughly 20 weeks there is no meaningful aortocaval compression and nothing to be gained.
  • ROSC already achieved. If she has a pulse, this becomes an obstetric decision made with obstetrics, not a resuscitation maneuver.
  • Obvious, unequivocal signs of irreversible death predating the arrest.

Things that are not contraindications, and which are used as reasons to delay:

  • Absent fetal heart tones. Do not look for them. The procedure is for the mother, the ultrasound takes time you do not have, and a normal fetal heart does not change the decision either.
  • No obstetrician present. Whoever is there does it. Waiting for the right specialist is the commonest way the window is lost.
  • Not being in an operating room. It happens where the arrest happened. Moving the patient means stopping compressions, and transport time is the one thing that cannot be recovered.
  • No consent. Emergency doctrine. This is standard of care.
  • No sterile field, no anesthesia, no formal instrument tray. A patient in cardiac arrest does not need anesthesia, and infection is a problem for survivors.
  • Uncertain dates. See above — palpate.
03

Equipment

  • A scalpel. A No. 10 blade. That is the whole essential list, and saying so out loud helps people start.
  • Two cord clamps and scissors — or any two clamps and anything that cuts.
  • Bandage or Mayo scissors for extending the uterine incision, if reaching for them costs nothing.
  • Abdominal packs and towels for after ROSC.
  • Suction, if it is already in the room.
  • Neonatal resuscitation equipment and a separate team — warmer, bag-mask with a neonatal mask, towels, cord clamp. This is a second resuscitation and it needs its own people. Where there is any warning at all, stage them before incision.
  • Oxytocin, drawn and ready for the moment ROSC is achieved — see §07.

A pre-assembled pack helps, but only if it is where the patient is. Many units keep a resuscitative hysterotomy tray on the maternal arrest trolley. If yours does, know where it lives. If it does not, the scalpel on the intubation trolley is sufficient, and the procedure has been done with less.

04

Resuscitating a Pregnant Patient

Several things people “know” about this are out of date.

  • Manual left uterine displacement, not lateral tilt. Stand at the patient's left and pull the uterus up and to the left with both hands, or push it leftward from her right side. Keep her supine. Tilting the whole body degrades compression quality substantially, and the evidence now favors displacement on a flat back. Continue displacement until the uterus is opened.
  • Standard hand position for compressions. The old teaching to move the hands higher on the sternum in pregnancy has not held up. Compress where you always compress.
  • Defibrillate at standard energies. Pads in the usual positions; move a lateral pad slightly if breast tissue is in the way. Fetal monitors come off before the shock.
  • Standard drugs at standard doses. No modification for pregnancy, and none withheld out of fetal concern — the fetus does not survive a mother who does not.
  • Airway early, and expect it to be hard. Mucosal edema, a fuller airway, larger breasts against the laryngoscope, and a reduced functional residual capacity with raised oxygen consumption mean desaturation is fast and the view is worse than you expect. Have a smaller tube ready — 6.0 to 7.0 — and go to a supraglottic device early rather than repeating attempts. Aspiration risk is high.
  • Access above the diaphragm. A femoral line delivers drugs through a vena cava the uterus is compressing. Use an upper-limb IV, a humeral IO, or an upper-body central line.
  • Think about the obstetric causes while you work. Hemorrhage (including concealed abruption and rupture), amniotic fluid embolism, pulmonary embolism, eclampsia and intracranial hemorrhage, peripartum cardiomyopathy, sepsis, anesthetic complications including high spinal and local anesthetic toxicity, and magnesium toxicity — for which the treatment is calcium, and which is easy to miss in a patient receiving an infusion nobody mentioned.
05

Technique

1
Announce the arrest time, and have someone call the clock
“Arrest at 14:06. I want the time called every minute. If there is no ROSC at 14:10 we are doing a hysterotomy.” Naming the decision in advance is what converts a four-minute target into something that actually happens, because in the moment nobody wants to be the person who says it.
2
Compressions and displacement continue throughout
Nothing stops for this. Not for the incision, not for the delivery. Someone maintains left uterine displacement until the uterus is open.
3
Vertical midline, umbilicus to symphysis, through everything
A single firm stroke from just above the umbilicus down to the pubic symphysis, through skin, fat, and fascia. There is very little bleeding in arrest — no perfusion pressure — and this surprises people the first time. Do not creep through the layers. Vertical is chosen because it is fast and forgiving of anatomy you cannot see.
4
Open the peritoneum, sweep the bladder down
Enter bluntly with fingers where you can, to avoid bowel and bladder. The bladder sits over the lower uterine segment and is often distended — push it inferiorly out of the way rather than cutting through it.
5
Vertical uterine incision — start small, extend with fingers
Make a short vertical incision in the lower uterine segment and continue until you are through the myometrium and into the cavity. Then put the scalpel down. Extend the opening upward with your fingers, or with bandage scissors advanced between two fingers held inside the uterus as a shield. Scalpel extension is how fetuses get lacerated, and it is entirely avoidable.
6
Deliver, clamp, cut, hand off
Deliver the fetus by whatever presentation you find — a hand under the head, or by the feet if breech. Double-clamp and cut the cord and hand the neonate to a different person with a different job. The mother remains your patient. If no neonatal team exists yet, that is a hard reality to state out loud and then work within.
7
Remove the placenta, then keep resuscitating
Deliver the placenta manually if it comes easily; do not spend time on it if it does not. The decompression has already happened. Reassess rhythm and continue the algorithm — venous return may now be dramatically better, and this is the point at which arrests sometimes convert.
8
Record every time
Arrest, decision, incision, delivery. Someone writes these down as they happen. They will be needed for the neonatal team, for the debrief, and for the review that follows.

07

If She Gets ROSC

The moment perfusion returns, an open atonic uterus becomes a hemorrhage of a scale few clinicians have seen. Be ready for it before it happens.

  • Oxytocin immediately — a dilute infusion, not an undiluted rapid push, which causes hypotension and arrhythmia in an already unstable patient. Add further uterotonics per the postpartum hemorrhage pathway, observing their contraindications.
  • Bimanual compression and uterine massage, and pack the uterus and abdomen with laparotomy sponges. Count them.
  • Activate massive transfusion and give TXA if within the window.
  • Move to an operating room for formal closure. The abdomen can travel packed and open; it must not stay that way.
  • Broad-spectrum antibiotics — the field was not sterile and now she is a survivor for whom infection matters.
  • Targeted temperature management and post-arrest care as for any other arrest. Pregnancy and recent delivery are not contraindications.
  • Treat the cause. The hysterotomy relieved an obstruction; it did not treat an embolism, an eclamptic bleed, or a cardiomyopathy.
08

Afterwards — the Team

  • Debrief, and mean it. This is among the most distressing events in clinical medicine, and it is frequently performed by people who have never done it and will never do it again. Debrief on the day and follow up afterwards, with a specific offer rather than a general one.
  • Expect the review, and welcome it. Maternal deaths are formally reviewed everywhere. Contemporaneous times and a clear account of the decision are what make that review useful rather than adversarial.
  • Rehearse it. Every unit that cares for pregnant patients — including emergency departments and non-obstetric hospitals — should have run this in simulation. The failure is almost never technical; it is the four minutes spent deciding. The single strongest predictor of doing it in time is having said out loud, beforehand, that you would.
09

Pearls

Call it a resuscitative hysterotomy. The name is the intervention: it tells the room this is for the mother, and that is what gets it started.

Fundus at or above the umbilicus is the trigger. Not dates, not an ultrasound, not an obstetrician's opinion.

Past four minutes is not too late. The rule is a prompt. Survivors exist well beyond it.

Do not go looking for fetal heart tones. They do not change the decision and they cost the window.

Do it where she is. Moving means stopping compressions.

Manual left uterine displacement, supine. Not tilt — tilt ruins the compressions.

Access above the diaphragm. Drugs given below a compressed vena cava may never arrive.

Put the scalpel down once you are in the uterus. Fingers from there.

Almost no bleeding in arrest — and torrential bleeding at ROSC. Have oxytocin drawn before you need it.

One scalpel is enough. Saying so is part of the technique.
