Resuscitative Thoracotomy (Clamshell)

The last-resort maneuver for the peri-arrest or arrested trauma patient — direct access to the heart, the hilum, and the descending aorta.

THORACIC
⚡ TECHNIQUE Indications Contraindications Equipment Complications & Pitfalls Pearls References
01

Indications

  • Penetrating trauma with witnessed loss of vital signs and <15 minutes of prehospital CPR (best outcomes)
  • Penetrating trauma with signs of life on arrival that are lost during resuscitation
  • Blunt trauma with witnessed arrest and <10 minutes of CPR (much lower survival, but still a candidate population at select centers)
  • Suspected pericardial tamponade, massive hemothorax, or air embolism amenable to thoracotomy-based intervention in the peri-arrest patient
02

Contraindications

  • Blunt trauma arrest with prolonged downtime and no signs of life — survival is exceedingly rare and most protocols exclude this group
  • Penetrating trauma with prolonged (>15 min) CPR and no signs of life
  • Injuries clearly incompatible with survival regardless of intervention
03

Equipment

  • Scalpel (#10 blade), Mayo scissors, rib spreaders (Finochietto retractor)
  • Internal defibrillator paddles
  • Vascular clamps (for aortic cross-clamping)
  • Suction, large-bore IV/central access already in progress
  • Available OR/surgical backup for definitive repair
04

Stepwise Technique

1
Confirm indication criteria rapidly — mechanism, downtime, and signs of life determine candidacy; this decision must be made in seconds, not minutes.
2
Make a left anterolateral thoracotomy incision at the 4th or 5th intercostal space, from the sternal border to the posterior axillary line.
3
Extend to a clamshell (bilateral anterolateral thoracotomy with transverse sternotomy) if right-sided or bilateral pathology is suspected, or if left-sided exposure alone is inadequate.
4
Insert a rib spreader and open the chest widely.
5
Open the pericardium longitudinally, anterior to the phrenic nerve, to relieve tamponade if present and expose the heart.
6
Control cardiac hemorrhage with digital pressure, staples, or sutures as indicated; identify and control any great vessel injury.
7
Perform internal cardiac massage if in arrest.
8
Cross-clamp the descending thoracic aorta if hemorrhage control below the diaphragm and improved cerebral/coronary perfusion is the goal.
9
Defibrillate internally if indicated using internal paddles.
10
Transition immediately to definitive OR management once any restoration of perfusion is achieved — this procedure is a bridge to definitive repair, not the endpoint.
Indication criteria determine survival more than technique does

Applying this outside validated criteria (mechanism, downtime, signs of life) consumes resources without a realistic survival benefit — know your institution's specific criteria before the patient arrives, not while cutting.

05

Complications & Pitfalls

  • Extremely high mortality even with correct indication and technique — set expectations with the team accordingly
  • Iatrogenic injury to the heart, great vessels, lung, or phrenic nerve during rapid exposure
  • Failure to control the actual source of hemorrhage/tamponade despite adequate exposure
  • Provider injury (sharp instruments, exposed bone edges) in a high-adrenaline, high-speed procedure — maintain situational awareness for the team's safety too
06

Pearls

Indication criteria matter enormously for outcome — this is not a universal salvage maneuver, and applying it outside validated criteria consumes resources without a realistic survival benefit.

Speed of exposure is everything; the incision-to-pericardium interval is the single biggest modifiable time factor.

Know your institution's criteria and have the conversation about candidacy before the patient arrives, not while cutting.
