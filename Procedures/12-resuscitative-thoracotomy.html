Resuscitative Thoracotomy (Clamshell)

The last-resort maneuver for the peri-arrest or arrested trauma patient — direct access to the heart, the hilum, and the descending aorta.

01

Indications

Candidacy is decided in seconds, and it decides the outcome more than technique does
PresentationStance
Penetrating thoracic trauma, arrest witnessed, signs of life within ~15 minStrongest indication. Best reported survival, particularly for a stab wound to the cardiac box with tamponade
Penetrating thoracic trauma, no signs of life, <15 min CPRReasonable — still a candidate group
Penetrating extra-thoracic trauma, signs of lifeWeaker, but defensible for aortic cross-clamping in exsanguination
Blunt trauma, signs of life presentLow survival, but performed at many centres. Know your institution's stance
Blunt trauma, no signs of lifeSurvival approaches zero. Most guidance advises against
Prolonged CPR (>15 min penetrating, >10 min blunt) with no signs of lifeAgainst
“Signs of life” has a specific meaning — pupillary response, spontaneous ventilation, a palpable pulse, measurable blood pressure, spontaneous movement, or organized electrical activity. It is not the same as “arrived with CPR in progress.” Establish what the prehospital team actually witnessed, and when, in the first fifteen seconds of handover.
The physiological targets, not just the diagnosis

This procedure exists to do four things: release tamponade, control intrathoracic hemorrhage, cross-clamp the aorta to redistribute what blood remains to heart and brain, and deliver internal cardiac massage or defibrillation. If none of those four is plausibly the problem, opening the chest will not help.

02

Contraindications

  • Blunt arrest with prolonged downtime and no signs of life
  • Penetrating trauma with >15 minutes of CPR and no signs of life
  • Injuries obviously incompatible with survival
  • No surgical capability to follow. This is a bridge. Opening a chest in a facility that cannot then repair what you find, and cannot transfer, commits the patient and exposes the team for nothing — a decision to make before the patient arrives, not over the body.
03

Equipment & Team

  • #10 scalpel, heavy Mayo scissors, and toothed forceps
  • Finochietto rib spreader
  • Heavy scissors, Gigli saw or a Lebsche knife for the sternum if converting to clamshell
  • Vascular clamps — a DeBakey or aortic clamp for the descending aorta, and a second for the hilum
  • Internal defibrillator paddles, and a defibrillator that will accept them
  • Foley catheter — an improvised but genuinely effective tamponade for a ventricular hole
  • Skin stapler, 2-0/3-0 pledgeted sutures, suction, packs
  • Full barrier PPE for everyone at the table — face shield, gown, double gloves. This is among the highest body-fluid-exposure procedures in medicine, performed fast, around cut bone and sharps.
  • Massive transfusion running before the chest is open, and a named person running it
04

Stepwise Technique

Incision to pericardium is the interval that matters — aim for well under a minute
1
Declare it, assign roles, arms up

Say out loud that you are performing a resuscitative thoracotomy.

Assign:

  • airway,
  • the transfusion, and
  • one person on instruments.

Abduct the left arm above the head.

Continue chest compressions and ventilation until the moment you cut.

2
Left anterolateral incision, 4th–5th interspace, one confident pass

From the sternal edge to the table — go further posteriorly than instinct suggests, because inadequate length is the commonest reason exposure fails.

Follow the curve of the rib, in the inframammary crease, retracting breast tissue superiorly rather than cutting through it.

Cut deeply through skin, fat and muscle in a single deliberate stroke; timid repeated passes waste the seconds this procedure is spending.

3
Open the intercostal muscle and pleura with scissors

Puncture the intercostal muscle just above the rib, then run heavy scissors along the superior border of the rib for the length of the incision, pushing the lung away.

Ask for ventilation to be held briefly if the lung is in the way.

4
Insert the spreader with the handle toward the axilla

A small practical point that matters enormously: orient the Finochietto's ratchet and handle inferiorly/laterally, toward the axilla.

Placed the other way, the handle sits over the sternum and physically blocks the clamshell extension you may be about to need.

Open it wide.

5
Extend to clamshell if you need the other side

If the injury is right-sided, bilateral, or exposure is inadequate, mirror the incision on the right and divide the sternum transversely with heavy scissors or a Gigli saw.

The internal mammary arteries will be cut and they must be found and ligated.

They often do not bleed while the patient is exsanguinated and pressureless — and then bleed briskly the moment you restore a blood pressure. Ligate them before that happens.

6
Open the pericardium longitudinally, anterior to the phrenic nerve

Identify the phrenic nerve running vertically on the lateral pericardium — stay anterior to it.

Tent the pericardium with forceps and incise longitudinally with scissors, then extend the opening widely. Under tamponade the pericardium is tense and bluish.

Evacuate clot with your hand; clot is exactly what the needle could not remove.

7
Control cardiac bleeding — finger first

Deliver the heart into the wound.

Put a finger over the hole; that alone controls most wounds and buys thinking time.

Then:

  • a Foley through the defect with the balloon inflated and gentle traction for a larger ventricular wound,
  • skin staples for a rapid closure, or
  • pledgeted horizontal mattress sutures placed underneath the coronary artery so you do not ligate it.

Atrial wounds can often be clamped with a side-biting vascular clamp and repaired.

8
Internal massage, correctly

Two hands, flat, hinged at the wrists — one posterior and one anterior, compressing the heart between the flat palmar surfaces at around 100/min.

Do not squeeze with fingertips: point pressure perforates a flaccid ventricle.

Compress from apex toward base.

9
Cross-clamp the descending aorta if you need it

Sweep the left lung anteriorly and medially.

The aorta lies posteriorly, against the vertebral bodies — and the structure immediately anterior to it is the esophagus, which is easy to clamp by mistake. A nasogastric tube makes the esophagus palpable and is worth the seconds.

Open the mediastinal pleura, bluntly dissect the aorta circumferentially, and apply the clamp.

Note the time: aim to release within roughly 30 minutes, and anticipate a reperfusion insult when you do.

10
Internal defibrillation — start low

Internal paddles applied directly to the heart, one anterior and one posterior.

Start at 10–20 J, not external energies.

External doses applied directly to myocardium cause injury and are unnecessary — you have removed the chest wall from the circuit.

11
If you get a perfusing rhythm, go to theatre now

The chest stays open, packed, with the clamp noted. Everything from here is damage control in an operating room.

If there is no response after adequate exposure, tamponade release and massage, stop and say so out loud — a clear termination is part of the procedure, and the team needs to hear it.

Protect the team while you work

Sharp bone edges, a scalpel and scissors moving fast, and blood everywhere.

  • Announce every sharp,
  • hand instruments rather than reaching, and
  • keep the number of hands in the chest to the minimum that works.

A needlestick or a splash here is a genuine occupational exposure, and the adrenaline that makes this procedure possible is exactly what makes people careless.

Complications & Pitfalls

  • Very high mortality even when everything is done correctly — the team should hear this framed before it begins
  • Phrenic nerve injury from opening the pericardium in the wrong plane
  • Coronary artery ligation during a hurried cardiac repair
  • Esophageal injury from a misplaced aortic clamp
  • Myocardial perforation from fingertip massage
  • Reperfusion injury after cross-clamp release
  • Occupational exposure — a real and under-discussed outcome for the team
  • Performing it outside criteria, consuming blood, people and exposure risk without realistic prospect of survival
07

Pearls

Candidacy decides outcome more than technique does. Have the criteria conversation before the patient arrives.

Cut long, cut deep, cut once. Inadequate exposure is the commonest technical failure, and timid incisions cost more time than they save.

Spreader handle toward the axilla — so it never blocks the clamshell.

Anterior to the phrenic nerve, longitudinally.

Ligate the internal mammaries before the blood pressure comes back and reminds you.

Flat hands, not fingertips.

The aorta is posterior; the esophagus is in front of it. Feel for the NG tube.

Internal defibrillation is 10–20 J.

Say the ending out loud. Terminating clearly is part of running the procedure well.
