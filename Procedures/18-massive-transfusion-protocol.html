01

When to Activate

The protocol exists to be pulled early. Over-activation costs product; under-activation costs the patient.

Activate on any of these — do not wait for a lab value:

  • Critical Administration Threshold (CAT+): ≥3 units of red cells in any 60-minute period. This is the most useful real-time trigger — it is the one you can recognize while it is happening.
  • ABC score ≥2 (one point each): penetrating mechanism · SBP ≤90 · HR ≥120 · positive FAST.
  • Shock index (HR ÷ SBP) >1.0, and especially >1.4.
  • Anticipated ≥10 units in 24 h or ≥4 units in 1 h — the classic definitions, useful for audit, too slow to be a bedside trigger.
  • Clinical gestalt. A ruptured AAA, a mangled pelvis, a boggy uterus that will not contract, or bright blood filling a chest tube is enough. Pulling the trigger on a patient who turns out not to need it is a recoverable error.

Non-trauma activations matter just as much: obstetric hemorrhage, upper GI variceal bleeding, ruptured aneurysm, and post-operative surgical bleeding all belong here.

02

Contraindications and Limits

  • None absolute in the presence of major hemorrhage.
  • Documented refusal of blood products. Follow the patient's stated wishes and your institution's policy. Have the alternative plan ready before you need it — cell salvage, TXA, fibrinogen concentrate, factor concentrates, and aggressive surgical control. Know which of these an individual patient does and does not accept; the categories are not uniform.
  • Uncontrolled surgical bleeding is not a transfusion problem. Product buys time to get to the OR, angiography suite, or the balloon. If activation is being used as a substitute for source control, the protocol will fail no matter how well it is run.
  • Futility. Massive transfusion in a patient with unsurvivable injury consumes a finite regional blood supply. This is a judgment call made early and explicitly, not one drifted into.
03

What You Need Running

  • The activation mechanism itself — one phone number or one order. Know it before the day you need it. The commonest delay in a massive transfusion is not the blood bank; it is the four minutes spent working out who to call.
  • Rapid infuser with integrated warmer (Belmont, Level 1, or equivalent) — capable of 500–1000 mL/min at 38 °C. A blood warmer on a gravity line is not a rapid infuser.
  • Two large-bore access points. Flow scales with the fourth power of radius and inversely with length: a short 14–16 G peripheral outperforms a long triple-lumen central line for volume. If central access is needed, use an introducer sheath (8.5 Fr), not a multi-lumen.
  • Forced-air warmer, warmed room, warmed fluids — hypothermia is generated by the resuscitation itself.
  • Point-of-care testing: blood gas with ionized calcium, lactate, hemoglobin; and viscoelastic testing (TEG/ROTEM) if available.
  • A whiteboard and a scribe. Product counts, time of activation, and calcium doses are all lost in real time without someone writing them down.

The Packs and the Ratio

Ratios are prescribed so nobody has to calculate during the event.

ComponentAdult pack (typical)Notes
Red cells6 unitsUncrossmatched O to start. O-negative for females of childbearing potential; O-positive is acceptable for males and post-menopausal females and protects a scarce O-neg supply.
Plasma (FFP / thawed)6 unitsThawing takes 20–30 min unless pre-thawed plasma is stocked — this is the usual reason pack 1 arrives red-cell-heavy.
Platelets1 apheresis unitOne apheresis unit ≈ 6 pooled units — this is what makes a 6:6:1 pack a “1:1:1” ratio. Miscounting this is how platelets get under-given.
CryoprecipitateOften not in pack 1Must usually be asked for. See §06 — fibrinogen falls first and is the thing most often missed.
Whole blood (LTOWB)Increasingly pack 1Low-titer group O whole blood delivers the balanced ratio in a single bag with one venipuncture-equivalent of dilution. Where stocked, it is the simplest way to get the ratio right.

What PROPPR actually showed. 1:1:1 versus 1:1:2 produced no difference in 24-hour or 30-day all-cause mortality. What it did show: more patients achieved anatomic hemostasis, and fewer died of exsanguination in the first 24 hours, with 1:1:1. That is the basis for the default — a reasonable empiric starting ratio, not a proven mortality intervention. It is a bridge to goal-directed therapy, and should be abandoned for viscoelastic guidance as soon as you have data.

Pediatric dosing
  • Red cells: 10–20 mL/kg per round
  • Plasma: 10–20 mL/kg
  • Platelets: 10–15 mL/kg
  • Cryoprecipitate: 5–10 mL/kg
  • Pediatric massive transfusion is commonly defined as >40 mL/kg of any product in 24 h, or >half the estimated blood volume in 3 h.
  • Children tolerate the citrate and potassium load of rapid transfusion worse than adults, not better. Calcium and potassium need checking sooner.

Drugs That Go With the Blood

These are the doses. They are forgotten more often than the blood is.

AgentAdult doseWhen / why
Tranexamic acid — trauma1 g IV over 10 min, then 1 g over 8 hWithin 3 hours of injury. Beyond 3 hours CRASH-2 showed a signal of harm — check the clock, not just the bleeding. Rapid push causes hypotension.

Fibrinogen (cryoprecipitate)10 pooled units (raises fibrinogen ~50–100 mg/dL)When fibrinogen <150 mg/dL, or <200 in obstetric hemorrhage. See §06.

Hypocalcemia is the fourth point of the diamond

Every unit of product carries citrate, which chelates ionized calcium. Ionized calcium <0.9 mmol/L on arrival independently predicts mortality and the need for massive transfusion. Calcium is required for platelet function and for nearly every step of the coagulation cascade — you cannot clot without it, and no amount of plasma substitutes for it. It is also negatively inotropic when low. Give calcium with the first pack and recheck the ionized level every 30 minutes; target >1.1–1.2 mmol/L.

Blood pressure (pre-control)SBP 80–90 / MAP 50–65Except TBI or spinal cord injury: SBP ≥110, MAP ≥80

Reading viscoelastic tests quickly. The point of TEG/ROTEM here is not elegance — it is that each abnormality maps to one bag.

  • Prolonged R time (TEG) / CT (EXTEM) → not enough clotting factors → plasma (or PCC in the anticoagulated).
  • Narrow alpha angle, prolonged K / low FIBTEM A10 → not enough fibrinogen → cryoprecipitate or fibrinogen concentrate.
  • Low MA (TEG) / low EXTEM A10 with normal FIBTEM → not enough platelets → platelets.
  • LY30 >3% / increased maximum lysis → hyperfibrinolysis → TXA.

Permissive hypotension until hemorrhage is controlled: a clot formed at SBP 85 survives; the same clot is blown off at SBP 140. The exceptions are absolute — a brain or cord that is also injured needs perfusion pressure more than it needs a protected clot.

Crystalloid is not resuscitation. Keep it under 1 L before blood arrives, and ideally near zero. It dilutes clotting factors, worsens acidosis, drops the temperature, and raises pressure enough to displace clot — four harms, no benefit.

07

Running It — Roles and Sequence

1
Activate out loud, and name the person
“I am activating massive transfusion. [Name], you are calling the blood bank.” Undirected instructions in a full room get executed by nobody. State the location, the patient identifier, and that this is an MTP activation.
2
Assign the four jobs
Blood bank liaison (the only person who talks to blood bank, and who keeps the runner moving) · Rapid infuser operator (does nothing else) · Scribe (units in, times, calcium doses, labs sent) · Team leader (source control and the decision to keep going). One person cannot hold two of these.
3
Start uncrossmatched now, send the type and screen simultaneously
Do not hold the first unit waiting for a crossmatch. Send the sample at the same moment the emergency-release blood is hung, so the switch to type-specific product is ready when the initial supply runs down.
4
Give TXA and calcium with pack 1
Both are time-sensitive and both are the things that get remembered at minute 40. Check the injury clock for the 3-hour TXA window before giving it.
5
Warm everything
Infuser warmer on and verified at temperature, forced-air blanket over whatever is not exposed, room heat up, wet clothing cut off and removed. Hypothermia here is iatrogenic and it is the leg of the lethal triad you have the most direct control over.
6
Get labs at activation and every 30–60 minutes
Blood gas with ionized calcium, CBC, fibrinogen, PT/INR, and viscoelastic if available. Fibrinogen specifically — it is not on a standard coagulation panel at many sites and it is the first thing to fail.
7
Drive toward source control the whole time
OR, interventional radiology, endoscopy, balloon tamponade, a binder, a tourniquet, uterotonics. Every round of product is a question about why the bleeding has not stopped. If the answer is “we are still waiting for the OR,” escalate that instead of hanging another unit.
8
Deactivate explicitly, and return the cooler
Call blood bank and say the protocol is off. Unopened coolers go back promptly — units held at the bedside past their window are destroyed, and a regional supply is finite. This step is skipped constantly.

Complications

  • Hypothermia — worsens platelet function and enzyme kinetics. Largely iatrogenic and largely preventable.
  • Ionized hypocalcemia — citrate-driven; causes coagulopathy, hypotension, and arrest. The single most under-treated complication of massive transfusion.
  • Hyperkalemia — potassium leaks from stored red cells; risk scales with unit age, infusion rate, and inversely with patient size.
  • Dilutional coagulopathy — from crystalloid and from red-cell-heavy ratios.
  • Metabolic alkalosis — a late effect as citrate is metabolized to bicarbonate.
  • TRALI (within 6 h, non-cardiogenic infiltrates) and TACO (volume overload) — both present as post-transfusion hypoxia and are distinguished mainly by volume status.
  • Acute hemolytic reaction — rare with emergency-release O product; the real risk is a mislabeled sample. Two-person identity verification does not stop during a resuscitation.
  • Transfusion-associated immunomodulation and infection risk — dose-dependent, and a reason to stop when bleeding stops.
  • Abdominal compartment syndrome after large-volume resuscitation — measure bladder pressure if the abdomen becomes tense and oliguria or high airway pressures develop.
10

Pearls

Three units in an hour is your trigger. Of all the scores, CAT+ is the one you can actually notice in real time.

Calcium with the first pack, every time. It is the most commonly omitted and most rapidly lethal deficiency in the whole protocol, and it is one syringe.

Check the clock before giving TXA. Within 3 hours it helps; beyond it, the trial data point the other way. Bleeding alone is not the indication — timing is.

One apheresis platelet equals six units. Miscount this and your “1:1:1” is a 6:6:6 — wildly over-plateleted — or you give six pooled bags thinking you are behind.

Fibrinogen fails first and is measured last. Order it explicitly; it is absent from many default coagulation panels.

Short and fat beats long and central. A 14 G peripheral moves more blood than a triple-lumen. If you want central, place an introducer sheath.

Keep the pressure low until the bleeding stops — unless the brain or cord is injured, where perfusion wins.

Every unit is a question about source control. The protocol is a bridge to an operation, an embolization, or a balloon. It is not a destination.

Deactivate and send the cooler back. Unglamorous, constantly forgotten, and the difference between a functioning regional blood supply and a depleted one.
