Massive Transfusion Protocol Activation & Sequencing

Getting blood products moving before the lab catches up — ratio-based transfusion as a bridge to definitive hemorrhage control.

HEMORRHAGE CONTROL
⚡ TECHNIQUE Indications Contraindications Equipment Complications & Pitfalls Pearls References
01

Indications

  • Anticipated or ongoing massive hemorrhage (commonly defined as anticipated need for ≥10 units PRBCs in 24 hours, or ≥4 units in 1 hour, or hemorrhage with hemodynamic instability unresponsive to initial resuscitation)
  • Clinical gestalt of significant ongoing hemorrhage should trigger activation without waiting to meet a strict numeric threshold — the protocol exists to be activated early
02

Contraindications

  • None absolute when major hemorrhage is present or strongly anticipated
  • Known and documented patient refusal of blood products (relative — follow your institution's policy and the patient's expressed wishes/advance directives)
03

Equipment

  • Institutional MTP activation mechanism (phone activation, EHR order set, or dedicated MTP phone line to blood bank)
  • Rapid infuser/blood warmer
  • Large-bore IV or central access sufficient for high-flow product administration
  • Point-of-care testing (ABG, TEG/ROTEM if available) to guide ongoing product ratios beyond the initial fixed-ratio phase
04

Stepwise Technique

1
Activate the protocol early and verbally, using your institution's specific activation phrase/mechanism — do not wait for confirmatory lab values in an obviously hemorrhaging patient.
2
Initiate transfusion in a fixed ratio, typically 1:1:1 (PRBC:FFP:platelets), as the default starting sequence while definitive hemorrhage control is pursued.
3
Ensure blood is warmed via a rapid infuser/blood warmer system before or during administration to avoid iatrogenic hypothermia.
4
Administer calcium supplementation proactively — citrate in transfused blood products chelates calcium and can cause clinically significant hypocalcemia during massive transfusion.
5
Send serial labs (ABG with ionized calcium, fibrinogen, coagulation studies) to guide product ratio adjustments as the resuscitation progresses beyond the initial empiric phase.
6
Communicate continuously with blood bank regarding ongoing need, product availability, and any anticipated de-escalation.
7
Formally deactivate the protocol once hemorrhage is controlled and the patient is stabilizing, to avoid unnecessary product waste and cost.
Activate on clinical suspicion, not lab confirmation

The entire value of the protocol is removing decision friction at exactly the moment friction is most costly — waiting for confirmatory labs in an obviously hemorrhaging patient defeats the purpose of having a protocol at all.

05

Complications & Pitfalls

  • Hypothermia from unwarmed product administration, which independently worsens coagulopathy — part of the lethal triad alongside acidosis and coagulopathy
  • Hypocalcemia from citrate load if calcium is not proactively repleted
  • Transfusion-related acute lung injury (TRALI) or transfusion-associated circulatory overload (TACO), particularly with large product volumes
  • Delayed activation — waiting for lab confirmation before activating costs time the patient may not have
  • Under-transfusion of platelets/plasma relative to PRBCs if ratio discipline is not maintained under the chaos of an active resuscitation
06

Pearls

Activate on clinical suspicion, not lab confirmation — the entire value of a protocol is that it removes decision friction in exactly the moment friction is most costly.

Warm the blood and replete calcium proactively rather than reactively — both are easy to forget in the chaos of an active resuscitation and both meaningfully affect coagulation.

The fixed 1:1:1 ratio is a starting point, not a permanent prescription — use point-of-care coagulation data to individualize as soon as it's available.

Deactivate promptly once hemorrhage is controlled; this closes the loop with blood bank and avoids unnecessary product utilization.
