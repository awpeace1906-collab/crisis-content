01

Indications

  • Cannot Intubate, Cannot Oxygenate — confirmed after optimized, and failed, attempts at all three of: face mask ventilation, supraglottic airway, and tracheal intubation
  • Progressive desaturation despite those optimized attempts
  • Any situation where the algorithm is exhausted and oxygenation cannot be achieved by any other route
This entry is the decision layer; the Cricothyrotomy entry is the technique layer. Knowing how to cut is necessary and not sufficient. Almost every published CICO disaster is a story about a team that could have performed the procedure competently and didn't start it soon enough.
02

Contraindications

None. If the diagnosis of CICO is correct, a front-of-neck airway is indicated. The only meaningful error in the other direction is misdiagnosing CICO when a further optimized attempt at mask, supraglottic, or tube would still have worked — which is why the section below is about what "optimized" actually means.

03

Recognizing CICO — The Part That Actually Fails

You are diagnosing a failure of oxygenation, not collecting failed attempts

“Optimized” has a specific meaning. Before you can call an attempt failed, it has to have been a real attempt:

  • Face mask: two-person technique, oral and nasal adjuncts in, jaw thrust, head position corrected, seal checked.
  • Supraglottic airway: correct size, a second attempt or a different device — not one hurried insertion that didn't seat.
  • Intubation: best operator available, best device available, external laryngeal manipulation, patient repositioned. A maximum of three attempts plus one by a more experienced operator is the commonly taught ceiling; the exact number matters less than the principle that repeating an identical failing attempt is not a new attempt.

Once all three limbs have genuinely failed and the saturation is falling, the diagnosis is made. There is no fourth category to try.

The clock you are actually racing

By the time saturations fall below roughly 90% they drop precipitously, not linearly — the oxyhaemoglobin dissociation curve's steep segment means you go from 90% to profound hypoxia in well under a minute in a patient with no reserve. Any plan that assumes you will have several minutes of gentle decline to make up your mind is wrong. Obese, pregnant, pediatric, and critically ill patients have essentially no reserve at all.

The human-factors failure modes, by name. These are what the audit data actually describes:

  • Task fixation — the operator who keeps re-attempting laryngoscopy because this time it will work. The strongest predictor of it is having just felt close to success.
  • Loss of situational awareness — nobody is watching the saturation trend because everybody is watching the mouth.
  • Diffusion of responsibility — several senior people present, none of whom believes it is their call to escalate.
  • Hierarchy — the person who first recognized CICO is the most junior in the room and raises it as a question rather than a statement.
  • Sunk cost — "we've come this far with this plan."
04

Declaring It — The Steps That Matter

1
Say the words out loud
“This is CICO. We are performing a front-of-neck airway.” Said as a declaration, not a question. Verbalizing the diagnosis is the single act that shifts the whole team out of intubation mode — without it, everyone continues optimizing the thing that has already failed. This is the step that is most often skipped and it costs more lives than any technical error.
2
Name people, not roles
“Sarah, get the CICO cart. James, you're on the neck with me. Priya, keep the saturation and the time out loud.” Unaddressed requests in a crowded room are absorbed by everybody and actioned by nobody. Assign a timekeeper explicitly — time perception collapses in these events.
3
Keep oxygen going through the front door
Do not abandon upper-airway oxygenation because you have declared CICO. Leave the supraglottic device or mask in place with high-flow oxygen and continue attempts at passive or active oxygenation while the neck is being prepared. Any residual patency buys seconds, and — importantly — it is also the exhalation route if you end up using a needle technique.
4
Default to scalpel–bougie–tube in adults
Decide this before the emergency, as an institutional default, so it is not debated at the bedside. Adults: scalpel technique. Children below roughly 8–12 years, or an adult neck where open technique is genuinely impossible: needle. Do not pick up a Seldinger kit because it feels familiar — see the Cricothyrotomy entry for why speed decides this.
5
Stop going back
Once the decision is made, further attempts at oral intubation or mask ventilation by the same operator are a distraction that consumes the only resource that matters. A second person may continue upper-airway oxygenation; the person doing the neck does the neck.
6
Confirm oxygenation, then stop and reassess everything
A rising saturation through the front-of-neck airway ends the emergency, not the episode. Secure the tube, get imaging, manage bleeding, sedate, and plan the definitive airway.
Rehearse the sentence, not just the procedure

Teams that have said “this is CICO, we are moving to a surgical airway” out loud in simulation say it faster in reality. It sounds trivial. It is the single highest-yield thing you can rehearse, because the barrier in the real event is almost never manual dexterity — it is the social and cognitive cost of being the person who declares that the plan has failed.

05

What Should Already Be True Before the Emergency

  • A CICO cart with a known location that every anesthetist, intensivist, and emergency clinician can find without asking.
  • One agreed default technique, institution-wide. Debate it at a governance meeting, never at a bedside.
  • A cognitive aid on the cart — a single laminated card. Working memory is the first thing hypoxic-patient stress takes away from you.
  • An airway assessment habit that flags the high-risk neck before induction: previous difficult airway, radiotherapy, neck mass, obesity, limited mouth opening, short thyromental distance, prior surgery.
  • A pre-induction verbalized plan for anyone flagged: “Plan A is videolaryngoscopy, plan B is a supraglottic, plan C is front-of-neck and the cart is in that corner.” Naming plan C out loud before you need it dramatically lowers the threshold to reach it.
  • Landmarks identified in advance in the high-risk patient — marking the cricothyroid membrane with a skin pen before induction takes fifteen seconds and removes the hardest step from the worst moment.

07

Complications & Pitfalls

  • Delayed declaration — the dominant failure mode, by a wide margin
  • Choosing the slower technique (Seldinger, or an unfamiliar commercial kit) when speed was the deciding variable
  • Standing down on transient improvement, then having to restart the decision from scratch with less reserve than before
  • Abandoning upper-airway oxygenation the moment CICO is declared — it still buys time, and it is the exhalation route for needle techniques
  • Communication breakdown — if CICO isn't said out loud, help doesn't mobilize and the room stays in intubation mode
  • No pre-agreed default, producing a bedside debate about technique while the patient desaturates
08

Pearls

The hard part is the decision, not the scalpel. Every element of preparation should be aimed at lowering the threshold to that decision.

Say it as a statement. “This is CICO, we are moving to a surgical airway” — not “do you think maybe we should…”

Name people, not roles. Unaddressed instructions are executed by nobody.

Mark the membrane before induction in a high-risk neck. Fifteen seconds then; the hardest step removed later.

Transient improvement is a trap. It is the most common reason a correctly-made decision gets reversed.

Rehearse it out loud. The team that has practiced the sentence moves faster than the individual who has only practiced the procedure.
