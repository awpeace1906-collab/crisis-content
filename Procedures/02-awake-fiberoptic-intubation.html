Awake Fiberoptic Intubation

The controlled, spontaneously-breathing approach to the anticipated difficult airway — topicalize, sedate lightly, scope while the patient protects their own airway.

01

Indications

  • Anticipated difficult airway — limited mouth opening, cervical immobility, airway mass or distortion, radiotherapy-scarred neck, prior documented difficult intubation
  • Upper airway pathology (tumor, abscess, angioedema, supraglottic swelling) where induction could convert a partial obstruction into a complete one
  • Unstable cervical spine where laryngoscopy positioning is contraindicated
  • Any situation where you want the patient's own respiratory effort preserved as the safety net through the intubation
The whole point is that nothing irreversible happens. Every other airway technique begins by removing the patient's ability to breathe for themselves and then betting you can replace it. This one doesn't place that bet. That is the reason to choose it, and also the reason oversedation is the cardinal sin — it quietly converts this procedure into the one you were trying to avoid.
02

Contraindications

  • A rapidly deteriorating airway. This technique costs 15–30 minutes done properly. A patient who does not have that is a patient for a surgical airway or an awake look with a plan to cut.
  • Uncooperative patient — delirium, intoxication, learning disability, young children (relative; may force an alternative plan rather than heavier sedation, which defeats the purpose)
  • Active vomiting or frank blood in the airway — you cannot see through either, and topicalization of a wet airway is unreliable
  • Severe local anesthetic allergy (relative — alternative agents exist)
  • Critical stenosis where the scope itself may obstruct what little lumen remains — think carefully before passing a 5 mm scope through a 6 mm airway
03

Equipment — Laid Out Before You Touch the Patient

  • Flexible scope, light source and screen, checked and focused before the patient is in the room. Anti-fog applied, or the tip warmed.
  • ETT preloaded on the shaft, pushed up to the handle and secured there. A reinforced or flexible-tip tube, or an Aintree/intubating catheter arrangement, snags far less than a standard PVC bevel.
  • One size smaller than you'd normally choose — a 6.0–7.0 in most adults. The narrower the tube, the smaller the step-off between scope and tube tip, and the step-off is what catches.
  • Lidocaine, and a plan for the total dose. Atomiser (MAD-type), nebuliser, and a syringe for spray-as-you-go down the working channel.
  • Antisialagogue — glycopyrrolate, given early enough to work (see below).
  • Vasoconstrictor for the nasal route — oxymetazoline or phenylephrine.
  • Sedation you can titrate in small increments — dexmedetomidine, remifentanil, or low-dose midazolam/fentanyl. Draw it up dilute.
  • Oxygen throughout — nasal cannula under the mask, or high-flow nasal oxygen, running the entire time.
  • The difficult airway trolley open in the room, and a named person ready to do a front-of-neck airway if this goes wrong.
Give the antisialagogue early or don't bother

Glycopyrrolate needs roughly 15–20 minutes to meaningfully dry secretions. Given as you walk into the room it contributes nothing except tachycardia. Secretions are the most common reason the view is unusable, and this is the cheapest intervention against them — but only if someone thought about it a quarter of an hour ago.

04

Topicalization — This Is the Procedure

A well-topicalized patient needs almost no sedation; a poorly topicalized one cannot be rescued by any amount of it

Work through the territories in order, each with its own nerve supply:

  • Nasal cavity (if nasal route) — vasoconstrictor plus lidocaine; anterior ethmoidal and sphenopalatine supply.
  • Oropharynx and tongue base — glossopharyngeal. This is the gag reflex. Atomized lidocaine, and ask the patient to hold it at the back of the throat rather than swallowing immediately.
  • Supraglottis, epiglottis, aryepiglottic folds — superior laryngeal nerve (internal branch).
  • Vocal cords and trachea below them — recurrent laryngeal. This territory is the one people under-treat, and it is where coughing comes from.

Spray-as-you-go through the working channel is the workhorse: advance a little, spray, wait ten or fifteen seconds, advance again. It topicalizes exactly what you are about to touch, in the right order, without a separate procedure.

Keep a running total of the lidocaine. Topical airway lidocaine is absorbed rapidly across mucosa — nebulized, atomized, and sprayed doses all add up, and it is easy to lose count across three different delivery devices. Know your institution's ceiling for topical airway use, say the running total out loud, and have intralipid available in the department. Early LAST is agitation, perioral tingling, and metallic taste — symptoms you may misread as inadequate sedation and treat by giving more of the wrong thing.

05

Stepwise Technique

1
Consent and coach the patient
Explain it in plain terms — that they will stay awake and breathing, that their throat will feel numb and strange, that they may feel pressure but not pain, and that coughing is normal. A patient who knows what is coming tolerates it dramatically better. This conversation is part of the technique, not a courtesy before it.
2
Position sitting up, and stand where you'll actually scope from
Sitting or semi-recumbent is more comfortable, improves their respiratory mechanics, and drains secretions away. Decide early whether you scope from in front (facing them) or from behind at the head, and set the screen where you can see it without turning away from the patient.
3
Oxygen on, monitoring on, sedation titrated to conversation
The endpoint for sedation is a calm patient who still answers you. If they stop responding to voice, you have gone too far. Dexmedetomidine preserves respiratory drive well but takes time to load; remifentanil blunts cough well but will cause apnoea if you rush it. Small increments, and wait for the peak effect before giving more — stacking doses while waiting is the classic route to an apnoeic patient.
4
Advance in the midline, and keep the scope straight
Keep the shaft straight between your hand and the patient — a looped or coiled shaft means your tip movements stop corresponding to your wrist movements, which is the single most common reason a novice gets lost. Advance under continuous vision. If you cannot identify a structure, withdraw until you can rather than pushing on in hope. The one rule that recovers every lost scope: pull back to something you recognize.
5
Identify the glottis, spray, and pass the cords
Landmarks in order: uvula and soft palate, epiglottis, then the cords. Spray through the channel before passing them — this is where cough is generated. Advance the tip through the glottis and confirm you are tracheal by seeing cartilaginous rings and the carina. Rings and a bifurcation are proof; a dark hole is not.
6
Railroad the tube — rotate, don't shove
Hold the scope absolutely still, as a rail, and advance the tube with a 90° counterclockwise rotation if it hangs up. The tube catches on the right arytenoid in the great majority of cases, and that rotation turns the bevel away from it. Escalating force does not work and causes trauma; rotation almost always does.
7
Confirm depth from inside before you withdraw
Before removing the scope, look: you should see the carina a few centimeters beyond the tube tip. This is a better depth check than any external marking. Then withdraw the scope slowly, watching that the tube does not come with it.
8
Confirm, secure, and only then induce
Capnography, chest rise, bilateral air entry. Secure the tube. Only now give the induction agent. Inducing before confirmation discards the entire safety rationale of the technique — and it happens, under time pressure, when everyone in the room can see the tube is “obviously” in.
Sedation depth is the whole safety margin

Every milligram you give trades away the thing that makes this technique safe. The patient who stops breathing during an awake fiberoptic has not had a complication of the procedure — they have had a complication of the sedation, and they are now an unanticipated CICO with a scope in their mouth. If you are reaching for more sedation, the problem is almost always inadequate topicalization, and more local is the correct answer.

Complications & Pitfalls

  • Oversedation converting an awake technique into an unplanned loss of airway — the central risk of the procedure
  • Local anesthetic systemic toxicity from cumulative topical dosing across multiple devices
  • Epistaxis obscuring the view and, occasionally, soiling the airway outright
  • Laryngospasm from instrumenting an inadequately topicalized larynx
  • Complete obstruction in a critical stenosis — the scope or the resulting edema taking the last of a marginal lumen
  • Esophageal intubation if orientation was lost and rings were never positively identified
  • Taking too long in a patient whose airway was deteriorating faster than the operator appreciated
08

Pearls

Topicalization buys you everything; sedation buys you very little and costs a great deal. When tempted to deepen sedation, give more local instead.

Keep the shaft straight. Loops are why the tip stops obeying your hand.

When lost, withdraw. Pulling back to a recognized structure recovers nearly every disoriented scope; pushing forward in hope recovers none.

Rings and carina, or you are not in the trachea. A dark hole is not a diagnosis.

Rotate the tube, don't force it. The right arytenoid is the obstacle, and 90° counterclockwise is the answer.

Confirm before you induce. This is the entire safety argument for the technique; do not spend it at the last step.

Decide your abort criterion out loud beforehand — a time or a number of attempts — because in the moment there is always a reason to try once more.

Glycopyrrolate at least fifteen minutes ahead. Secretions ruin more of these than any technical problem.
