Field Amputation

An exceedingly rare, last-resort extrication technique — entrapment with an unsalvageable limb and a threat to life that transport delay would worsen.

01

Indications

This is an extrication procedure, not a surgical one. The goal is a released patient, not a good stump.

  • Entrapment that cannot be relieved by any other means, where remaining trapped will kill the patient — progressing structural collapse, fire, rising water, an unstable scene, or physiological deterioration that will not survive the extrication timeline
  • Completion of a near-total traumatic amputation — a limb held by a bridge of skin, tendon, or a strip of muscle, where dividing that bridge frees the patient. This is by far the most common version of this procedure, and it is a fundamentally different and easier act than cutting through an intact limb.
  • An unsalvageable limb whose continued attachment is itself the threat — extensive crush with a non-viable segment, in a patient deteriorating from that segment
  • Immediate scene danger requiring evacuation now, where the entrapment is the only thing preventing it

What makes this indicated is almost never the limb. It is the clock, the scene, or the patient's physiology. A mangled limb in a stable patient at a safe scene, however dreadful it looks, is an operating-room problem.

02

Contraindications

  • Any remaining technical rescue option. Cutting the environment beats cutting the patient, every time, even when it is slower. Hydraulic spreaders, cutting torches, airbags, cribbing, and concrete saws release far more patients than scalpels do. Exhaust this first and say out loud that you have.
  • A salvageable limb in a patient who can wait. If the delay is survivable, the limb goes to the operating room attached.
  • A scene too unsafe for the operator. A second casualty helps nobody. This is the incident commander's call, not the clinician's.
  • No means of hemorrhage control. Do not cut without a functioning tourniquet already applied and tightened. This is not a sequencing preference — it is the difference between an extrication and an exsanguination.
  • Death already declared. Amputation to recover a body is a medical examiner and rescue decision, made outside this framework.
03

The Decision, and Who Makes It

Field amputation is among the rarest procedures in this library, and most clinicians will never perform one. The single most useful preparation is knowing your system's activation pathway before the day it is needed — who carries the kit, who is credentialed, and what number summons them.

  • It is physician- or surgeon-led in almost every system — a HEMS physician, an urban search-and-rescue medical team member, or a surgeon dispatched to the scene. It is not a standard prehospital scope of practice.
  • It is a joint decision with technical rescue and the incident commander. The clinician owns the patient; the rescue team owns whether any other route out exists, and whether the scene will still be standing in twenty minutes.
  • Two clinicians if at all possible. One to perform, one to manage the airway, sedation, and resuscitation. Doing both alone is possible and is much worse.
  • Consent is usually impossible. Act under emergency doctrine. If the patient is awake and able, tell them what is happening and why — they are frequently conscious throughout, and that fact deserves acknowledging rather than avoiding.
  • Brief the team before you start — including the rescue crew who will see it. Say what is about to happen, who does what, and what the signal to stop is. Then debrief afterwards. This is one of the small number of procedures where the psychological burden on the team is a foreseeable, plannable consequence rather than an afterthought.
04

The Kit

  • Two tourniquets minimum, windlass-type, applied proximally and tightened before the incision. Two, because one can fail and there is no re-doing this.
  • Gigli saw with handles — a flexible wire saw. The workhorse of field amputation: it needs no power, works in confined and awkward spaces where a rigid blade cannot be swung, packs into nothing, and cuts bone quickly. If your kit holds one instrument for bone, this is it.
  • Amputation knife or a large scalpel (No. 22 blade), plus heavy trauma shears — which alone are often enough to complete a near-total amputation.
  • Bone cutters for small bones and for trimming spicules.
  • Arterial clamps and ties — large vessels should be secured rather than left to a tourniquet alone where the anatomy allows.
  • Hemostatic gauze and bulky dressings for the stump.
  • Ketamine (and a benzodiazepine), airway equipment, and a monitor. See §05.
  • IV/IO access, warmed crystalloid, and blood products if the system carries them. Also calcium and the means to treat hyperkalemia — see §06.
  • Headlamp, and eye protection for everyone within range.
  • A marker and a watch. Tourniquet time starts the moment it is tightened.
05

Anesthesia at the Scene

A trapped, hypothermic, hypovolemic patient in a confined space is among the worst environments imaginable for sedating someone. The plan has to fit that.

  • Ketamine is the agent of choice — it preserves airway reflexes and respiratory drive, and it supports blood pressure rather than dropping it. Typical dissociative dosing is around 1–2 mg/kg IV or 4–5 mg/kg IM where no line exists, reduced substantially in shock, with repeat increments titrated to effect. Confirm against your own service's formulary.
  • Add a benzodiazepine for amnesia and to blunt emergence, if the hemodynamics tolerate it.
  • Opioid as an adjunct, with the usual caution about stacking respiratory depression on a patient you cannot reposition.
  • Have an airway plan you can actually execute in that space. If the patient is head-down under a vehicle, ask honestly whether you could intubate them where they lie. If the answer is no, that constrains the sedation depth you can safely aim for.
  • Local anesthetic infiltration at the incision line is a reasonable adjunct and costs nothing.
  • They may remain aware. Talk to them. Have someone whose only job is to be at their head.
06

Crush Physiology — Treat Before You Release

The danger is the moment of release, not the cut

A limb crushed for hours is an ischemic reservoir of potassium, myoglobin, phosphate, and acid. The instant circulation is restored or the compressing force is removed, that load reaches the heart — the phenomenon historically called “rescue death” or “smiling death,” in which a conscious, talking, apparently stable trapped patient arrests within minutes of being freed. The resuscitation for this happens before extrication, not after.

  • Fluids before release. Start warmed crystalloid early and run it generously before the limb is freed — often 1–1.5 L/h in an adult, begun while still entrapped and continued through extrication. Confirm rates against your own crush-injury protocol.
  • Have calcium drawn up for hyperkalemic cardiac membrane stabilization, and have your hyperkalemia treatments ready rather than in a bag somewhere.
  • Monitor the ECG through the release. Peaked T waves and a widening QRS are the warning.
  • Consider sodium bicarbonate for the acid load and to alkalinize urine against myoglobin, per your protocol.
  • One genuine advantage of amputation over simple release: the limb comes off above a tightened tourniquet, so the ischemic contents of the crushed segment leave with it rather than washing into the circulation. Tourniquet-first is not only hemorrhage control — it is metabolic containment.
  • Watch for rhabdomyolysis and acute kidney injury downstream: pigmented urine, rising CK, and the potassium trend.

Technique

1
Confirm no other route out, out loud
Ask the rescue lead directly: “Is there any way to get this patient out without amputating?” Get an explicit answer and note the time. This is the decision that will be examined afterwards, and the people who can answer it are standing next to you.
2
Resuscitate and sedate before you cut
Access, fluids running, calcium available, monitor on, ketamine given and working. Airway person at the head. Do not begin because the scene is loud and urgent — begin because the patient is as ready as they will be.
3
Two tourniquets, as proximal as the space allows, tight
Apply and tighten both — not staged, not loosely pre-positioned. Tighten to loss of distal arterial signal. Write the time on the patient's skin. Every downstream decision depends on that number and it is routinely lost at handover.
4
Cut at the most distal point that achieves release
Preserve every centimeter of length you can. This is not a definitive amputation and no flaps are being planned — length preserved now is prosthetic function and reconstructive options later. Choose the level by what frees the patient, not by textbook amputation levels. Through a joint is often faster and easier than through a shaft, and disarticulation is entirely acceptable here.
5
Guillotine: skin, then muscle, then bone
A single circumferential incision through skin and soft tissue at one level — no flaps, no shaping. Clamp or tie large vessels as you meet them if you can see them; the tourniquet covers what you cannot. Retract the soft tissue proximally and pass the Gigli saw around the bone, cutting with long, steady, full-length strokes rather than short fast ones. Divide any remaining attachments and the limb is free.
6
If it is a near-total amputation, this is much simpler
Most field amputations are the completion of an injury that has already happened. Tourniquet first as always, then divide the remaining skin, tendon, or muscle bridge with heavy shears or a scalpel. No saw, no bone work. Recognizing that you are in this situation rather than the other one is worth the ten seconds it takes to look.
7
Extricate immediately and reassess the tourniquets
Move the patient out. Then look at the stump again — tourniquets loosen during extrication, and bleeding that was controlled in situ can restart the moment the patient is moved. Add a third tourniquet or pack the stump if needed.
8
Dress, warm, and resuscitate properly
Hemostatic gauze and a bulky dressing over the stump. Aggressive rewarming — these patients are cold from prolonged exposure and the coagulopathy that follows is avoidable. Blood products, TXA within the window, calcium, and analgesia.
9
Bring the amputated part with the patient
Wrapped in saline-moistened gauze, in a sealed bag, on ice — never in direct contact with ice and never frozen. Replantation is unlikely after a crush entrapment, but the decision belongs to the surgeon, and the part may also matter for identification and for tissue coverage.

Complications

  • Exsanguination — the immediate risk, and almost entirely a function of whether the tourniquet was on and tight before the incision.
  • Hyperkalemic arrest on reperfusion and crush syndrome — the most under-anticipated hazard of the whole procedure.
  • Rhabdomyolysis and acute kidney injury.
  • Hypothermia and trauma-induced coagulopathy — prolonged entrapment, exposure, and cold fluids all compound.
  • Infection — the field is filthy by definition. Broad antibiotics and tetanus prophylaxis; the stump needs formal surgical revision and washout.
  • Insufficient residual limb length, restricting prosthetic options. The reason to cut as distally as release allows.
  • Nerve and vessel retraction making later revision harder.
  • Awareness and recall in an under-sedated patient, with lasting psychological consequences.
  • Psychological injury to the team and to the rescue crew. Foreseeable, and therefore something to plan for rather than absorb.
10

After

  • Pre-alert the receiving trauma center with the specifics: the level, tourniquet times, the drugs given, the entrapment duration, and the potassium risk. They should have an operating room and blood ready.
  • Tourniquet times, written and spoken. The single number most often lost and most consequential.
  • Entrapment duration matters as much as tourniquet time for predicting the metabolic picture.
  • Serial potassium, CK, creatinine, lactate, and gas — and urine color.
  • The stump goes to the operating room for formal revision, washout, and a decision about closure. A field amputation is never definitive.
  • Debrief the whole team, including the rescue crew, and follow up afterwards rather than only on the day. Make the offer specific and repeat it.
  • Document the decision-making carefully: who was present, what extrication options were exhausted, who concurred, and the time each decision was made. This will be reviewed, and it should be.
11

Pearls

Cut the environment, not the patient — until you genuinely cannot. Then say out loud that you cannot, and note who agreed.

Most field amputations are completions. A bridge of skin and tendon divided with heavy shears. Look before you assume you need a saw.

Tourniquets on and tight before the blade. Two of them, and write the time on the skin.

The danger is the release, not the cut. Fluids running and calcium drawn before the limb comes free.

Amputating above a tourniquet contains the crush load — the ischemic metabolites leave with the limb instead of reaching the heart.

Keep every centimeter of length. No flaps, no shaping, most distal level that frees them. Disarticulation is fine.

The Gigli saw is the right tool — no power, confined spaces, packs into nothing. Carry a spare wire.

Ketamine, and someone whose only job is the patient's head.

Take the limb with you, cool but never frozen.

Know your system's pathway before the day. The best preparation for a procedure you will probably never do is knowing exactly who to call.

Brief before, debrief after. Including the rescue crew.
