Field Amputation

An exceedingly rare, last-resort extrication technique — entrapment with an unsalvageable limb and a threat to life that transport delay would worsen.

MASS CASUALTY
⚡ TECHNIQUE Indications Contraindications Equipment Complications & Pitfalls Pearls References
01

Indications

  • Entrapment of a limb that cannot be extricated by any other means, where the limb is already unsalvageable (mangled, non-viable) and continued entrapment poses an immediate threat to the patient's life
  • Confined-space or structural collapse scenarios where standard extrication (cutting the entrapping structure rather than the patient) has been exhausted and is not feasible within a survivable timeframe
02

Contraindications

  • Any scenario where standard extrication techniques (cutting away the entrapping material rather than the patient) remain feasible, even if slower — this is always the preferred approach
  • A potentially salvageable limb where extrication delay does not pose an immediate life threat
03

Equipment

  • Amputation kit if available (surgical saw or heavy shears, hemostatic/tourniquet equipment, sedation/analgesia)
  • Whatever cutting instrument is available in a true improvised scenario
  • Tourniquet(s) for immediate hemorrhage control
04

Stepwise Technique

1
Exhaust all standard extrication options first — this procedure is a genuine last resort, typically performed by a physician or surgeon responding to the scene in coordination with technical rescue/fire services, not a routine field decision.
2
Confirm the limb is non-viable and that continued entrapment is an immediate threat to survival (structural collapse progression, fire, rising water, or physiologic deterioration from prolonged crush injury).
3
Provide maximal available analgesia/sedation given the circumstances.
4
Apply a proximal tourniquet before amputation to control hemorrhage from the outset.
5
Amputate through the most distal viable/accessible point that achieves extrication, using whatever surgical or improvised means are available.
6
Complete extrication and immediately transition to standard trauma/hemorrhage resuscitation, including reassessment of tourniquet effectiveness and definitive surgical management planning.
Every standard extrication option comes first

This is one of the rarest procedures in the entire library for a reason — nearly every entrapment scenario resolves through cutting the environment, not the patient. Tourniquet control must be established before the cut, not improvised after.

05

Complications & Pitfalls

  • Massive hemorrhage if tourniquet control is not established before or immediately upon amputation
  • Profound psychological impact on both patient and providers — this is one of the most extreme interventions in emergency medicine and should be recognized as such for the entire team
  • Infection risk given the non-sterile, improvised nature of most field amputation scenarios
  • Crush syndrome/reperfusion physiology if a tourniquet is released or extrication occurs after prolonged entrapment without appropriate resuscitation first (see Crush Syndrome/Entrapment content elsewhere in Crisis Management)
06

Pearls

This is one of the rarest procedures in the entire library for a reason — nearly every entrapment scenario resolves through cutting the environment, not the patient. Treat this as the true last resort it is.

Tourniquet control before the cut, not after — hemorrhage control needs to be established as part of the plan, not improvised in the moment of extrication.

This procedure is typically physician/surgeon-led when performed in the field (e.g., HEMS physician response, USAR medical team) rather than a standard paramedic-level intervention — know your scope and your system's activation pathway for this exact scenario before you ever need it.
