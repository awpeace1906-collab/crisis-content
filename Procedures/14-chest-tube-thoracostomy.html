Chest Tube Placement (Tube Thoracostomy)

A full open technique for hemothorax and pneumothorax — from site selection through the post-placement film and removal timing.

THORACIC
⚡ TECHNIQUE Indications Contraindications Equipment Complications & Pitfalls Pearls References
01

Indications

  • Pneumothorax (simple, traumatic, or post-finger-thoracostomy definitive management)
  • Hemothorax
  • Traumatic hemopneumothorax
  • Empyema or complicated pleural effusion requiring drainage (non-emergent variant of the same technique)
02

Contraindications

  • No absolute contraindications in a true emergent pneumothorax/hemothorax with hemodynamic compromise
  • Relative: coagulopathy (correct if time permits and patient is stable enough), diaphragmatic hernia at the intended site (imaging guidance preferred if not emergent), extensive pleural adhesions (may require alternate site or surgical approach)
03

Equipment

  • 2% chlorhexidine solution, sterile gauze, sterile drapes
  • Local anesthetic (if patient status allows)
  • Chest tube, 32–36 Fr for adult trauma/hemothorax indications
  • Two clamps/forceps, #10 scalpel
  • Kelly clamp (or equivalent) for blunt dissection
  • Suture for securing the tube
  • Pleural drainage system (e.g., Pleurovac)
  • Petroleum-based gauze, additional sterile gauze, pressure dressing supplies
04

Stepwise Technique

1
Position the patient supine or semi-recumbent with the arm raised over the head to expose the lateral chest wall.
2
Identify the insertion site — typically the 4th or 5th intercostal space, anterior to mid-axillary line, at or above the level of the nipple.
3
Consider local anesthetic depending on patient status.
4
Sterilize widely with 2% chlorhexidine; create a large sterile field with sterile gauze; drape, exposing only the marked area.
5
Grasp the proximal free end of the chest tube (32–36 Fr) with a clamp/forceps and the distal tip with another — do not let instrument tips pass the end of the tube.
6
Make a 1.5–3.0 cm incision with a #10 scalpel, parallel to the rib, through subcutaneous tissue to the level of the intercostal muscles.
7
Bluntly dissect superiorly with a Kelly clamp, tunneling up to the intercostal space directly above the rib — stay off the inferior rib margin to avoid the subcostal neurovascular bundle.
8
At the intercostal space, position the clamp tips at the superior rib edge and apply firm, controlled pressure to pass through the parietal pleura (this may require significant force).
9
Insert a finger into the thoracic space to confirm thoracostomy and check for adhesions or unanticipated findings.
10
Insert the chest tube along the dissected tract, directing it apically/posteriorly for fluid or anteriorly/superiorly for pneumothorax evacuation.
11
Advance until the last side hole is within the pleural space, with the proximal hole at least 2 cm beyond the rib margin — skin-level marker typically 10–14 cm in an average adult.
12
Secure with mattress or interrupted sutures on both sides of the incision, snugging tissue around the tube for an air-tight seal; wrap suture around the tube twice and tie.
13
Connect to a sterile pleural drainage system at initial suction of –20 cm H2O; unclamp the distal end once connected.
14
Tape the tube to the patient's side; dress with petroleum-based gauze, additional sterile gauze, and pressure dressings.
15
Obtain an AP chest radiograph to confirm placement and monitor for complications (bleeding, hemothorax, air leak, organ perforation).
16
Time removal based on the original indication; obtain a follow-up chest radiograph 12–24 hours after removal.

Complications & Pitfalls

  • Neurovascular bundle injury from dissecting along the inferior rib margin instead of the superior edge
  • Organ perforation (lung, liver, spleen, diaphragm) from excessive force or poor trajectory control
  • Malposition — tube kinking, insufficient insertion depth, or a side hole left outside the pleural space
  • Inadequate seal at the skin leading to air leak or subcutaneous emphysema
  • Infection from break in sterile technique, particularly relevant in non-emergent placements
  • Failure to confirm with a finger sweep before tube passage — this is the step that catches adhesions and confirms true pleural entry
06

Pearls

The finger check before tube insertion is non-negotiable — it's the difference between confirmed pleural entry and a blind pass into an unexpected structure.

Direction after entry matters clinically: apical/posterior for air, basilar/posterior for fluid — but in an emergent trauma placement, adequate drainage of the acute threat takes priority over perfect directional placement.

Firm, controlled pressure through the pleura is expected — hesitation here often causes more tissue trauma than a confident, controlled pass.

Don't skip the post-placement film — it's the only way to confirm true intrathoracic position and catch a missed complication before it becomes clinically significant.
