Chest Tube Placement (Tube Thoracostomy)

A full open technique for hemothorax and pneumothorax — from site selection through the post-placement film and removal timing.

THORACIC

01

Indications

  • Pneumothorax — traumatic, tension (after immediate decompression), or a simple pneumothorax large enough or symptomatic enough to need drainage
  • Hemothorax and traumatic hemopneumothorax
  • Any pneumothorax in a patient about to receive positive-pressure ventilation or go to the operating room — a small pneumothorax that was tolerable breathing spontaneously can tension under a ventilator
  • Empyema or complicated parapneumonic effusion (a non-emergent variant of the same technique)
  • Definitive management after a finger thoracostomy or needle decompression — neither of those is an endpoint
02

Contraindications

  • None absolute in a tension pneumothorax or hemothorax with hemodynamic compromise
  • Coagulopathy — relative; correct it if the patient is stable enough to wait, and prefer a smaller-bore tube
  • Suspected diaphragmatic hernia at the intended site — abdominal contents in the chest will be entered instead. If the mechanism suggests it and the patient can wait, image first.
  • Dense pleural adhesions — may need an alternative site or a surgical approach
  • Skin infection over the site — choose a different interspace
03

Equipment

  • Tube. Traditional teaching is 32–36 Fr for traumatic hemothorax. Note this is genuinely contested — multiple trials comparing 14 Fr pigtail catheters against large-bore tubes for traumatic hemothorax have found comparable drainage and failure rates with considerably less pain. Know which your institution and trauma service favour, and know that "bigger drains blood better" is no longer an unexamined truth.
  • #10 scalpel, large Kelly clamp, and a second clamp for the tube tip
  • 0 or 1-0 silk/nylon on a cutting needle for securing
  • Drainage system (three-chamber or digital), primed, with tubing long enough to reach
  • Local anesthetic — plenty of it. 20–30 mL of lidocaine is not excessive for an awake patient; the periosteum and parietal pleura are the painful bits and are frequently under-infiltrated.
  • Chlorhexidine, full drapes, petroleum gauze, dressings, tape
  • Systemic analgesia on board before you start, not requested afterwards
Do not use a trocar

Tubes supplied with a sharp internal trocar are still found on some trolleys.

Passing a tube on a trocar converts a controlled blunt dissection into a blind stab and is the classic mechanism for lung, cardiac and intra-abdominal injury.

Blunt dissection and a finger, every time. If the tube you have comes with a trocar, remove it and set it aside before you start.

04

The Triangle of Safety

Get the site right and most of the complications disappear

Boundaries:

  • Anterior: lateral border of pectoralis major
  • Posterior: anterior border of latissimus dorsi
  • Inferior: the 5th intercostal space, followed along the rib — not a horizontal line.
    • The traditional description (BTS) is a horizontal line at the level of the nipple.
    • On the lateral wall the ribs climb toward the back at roughly 20–25°, so a level line drawn back from the nipple drifts about one interspace low by the latissimus edge — toward the diaphragm (see the figures above).
  • Apex: the axilla

Why this triangle and not somewhere more convenient: the chest wall is thinnest here, so dissection is shorter and less traumatic.

  • Going lower risks the diaphragm and, beneath it, the liver on the right and the spleen on the left — and the diaphragm rises much higher than people expect, especially supine and in expiration.
  • Going too far posteriorly puts the tube under the patient where it kinks and is agonizing to lie on.
  • Going too far anteriorly and medially approaches the internal mammary vessels.
Count from a landmark you can actually feel

The nipple is an unreliable landmark in women, in the obese, and in anyone lying on their side.

  • Count down from the sternal angle to the 2nd rib and work down, or
  • use the inframammary fold with the breast tissue retracted superiorly.

Placing a tube through breast tissue is painful, cosmetically poor, and easily avoided.

05

Stepwise Technique

1
Position and mark

Supine or semi-recumbent, with the arm abducted and the hand behind the head to open the axilla.

Identify and mark the triangle of safety before you prep — landmarks are much harder to find through drapes.

Confirm the side against the imaging and, out loud, against the examination findings.

2
Anesthetize generously, and reach the two structures that hurt
  1. Skin first,
  2. then infiltrate down onto the rib periosteum,
  3. then walk the needle over the superior edge of the rib and infiltrate the parietal pleura.

Those two are the painful structures; a wheal in the skin alone leaves the patient feeling everything that matters.

Aspirating air or blood as you enter the pleura also confirms your depth and your diagnosis before you cut.

3
Incise parallel to the rib, over the rib below your target space

A 2–3 cm incision through skin and subcutaneous fat, sited over the rib below the interspace you intend to enter.

That offset is what creates the short oblique tunnel — it makes the tract self-sealing when the tube comes out.

4
Blunt-dissect up and over the rib

Open the Kelly clamp to spread tissue, withdraw, reinsert, and repeat — tunneling superiorly over the top of the rib.

Stay on the upper border. The intercostal neurovascular bundle runs in the groove beneath each rib, so hugging the inferior margin of the space above is how you find the artery.

Expect this to take longer than you want; this is the part that protects everything underneath.

5
Puncture the pleura deliberately

With the closed clamp tip on the superior edge of the rib, push through the parietal pleura with controlled force, then open the clamp to widen the hole.

There is usually a distinct give, often a rush of air or blood.

Control the depth with your hand — choke up on the instrument so it cannot plunge.

6
Put a finger in. Every time.

Sweep a gloved finger through the tract into the pleural space. You are:

  • confirming you are inside the chest and not in a false tract,
  • feeling for lung sliding under your fingertip, and
  • sweeping for adhesions or — the reason this step exists — abdominal contents from an unrecognized diaphragmatic injury.

This is the single step most often skipped to save seconds and most likely to cause a serious injury when it is.

7
Railroad the tube on the clamp

Clamp the tube tip and guide it along your finger through the tract.

  • Posteriorly and apically is a reasonable default that drains both air and fluid in a supine trauma patient;
  • strictly apical for air, basal for fluid, if you have the luxury of choosing.

Advance until every side hole is inside the pleural space — the last hole sits on the tube's radiopaque line interruption, and leaving it outside gives a persistent air leak and subcutaneous emphysema.

Typically 10–14 cm at the skin in an adult; more in a large chest.

8
Secure it so it cannot move

A single heavy interrupted stitch closing the incision snugly around the tube, then wrap the free ends around the tube several times and tie.

Add a second anchoring stitch.

Do not place a purse-string — it is obsolete, it necroses the skin edge, and it leaves a puckered scar without improving the seal.

Then tape the tubing to the chest wall in a loop so that a tug pulls on the tape rather than the tube.

9
Connect, look for swing and bubble, then film

Connect to the drainage system below the level of the chest.

Look immediately for respiratory swing in the fluid column — that is your confirmation the tube communicates with the pleural space.

Suction at around −20 cm H2O if indicated.

Then get a chest radiograph for position and re-expansion.

06

Reading the Drain

The bottle tells you what is happening inside the chest
What you seeWhat it means
Swinging (fluid column rises and falls with respiration)The tube is patent and communicating with the pleural space. This is the reassuring sign you want.
Bubbling on coughing onlyA small air leak, resolving. Expected early after a pneumothorax.
Continuous bubblingOngoing air leak — from the lung, or from the system. Check the system first: a side hole outside the chest, a loose connection, or a cracked bottle are all commoner than a bronchopleural fistula.
No swing, no bubblingEither the lung is fully re-expanded and the tube is redundant, or the tube is blocked, kinked, or clamped. Do not assume the happy explanation — check the tube and the film.
Sudden stop in a previously draining hemothoraxClot occluding the tube. The blood is still accumulating; the drain has simply stopped telling you about it.
Brisk ongoing blood lossInstitutional thresholds vary, but figures around 1–1.5 L immediately, or >200 mL/hr for 2–4 hours, are widely used triggers for surgical consultation. Trend and physiology matter more than any single number.
Drain a large chronic effusion slowly

Removing a very large, long-standing effusion rapidly risks re-expansion pulmonary edema — uncommon but occasionally severe.

A widely taught ceiling is around 1–1.5 L in one sitting, then clamp and continue later, stopping earlier if the patient develops chest tightness or cough.

This does not apply to acutely draining a hemothorax or tension pneumothorax, where you drain what needs draining.

Troubleshooting & Failure Modes

ProblemUsual causeWhat to do
Cannot get through the pleuraNot enough controlled force, or dissecting into the ribReconfirm you are on the superior rib edge; commit with controlled pressure. Hesitation causes more trauma than a decisive pass
Subcutaneous emphysema after placementA side hole left outside the pleura, or tube dislodgedCheck the film and the insertion depth. Advance or replace — do not simply add dressings
Persistent large air leakSystem fault far more often than bronchopleural fistulaWork from patient to bottle: insertion site, connections, tubing, bottle. Then consider a genuine lung injury

Tube appears intraparenchymal on CTPassed into lung, usually through adhesionsSurgical/thoracic input before manipulating it

Complications & Pitfalls

  • Intercostal neurovascular injury from dissecting along the inferior margin of the space
  • Organ injury — lung, liver, spleen, diaphragm, and rarely heart or great vessels. Nearly always a consequence of a trocar, a site that was too low, or a skipped finger sweep
  • Placement into the abdomen through an unrecognised diaphragmatic rupture
  • Malposition — fissural, subcutaneous, intraparenchymal, or simply not in far enough
  • Re-expansion pulmonary edema after rapid drainage of a large chronic effusion
  • Empyema and retained hemothorax as later consequences of incomplete drainage
  • Inadequate analgesia — consistently under-recognized, and one of the procedures patients recall most unpleasantly
09

Removal

  • Criteria: the original indication is resolved — no air leak, drainage volume acceptably low, lung re-expanded on film, and the patient off positive pressure or stable on it per your unit's practice.
  • Analgesia first. Removal hurts.
  • Have the occlusive dressing prepared and in your hand before the tube moves.
  • Timing with the respiratory cycle: remove at end-inspiration or during a Valsalva, when intrapleural pressure is least likely to draw air in. Practice varies; what matters is that the patient is not inhaling as the tube exits and the dressing goes on immediately.
  • One smooth, brisk pull, then seal.
  • Follow-up film a few hours after removal to exclude a recurrent pneumothorax.
10

Pearls

The finger sweep is not optional. It is what stands between a routine procedure and putting a tube through a diaphragm into a liver.

Superior edge of the rib, always. The bundle lives under the rib above.

Anesthetize the periosteum and the pleura, not just the skin. Those are the structures that hurt.

Swing confirms position better than the film does — and you get it instantly, at the bedside.

Continuous bubbling is a system fault until proven otherwise. Work from the patient outward before diagnosing a lung injury.

No trocar. No purse-string. Both are legacy practice that cause harm.

A tube that stops draining a hemothorax has not cured it. Suspect clot, and re-image rather than relaxing.
