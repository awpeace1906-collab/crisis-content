Percutaneous / Surgical Tracheostomy

The definitive-airway conversation, not the crisis-moment tool. Adapted from the author's original “Introduction to Surgical Airways” teaching deck, used with permission.

AIRWAY

01

Indications

  • Long-term ventilator-assisted respiration anticipated
  • Failure to wean or prolonged weaning from mechanical ventilation
  • Inability to protect the airway with an anticipated prolonged course
  • Definitive airway control following cricothyrotomy as a bridge (conversion to tracheostomy once stabilized)
02

Contraindications

  • Emergent CICO scenario — tracheostomy is not the front-line rescue airway; cricothyrotomy is faster and anatomically more accessible in a crisis
  • Uncorrected coagulopathy for elective/semi-elective percutaneous approach (relative)
  • Distorted neck anatomy or inability to access the site (favors surgical/open approach in a controlled setting, e.g., OR)
03

Equipment

  • Scalpel, retractors, dissection instruments, suction, tracheostomy tube, suture
  • OR setting typically required — this is a controlled, planned procedure, not a rapid rescue technique
  • Bronchoscopic guidance where available for the percutaneous dilational approach
04

Stepwise Technique

Open surgical tracheostomy
1
Position patient supine with neck extended (if not contraindicated by cervical spine precautions).
2
Identify the site — closer to the sternoclavicular notch than the cricothyroid membrane, at approximately the 2nd–3rd tracheal ring level.
3
Make a transverse or vertical skin incision as per surgeon preference/institutional protocol.
4
Perform meticulous dissection through the strap muscles, retracting to expose the thyroid isthmus.
5
Divide or retract the thyroid isthmus as needed to expose the trachea.
6
Hold the field open with retractors.
7
Create the tracheal opening at the bottom of the 3rd and top of the 4th tracheal ring — excise a small window rather than a complete tracheal ring resection.
8
Insert the tracheostomy tube into the created opening.
9
Confirm placement (chest rise, EtCO2, bilateral breath sounds).
10
Secure the tube, dress the site.
This is not a rescue airway

Tracheostomy belongs to the planned, definitive-airway conversation once a patient is stabilized — attempting it as a first response in a true CICO emergency costs time that cricothyrotomy does not.

05

Complications & Pitfalls

  • Not a rapid rescue airway — attempting tracheostomy in a true CICO emergency costs time that cricothyrotomy does not
  • Requires an OR in the standard surgical approach — logistically incompatible with emergent bedside crisis management outside specific percutaneous bedside protocols
  • More destructive/invasive than cricothyrotomy — greater tissue disruption, longer procedure time, higher bleeding risk from the thyroid isthmus and surrounding vasculature
  • Over-resection of tracheal rings — removing a fully transected ring segment is a technical error; the opening should be a window, not a full ring excision
  • Bleeding from thyroid isthmus if not adequately controlled during dissection/division
06

Pearls

If the question is “can this patient breathe right now,” the answer is cricothyrotomy, not tracheostomy — tracheostomy belongs to the planned, definitive-airway conversation.

Site selection matters: too high risks subglottic stenosis and interferes with a prior cricothyrotomy site; too low risks innominate vessel injury.

When converting a crisis cricothyrotomy to a definitive tracheostomy, this is typically done electively in a controlled OR setting once the patient is stabilized.
