01

Indications

  • Anticipated prolonged mechanical ventilation — the commonest reason by far
  • Failure to wean, or a protracted weaning course where a trachy improves comfort, sedation requirements, and secretion clearance
  • Inability to protect the airway with an expected prolonged course (bulbar weakness, severe brain injury)
  • Upper airway obstruction requiring a durable bypass
  • Definitive conversion following an emergency cricothyrotomy, once the patient is stable
This is not a rescue airway

If the question is “can this patient breathe in the next four minutes,” the answer is a cricothyrotomy, not a tracheostomy.

  • The cricothyroid membrane is superficial, avascular, and immediately below the skin;
  • the trachea at the 2nd–3rd ring sits deeper, behind the strap muscles and often behind the thyroid isthmus, in a more vascular field.

Attempting a tracheostomy in a true CICO costs time the patient does not have. See the Cricothyrotomy entry.

Contraindications & Choosing Percutaneous vs Open

  • Emergent CICO — absolute, for the reason above
  • Uncorrected coagulopathy (relative; correct first for an elective procedure)
  • Age under ~12–16 — pediatric tracheostomy is an open, specialist procedure; percutaneous technique is not appropriate
FeatureFavors percutaneous (bedside)Favors open (theatre)
AnatomyPalpable landmarks, normal neckObesity, short neck, impalpable cricoid, goitre, fixed neck
Prior surgery/radiotherapyNonePrevious neck surgery or radiotherapy — distorted planes, scarred tissue
CoagulationNormal or correctedCoagulopathy, or high aberrant vessels on ultrasound
SpineNeck can be extendedUnstable cervical spine, fixed flexion deformity
LogisticsAvoids transfer of an unstable ICU patientDifficult anatomy worth doing under direct vision
UrgencySemi-electiveSemi-elective — neither is an emergency technique

Equipment & Team

Percutaneous dilational (bedside ICU)

  • Commercial PDT kit — introducer needle, guidewire, single graduated dilator (or sequential dilators), loading dilators, tracheostomy tube
  • Bronchoscope with a dedicated operator — a second clinician whose only job is the scope and the view
  • Ultrasound to map midline, tracheal rings, thyroid isthmus and, critically, aberrant anterior vessels before puncture
  • Capnography, suction, full monitoring, emergency reintubation equipment immediately to hand
  • A named airway operator at the head managing the existing ETT — this role is as important as the operator at the neck

Open surgical (theatre)

  • Scalpel, retractors (including a Cricoid/Langenbeck), dissecting scissors, diathermy, suction, ties/sutures
  • Tracheostomy tubes: your intended size, plus one smaller, plus a cuffed ETT for rescue
  • Tracheal dilator (Trousseau) and tracheal hook — the instruments that save a lost tract
  • Shoulder roll for neck extension
Have a smaller tube and a reintubation plan open in the room

The two moments this procedure goes wrong are:

  • losing the airway at the head during the exchange, and
  • failing to place the tracheostomy tube after the trachea is already open.

Both are survivable if a cuffed ETT and a smaller trachy tube are physically present and someone is assigned to the oral airway. Both are disasters if they must be fetched.

04

Percutaneous Dilational Tracheostomy — Stepwise

Two operators, two airways, one shared moment of vulnerability
1
Position, pre-oxygenate, and map with ultrasound

Shoulder roll, neck extended (if the spine allows), FiO2 1.0.

Scan before you prep:

  • identify midline,
  • count the rings,
  • find the cricoid,
  • locate the thyroid isthmus, and
  • look specifically for an aberrant anterior jugular or high innominate vessel crossing your intended puncture site.

Mark the skin.

2
Withdraw the ETT under bronchoscopic vision — do not extubate

The head operator withdraws the existing tube until the cuff sits just below the cords, with the bronchoscope confirming.

This is the single most dangerous step of the procedure:

  • pulled too far you have extubated a critically ill patient;
  • left too deep and your introducer needle will puncture the ETT cuff or the tube itself.

The scope stays in the ETT throughout.

3
Puncture between the 1st–2nd or 2nd–3rd rings, transilluminating and watching from inside

Small horizontal skin incision, blunt-dissect down to pretracheal tissue, then introduce the needle in the midline, angled caudally, aspirating air.

The bronchoscopist confirms the needle enters in the midline, at the correct interspace, and not through the posterior wall.

  • Too high (through the cricoid or the 1st ring) causes subglottic stenosis;
  • too low risks the innominate artery.
4
Wire in, confirm it heads toward the carina

Thread the J-wire caudally and have the bronchoscopist confirm it is running down toward the carina, not up toward the cords.

Remove the needle.

The wire is now the only thing preserving your tract — it does not get released.

5
Dilate over the wire, countering with the other hand

Pass the loading dilator, then the graduated dilator over the wire with a firm, steady, rotating advance.

Support the anterior neck with your other hand to counter the force — unopposed pushing indents the trachea and risks posterior wall injury.

Keep the wire moving freely; if it kinks, stop, straighten and start the dilation again rather than forcing over a bent wire.

6
Railroad the tracheostomy tube, then remove obturator and wire together

Load the tube on its dilator, advance over the wire into the trachea, then remove the inner dilator and the wire as one.

Inflate the cuff, connect the circuit, and confirm with capnography and the bronchoscope passed down the new tube — you want to see rings and carina through the trachy, not just hear breath sounds.

7
Only now remove the ETT

The oral tube stays in place, cuff down, until the tracheostomy is confirmed working. It is your rescue airway, and withdrawing it early is how a recoverable problem becomes an emergency.

Once confirmed, remove it, secure the trachy with ties and/or sutures, and document tube size, depth, and the number of dilations.

05

Open Surgical Tracheostomy — Stepwise

1
Position and mark

Supine, shoulder roll, neck extended if permitted.

Palpate and mark the thyroid notch, cricoid, and sternal notch.

Your target is the 2nd–3rd tracheal ring — roughly midway between cricoid and sternal notch, closer to the notch than the cricothyroid membrane.

2
Incise and stay midline

Transverse incision:

  • about two fingerbreadths above the sternal notch (better cosmesis), or
  • vertical midline (faster, extensible).

Then dissect strictly in the midline raphe between the strap muscles and retract them laterally — the midline is the avascular plane, and wandering off it is what produces bleeding.

3
Deal with the thyroid isthmus deliberately
  • Retract it superiorly if it will move;
  • if not, divide it between clamps and suture-ligate both ends.

A partially divided, inadequately ligated isthmus is a classic source of troublesome postoperative bleeding into the airway. Do not simply diathermy across it and hope.

4
Expose the rings, place a tracheal hook, and confirm your level

Clear the pretracheal fascia, identify the cricoid and count down.

A tracheal hook under the cricoid steadies and lifts the larynx.

Recount before you cut — this is the last moment the level is free to change.

5
Open the trachea as a window, not a resected ring

Create the opening at the bottom of the 2nd/top of the 3rd ring — a small window or a hinged flap per your institution's practice.

Do not resect a full ring circumferentially; over-resection is a technical error that predisposes to tracheomalacia and stenosis.

Warn the anesthetist before entering: FiO2 should be reduced and diathermy kept away from the open airway, because an airway fire is a real hazard at this moment.

6
Withdraw the ETT, insert the tube, confirm before committing

Ask for the ETT to be withdrawn to just above the stoma — not removed.

Insert the tracheostomy tube, inflate the cuff, connect and confirm with capnography and chest rise.

Only then is the oral tube removed.

Secure with ties and stay sutures.

Stay sutures and a documented tract are the gift you leave the night team

In the first week the tract is immature and will not reliably reopen if the tube falls out.

  • Place stay sutures (traction sutures on either side of the tracheal opening, taped to the chest and clearly labeled),
  • record the tube size and type at the bedside, and
  • ensure the same-size and one-size-smaller tubes stay with the patient.

An accidental decannulation on day two with no stay sutures is a genuine emergency.

07

Complications & Pitfalls

  • Loss of the airway during the exchange — the defining intraoperative risk of both techniques
  • False passage and pretracheal placement, particularly with percutaneous dilation over a kinked wire
  • Bleeding — isthmus, anterior jugular veins, aberrant vessels; and late, catastrophic tracheo-innominate fistula from a tube placed too low
  • Posterior tracheal wall injury and tracheo-esophageal fistula — from unopposed dilation force or an over-deep needle
  • Subglottic stenosis from a stoma placed too high (through the cricoid or 1st ring)
  • Tracheomalacia and tracheal stenosis from over-resection of cartilage
  • Airway fire at the moment of tracheal entry with diathermy and a high FiO2
  • Accidental decannulation into an immature tract — the commonest serious ward-level emergency afterwards
  • Attempting it as a rescue in CICO — the error this whole entry exists to prevent
08

Pearls

If the question is “can this patient breathe right now,” the answer is a cricothyrotomy. Tracheostomy is a planned conversation about the next fortnight.

The oral tube stays in until the tracheostomy is proven. It is the rescue airway, and removing it early converts every recoverable problem into an emergency.

Site selection is the long game. Too high buys subglottic stenosis; too low buys a tracheo-innominate fistula. Count the rings, and count them again before cutting.

Ultrasound before you puncture, specifically to find the vessel that is not supposed to be there.

Midline is the avascular plane. Nearly all troublesome bleeding comes from drifting off it.

A window, not a resected ring.

Stay sutures, a labeled bedside sign, and a spare tube one size down. The procedure's most dangerous day is often day two, on a ward, with a team who didn't place it.
