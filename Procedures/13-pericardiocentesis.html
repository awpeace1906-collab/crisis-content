Pericardiocentesis

Needle decompression of cardiac tamponade — subxiphoid approach, ultrasound-guided when time allows.

01

Indications

  • Cardiac tamponade with haemodynamic compromise — hypotension, raised venous pressure, and echocardiographic tamponade physiology
  • Peri-arrest or arrest where tamponade is a credible reversible cause
  • Symptomatic effusion needing drainage in a controlled setting (a different, far less urgent version of the same procedure)
Tamponade is an echocardiographic diagnosis, not a volume one. A small effusion that accumulated fast can tamponade; a litre that collected over months may not. Look for right atrial systolic collapse (sensitive), right ventricular diastolic collapse (specific), a plethoric non-collapsing IVC, and exaggerated respiratory variation in inflow velocities. Beck's triad is present in a minority of cases and its absence excludes nothing.
02

Contraindications — and When to Do Something Else

  • None absolute in an unstable patient with confirmed tamponade.
  • Traumatic haemopericardium with surgical capability available — this is the important one. Blood in the pericardium after penetrating trauma clots, so a needle frequently drains little or nothing and the negative tap is falsely reassuring. If you have a surgeon and an operating room, the answer is thoracotomy or sternotomy; the needle is at best a bridge while that is mobilised.
  • Aortic dissection with haemopericardium — relative, and genuinely dangerous. Decompressing can raise the blood pressure, re-establish flow through the false lumen, and precipitate catastrophic rupture. Drain only what restores a perfusing rhythm, and get to theatre.
  • Uncorrected severe coagulopathy — relative; correct alongside, not instead.
03

Equipment

  • Ultrasound with a phased-array cardiac probe — this is the single item that changes the procedure's safety profile, and it should be considered standard rather than optional
  • Long needle: 18 ga spinal needle (~9–15 cm), or a dedicated pericardiocentesis kit
  • Guidewire, dilator and pigtail catheter — reaccumulation is the norm, not the exception
  • Three-way stopcock and a large syringe, plus a collection system
  • Agitated saline — 10 mL saline with a small air bubble, briskly exchanged between two syringes. This is how you confirm needle position (see below), and it costs nothing to prepare in advance.
  • Full monitoring, defibrillator to hand, and sterile prep/drapes
04

Choosing the Window

With ultrasound, the subxiphoid route is often the wrong answer

The rule with ultrasound is simple: scan all the windows and puncture wherever the fluid is largest, closest to the skin, and free of lung or liver in the path. The needle follows the fluid, not a memorised landmark.

  • Apical — frequently the best target. Needle enters just lateral to the apex beat, directed toward the right shoulder, running roughly parallel to the long axis of the heart. Risks the lingula and the left internal mammary if too medial.
  • Parasternal — a short, direct route to an anterior effusion. Enter immediately lateral to the sternal edge to avoid the internal mammary artery, which runs about 3–5 cm from the sternal border; a perpendicular puncture through the 5th intercostal space is short and often the safest of all when the fluid is anterior.
  • Subxiphoid — the traditional blind landmark, and the right choice when there is no ultrasound or the chest is inaccessible. Enter just left and inferior to the xiphoid at about 45° to the skin, aiming toward the left shoulder. It has the longest needle path of the three and traverses the liver — which is why it is no longer the automatic first choice when imaging is available.
05

Stepwise Technique

1
Position and scan before you prep
Sit the patient up 30–45° if tolerated — it pools the effusion dependently and brings it toward the anterior chest wall. Then scan subxiphoid, parasternal and apical and choose your window deliberately, noting depth to fluid and what lies in the path.
2
Prep, drape, and anaesthetise the whole track
Full sterile technique if there is any time at all. Infiltrate generously down to and including the parietal pericardium in an awake patient.
3
Advance under vision, aspirating continuously
Keep the needle tip in view — in-plane if your skill allows, otherwise watch for tissue movement and the tenting of the pericardium as you press against it. Aspirate the whole way in. Entry into the pericardial space is usually a distinct give followed by free return of fluid.
4
Confirm with agitated saline before you dilate
This is the step that prevents the disaster. Inject a few millilitres of agitated saline down the needle while watching on ultrasound. Bubbles appearing in the pericardial space, surrounding the heart confirm correct position. Bubbles appearing inside a cardiac chamber mean you are in the heart — withdraw, do not dilate, and reassess. Never railroad a dilator over a wire whose position you have not confirmed.
5
Aspirate to effect, not to dryness
Remove enough to restore perfusion — often only 30–100 mL transforms the haemodynamics, because tamponade physiology sits on the steep part of the pericardial pressure-volume curve. Chasing complete drainage through a needle adds risk without adding benefit.
6
Leave a pigtail over a wire
Seldinger the catheter in and secure it. Reaccumulation is expected; a drain converts a procedure you would otherwise repeat under worse conditions into a monitored one. Send fluid for the studies the clinical context demands.
7
Re-scan, and keep the definitive plan moving
Confirm the effusion has reduced and the chambers are filling. Then continue arranging whatever actually fixes the cause — a surgical window, thoracotomy, or treatment of the underlying disease. Pericardiocentesis buys time; it rarely cures.
Is this blood from the pericardium or from a ventricle?

Two checks, neither perfect. Pericardial blood is usually defibrinated and does not clot in the syringe, whereas fresh intracardiac blood clots — useful, but unreliable with very recent bleeding. Comparing the aspirate's haematocrit with a venous sample is slower but more objective. Agitated saline under ultrasound is better than either and gives an answer in seconds, which is why it belongs in the routine rather than the troubleshooting list.

Complications & Pitfalls

  • Cardiac chamber puncture or laceration — classically the right ventricle, which sits immediately anterior
  • Coronary artery laceration, with the haemopericardium it causes
  • Pneumothorax, and rarely hepatic injury via the subxiphoid route
  • Arrhythmia from myocardial irritation
  • Pericardial decompression syndrome — uncommon but well described: paradoxical ventricular dysfunction and pulmonary oedema after rapid drainage. An argument for draining to effect rather than to dryness
  • The falsely reassuring dry tap in traumatic tamponade — arguably the most dangerous outcome of the whole procedure, because it ends the search
  • Delay to definitive management while attention stays on the needle
08

Pearls

Puncture where the fluid is, not where the textbook says. With ultrasound the apical or parasternal window is frequently shorter and safer than subxiphoid.

Agitated saline before dilating. Seconds to perform, and it is the difference between a drain in the pericardium and a dilator in a ventricle.

Drain to effect. Thirty to a hundred millilitres often restores the blood pressure; chasing dryness adds only risk.

A dry tap after penetrating trauma means clot, not absence of tamponade. Escalate rather than relax.

Leave a pigtail. Reaccumulation is the rule.

Sit them up. Thirty to forty-five degrees brings the effusion to the needle.

If there is a surgeon and an operating room, a traumatic haemopericardium belongs to them — you are buying minutes, not providing treatment.
