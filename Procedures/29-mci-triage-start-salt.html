Mass Casualty Triage — START / SALT Execution

The initial sort when casualties outstrip resources. Two frameworks, one discipline: sort fast, intervene minimally, keep moving, reassess constantly.

01

When This Applies

  • Any incident where casualties exceed immediately available resources. That ratio, not a headcount, is what defines a mass casualty incident — four critical patients is an MCI for a two-ambulance service.
  • The initial sorting pass at the scene, before individualized care begins
  • Again, repeatedly, as resources arrive and patients change — triage is a process, not a single pass

START (Simple Triage and Rapid Treatment) remains the most widely used initial sort in US prehospital systems. SALT (Sort, Assess, Lifesaving interventions, Treatment/transport) is the national model recommended under the Model Uniform Core Criteria, adding a global voice sort and a short list of interventions performed during the sort. Both do the same job; use whichever your region has adopted.

The governing principle is uncomfortable and has to be stated plainly: the goal changes from doing everything possible for each patient to doing the most good for the greatest number. Resources go to the salvageable, which means some patients who would receive maximal effort on an ordinary day will not. Making that shift deliberately, and saying so out loud, is what separates triage from chaos.

Scene safety comes before any of this

Triage does not begin until the scene is safe. Secondary devices are deliberately timed for the arrival of responders; structural collapse continues; active threats move. Unstable structures, fire, traffic, hazardous materials and contamination all take precedence, and a contaminated scene requires decontamination before or alongside triage. A responder who becomes a casualty subtracts twice — one fewer rescuer and one more patient.

02

Equipment and Roles

  • Triage tags or tape — Red/Immediate, Yellow/Delayed, Green/Minor, Gray or Black for Expectant and Deceased. Tape is faster to apply than tags and easier to see; tags carry information. Know which your system uses.
  • Tourniquets — several, carried on the person doing the sort.
  • Basic airway adjuncts, occlusive dressings, and decompression needles.
  • Antidote auto-injectors where the incident involves nerve agents or cyanide.
  • Nothing else. No monitors, no IV starts, no detailed examination. Diagnostic equipment during the sort is a trap: it slows the pass and it does not change the category.

The roles, assigned explicitly and early: Incident command (owns the scene and the whole picture) · Triage officer (sorts and moves, and treats nobody) · Treatment officer (runs the casualty collection areas) · Transport officer (decides who goes where, and in what order) · Staging (holds and assigns incoming units).

The triage officer's job is to sort, not to treat. This is the hardest discipline in the whole framework and the one that fails most often — the pull to stop and fully manage the first critically injured person found is enormous, and giving in to it means the rest of the scene goes unsorted.

03

START — Adult

Under 60 seconds per patient. Respirations, perfusion, mental status — in that order, stopping at the first Red.

1
Clear the walking wounded first
Call out for anyone who can walk to move to a designated area. Tag them Green. This removes a large fraction of the scene from individual assessment in a single sentence — and it is why you say it before you touch anyone. Someone must go with them: Green patients are not unassessed forever, and occasionally one of them is deteriorating.
2
Respirations
Not breathing → open the airway with a single positioning maneuver. Breathing now → Red. Still not breathing → Black.
Rate above 30/min → Red. Below 30 → go on to perfusion.
3
Perfusion
No radial pulse (or capillary refill beyond 2 seconds) → control major hemorrhage and tag Red. Radial pulse present → go on to mental status. The radial pulse is the more reliable of the two in cold, dark, or dirty conditions.
4
Mental status
Cannot follow a simple command → Red. Can follow commands → Yellow.
5
Tag, say it out loud, and move on
The only interventions permitted during START are opening an airway and stopping catastrophic hemorrhage — both take seconds and both save lives that would otherwise be lost before the treatment phase. Anything further waits.

JumpSTART — for children, and the differences matter. A child's normal respiratory rate would be tagged Red by adult criteria, and children are far more likely to arrest from a primary respiratory cause and to respond to rescue breaths.

  • Respiratory rate: Red if below 15 or above 45 per minute — not the adult threshold of 30.
  • If apneic: check for a pulse. No pulse → Black. Pulse present → give 5 rescue breaths. If breathing resumes → Red. If not → Black. This rescue-breath step has no adult equivalent and is the heart of JumpSTART.
  • Mental status by AVPU: inappropriate response to pain, posturing, or unresponsive → Red.
  • Infants and children who cannot walk are carried to the Green area and assessed there — the walking sort does not work for them.
04

SALT

1
Sort — the global voice sort
Two instructions, shouted, before anyone is touched: “If you can hear me and can walk, come to [place].” Then, to those remaining: “If you can hear me, wave or move a limb.”
That produces three groups and their assessment order, in reverse: still or obviously in extremis → assessed first; waving or purposefully moving → second; walking → last. Most of the categorization work is done before a single hand is laid on anyone.
2
Assess, and perform lifesaving interventions as you go
Working in that priority order, assess each patient individually — and unlike START, perform the interventions during the pass rather than deferring them. The permitted list is deliberately short: open the airway (and in a child, two rescue breaths), control major hemorrhage with a tourniquet or packing, decompress a tension pneumothorax, and give auto-injector antidotes. Each takes seconds. Anything beyond belongs to the treatment phase.
3
Assign a category, after the intervention
Four questions, in order:
Breathing? No, after the airway is opened → Dead.
Obeys commands or makes purposeful movements? Has a peripheral pulse? Not in respiratory distress? Major hemorrhage controlled? — if any is a no → Immediate, provided survival is likely with the resources available; if survival is unlikely given those resources → Expectant.
All yes, with injuries → Delayed. All yes, minor injuries only → Minimal.
4
Treatment and transport — then re-triage
Move patients to collection areas by category, and reassess at every stage. Categories are a snapshot of the patient against the resources available at that moment, and both change.
05

The Expectant Category

The category people avoid using, and misusing it in either direction causes harm.

  • Expectant is not a synonym for dead, and it is not abandonment. It means: injuries unlikely to be survivable given the resources presently available, so those resources go elsewhere first.
  • It is explicitly resource-relative. The same patient is Immediate in a fully staffed trauma center and Expectant at a rural scene with two crews. As resources arrive, Expectant patients are reassessed and can be re-categorized upward. Build that reassessment into the plan rather than leaving it to conscience.
  • Comfort care is still care. Analgesia, a blanket, a place out of the rain, and somebody with them if anyone can be spared. Assign it when the surge subsides.
  • Name the decision out loud and share it with incident command. Individual responders should not be silently carrying it alone, and it is the part of an MCI that responders carry longest afterwards.

07

Afterwards

  • Account for everyone — tag numbers, destinations, and the Green area. Reunification and identification begin immediately and take far more effort than anyone expects.
  • Hand over triage categories and their reasoning to receiving facilities, and tell them the total scene picture — a hospital that knows forty more are coming behaves differently from one that does not.
  • Debrief the responders, properly and more than once. The expectant decisions are what people carry, and the effect is delayed. Make specific offers of support rather than general ones, and repeat them weeks later.
  • Review honestly. Over- and under-triage rates, time to first transport, distribution across hospitals, and where the process broke. Every MCI review finds the same three things — communications, tracking, and distribution — which means they are fixable in advance.
  • Drill it. Triage decisions are made under conditions that make deliberation impossible, so the algorithm has to be automatic. A card in a pocket is not a substitute for having done it.
08

Pearls

Sort, do not treat. The pull to stay with the first critical patient is the failure mode that costs the most lives, and knowing that in advance is the only defense.

The voice sort does most of the work. Two shouted sentences categorize most of a scene before you touch anyone.

Go to the quiet one. Noise means an airway and perfusion.

Over-triage is not the safe option. Tagging everyone Red degrades care for the people who actually need it.

Children need JumpSTART — different rates, and rescue breaths before you call an apneic child Black.

Only four interventions during the sort: airway, hemorrhage, decompression, antidote. Nothing else.

Expectant is resource-relative and reversible. Reassess when help arrives, and give comfort care when you can spare it.

Don't send everyone to the nearest hospital — the walking wounded have already filled it.

Say your tag out loud. It gives command a running count and catches your own errors as you go.

Re-triage. One pass is not triage; it is the first pass.

Scene safety first. Secondary devices target responders.
