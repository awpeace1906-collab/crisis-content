Active Internal Rewarming (ECMO/CPB Crossover)

Core rewarming for severe hypothermia with cardiac instability — where extracorporeal circulation becomes both the rewarming method and the resuscitation.

01

Indications

  • Hypothermic cardiac arrest — extracorporeal rewarming is the definitive management, and outcomes here are unlike any other arrest population
  • Severe hypothermia (below about 28 °C) with cardiac instability — ventricular arrhythmia, profound bradycardia, or hypotension unresponsive to volume
  • Hypothermia with a core temperature that will not rise despite adequate external and minimally invasive rewarming
  • Hypothermia complicated by a condition requiring circulatory support in its own right
Why this population is different

Cooling that occurs before hypoxia is profoundly neuroprotective: cerebral oxygen consumption falls roughly 6–8% per degree, and intact neurologic survival has been documented after hours of CPR and core temperatures below 15 °C. Nobody is dead until they are warm and dead.

The critical qualifier is the order of events. A patient who cooled first and then arrested has an excellent prognosis. A patient who was asphyxiated first — a prolonged avalanche burial with an obstructed airway, or a warm-water drowning — and cooled afterwards does not. That sequence, not the temperature, is what the prognosis turns on.

Staging, and Measuring the Temperature Properly

StageClinical pictureCore temp (approx.)
HT IConscious, shivering35–32 °C
HT IIImpaired consciousness, shivering has stopped32–28 °C
HT IIIUnconscious, vital signs present but may be barely detectable28–24 °C
HT IVNo detectable vital signs — apparent deathBelow ~24 °C

Take the pulse for a full minute, with ECG and ideally ultrasound. A profoundly hypothermic patient may have a rate of 10 with a barely palpable pulse, and being wrongly declared pulseless leads to chest compressions on a perfusing, irritable myocardium.

Measure the core temperature correctly, because everything depends on it.

  • Esophageal probe in the lower third of the esophagus — the standard in an intubated patient. Accurate and responsive.
  • Epitympanic probe with the ear canal insulated — acceptable in a non-intubated patient, and only if there is no snow or water in the canal and a pulse is present.
  • Rectal and bladder lag substantially, and lag most during active rewarming — exactly when you need accuracy.
  • Infrared tympanic thermometers are not adequate, and most standard clinical thermometers do not read low enough to be useful at all.
03

Handling — Rescue Collapse and Afterdrop

Rescue collapse: arrest during or shortly after extrication. The cold myocardium is exquisitely irritable and will fibrillate with mechanical stimulation, and standing a patient up removes the hydrostatic support their vasoconstricted, volume-depleted circulation depends on.

  • Keep them horizontal throughout extrication and transport.
  • Move them as little and as gently as possible. No rough handling, no sitting up, no unnecessary transfers.
  • Remove wet clothing by cutting, and insulate with a vapor barrier plus insulation.

Afterdrop: the core temperature continues to fall after rescue, as cold peripheral blood returns centrally and heat continues to equilibrate inward. Do not massage or actively warm the limbs; direct external warming at the trunk — axillae, chest, groin, neck. Keep the periphery still.

04

Resuscitating a Cold Heart

Below roughly 30 °C the myocardium responds poorly to both electricity and drugs, and drugs given are not metabolized — they accumulate and then act all at once as the patient warms.

  • Defibrillation: attempt up to three shocks. If unsuccessful, defer further attempts until the core is above 30 °C. Below that, repeated shocks achieve nothing.
  • Drugs: European and mountain-medicine guidance is to withhold epinephrine and other ACLS drugs until above 30 °C, then double the dosing interval between 30 and 35 °C, returning to standard intervals above 35 °C. American guidance is more permissive, allowing that drugs may be reasonable to consider. The reason for caution is accumulation, not futility — know which protocol your service follows.
  • Compressions continue. The chest is stiff; a mechanical device is a substantial advantage over a long transport.
  • Where continuous CPR is genuinely impossible — a technical rescue, a long carry off a mountain — guidelines permit intermittent CPR: below 28 °C, alternating 5 minutes of CPR with pauses of no more than 5 minutes; below 20 °C, pauses of up to 10 minutes. This is an explicit concession to terrain, not a general license to interrupt.
  • Intubate if indicated. Fear of triggering fibrillation should not prevent airway management; careful technique is enough.
  • They are profoundly volume depleted from cold diuresis. Give warmed crystalloid generously.
05

Who Goes on a Circuit — and Where

  • Serum potassium is the single most useful triage marker. A markedly elevated potassium indicates cell lysis that preceded the cooling — that is, death before hypothermia. Thresholds vary: above 12 mmol/L is widely used as indicating non-survivability, with a lower threshold around 8 mmol/L applied after avalanche burial. Confirm the figure your regional protocol uses.
  • The HOPE score is the modern validated tool estimating survival probability with extracorporeal rewarming, and it has largely replaced potassium alone as the decision instrument where it is available.
  • Asphyxia before cooling — airway obstruction in an avalanche burial beyond about 60 minutes without an air pocket, or submersion in non-icy water — carries a poor prognosis regardless of temperature.
  • Obvious lethal injury, a chest that will not compress, or ice within the airway are the findings that justify not resuscitating. Rigidity and fixed dilated pupils are not — both are expected in deep hypothermia.
  • Take them to a center with extracorporeal capability, bypassing closer hospitals. This is a declared bypass decision in the same category as trauma or STEMI, and it needs to be made prehospital, by protocol, rather than discovered after arrival at a hospital that cannot help.
  • Call ahead early. The receiving team needs to assemble a cannulation team and prime a circuit before the patient arrives.

07

Extracorporeal Rewarming

Cannulation follows the standard peripheral VA pathway — see the ECMO cannulation entry for technique, sizing, wire confirmation, and the distal perfusion catheter. What differs here is the intent and the pacing.

  • VA-ECMO is generally preferred over cardiopulmonary bypass: it needs less anticoagulation, and crucially it can continue supporting the circulation after rewarming is complete — which matters, because a rewarmed myocardium is often stunned and not immediately able to sustain an output.
  • Rewarm gradually. Keep the blood-to-core gradient modest — commonly no more than about 10 °C — and many centers now deliberately rewarm slowly, on the order of 1–2 °C per hour after arrest, to limit reperfusion injury rather than racing to normothermia.
  • Re-attempt defibrillation and standard drugs as thresholds are crossed — a rhythm that was refractory at 26 °C often converts readily at 30.
  • Expect enormous volume requirements as the periphery vasodilates. Hypotension on rewarming is anticipated, not a surprise.
  • Consider deliberate normothermia rather than overshoot — these patients have had a cardiac arrest, and the post-arrest fever-avoidance principles apply once they are warm.

Complications

  • Rescue collapse and ventricular fibrillation from handling a cold, irritable myocardium.
  • Afterdrop and continued cooling after rescue.
  • Rewarming shock — vasodilation outpacing a profoundly volume-depleted circulation.
  • Hyperkalemia and arrhythmia during reperfusion.
  • Rhabdomyolysis and acute kidney injury.
  • Coagulopathy and DIC — hypothermia impairs both platelet function and enzyme kinetics, and laboratory clotting studies run at 37 °C look deceptively normal.
  • Pulmonary edema and ARDS.
  • Pancreatitis — classically described after severe hypothermia and easily missed.
  • Frostbite of the extremities, needing its own management once the core is safe.
  • All the complications of extracorporeal support — bleeding, limb ischemia, and the rest.
10

Pearls

Nobody is dead until they are warm and dead — unless they were asphyxiated first. That sequence is the prognosis.

Feel for a pulse for a full minute. A rate of 10 is easy to miss and compressions on a perfusing cold heart cause fibrillation.

Esophageal probe. Rectal and bladder lag most during rewarming, which is when you are relying on them.

Horizontal and gentle. Rescue collapse is a handling complication.

Warm the trunk, never the limbs.

Three shocks, then stop until 30 °C. And be cautious with drugs that will not be cleared.

Fixed pupils and rigidity mean nothing while cold.

Potassium is your triage test, and the HOPE score where you have it.

Bypass to a center with a circuit, and call them before you leave the scene.

No circuit? Two chest tubes. Thoracic lavage is far better than the alternatives and available almost anywhere.
