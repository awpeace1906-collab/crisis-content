Finger Thoracostomy / Needle Decompression

Immediate decompression of tension pneumothorax — the bridge to a formal chest tube, not a replacement for one.

THORACIC

01

Indications

  • Tension pneumothorax — hypotension with unilateral absent or reduced breath sounds, respiratory distress, distended neck veins, and rising airway pressures in a ventilated patient
  • Traumatic cardiac arrest — bilateral finger thoracostomies are a standard component of the algorithm, performed for every traumatic arrest regardless of examination findings
  • Any decompensating patient where tension physiology is a credible explanation and you cannot afford to wait for imaging
Tracheal deviation is a late and unreliable sign

Waiting for the textbook triad means waiting too long. Distended neck veins are frequently absent in a hypovolaemic trauma patient — there is no volume to distend them. In a ventilated patient the earliest reliable signal is often a sudden rise in peak airway pressure with falling blood pressure. Treat the physiology you have, not the sign you were taught to look for.

02

Contraindications

  • None in a genuine tension pneumothorax with haemodynamic compromise. The risk of inaction dominates.
  • The real hazard is not a contraindication but a misdiagnosis — decompressing a chest that was never under tension, when the hypotension was haemorrhage, tamponade or a right mainstem intubation.
03

Equipment

Needle decompression

  • Large-bore cannula, and length is the whole problem. A standard 5 cm catheter fails to reach the pleural space in a substantial proportion of adults — chest wall thickness at the 2nd intercostal space commonly exceeds it. An 8 cm cannula is what most current trauma guidance recommends. Check what is actually on your trolley.
  • A syringe, so you can aspirate rather than rely on hearing a hiss in a noisy resuscitation room

Finger thoracostomy

  • Scalpel and a large blunt clamp — that is genuinely all you need
  • Gloves, and whatever prep the situation permits; in an arrest, do not trade time for sterility you cannot achieve anyway
  • A chest tube and drainage system ready to follow, because this is not the endpoint
04

Choosing the Site

The classic teaching site is the one most likely to fail

2nd intercostal space, midclavicular line — the traditional landmark. Problems: the chest wall is thickest here, so a short cannula frequently does not reach the pleura; and going too medial approaches the internal mammary vessels and the subclavian.

4th–5th intercostal space, anterior axillary line — increasingly preferred, and the same site as a chest tube. The chest wall is thinner, the success rate is better, it is where you will be putting the tube anyway, and it is the natural site for a finger thoracostomy.

Practical answer: if you are going lateral, you may as well cut. Finger thoracostomy at the lateral site is more reliable than a needle at either site, and it does not depend on a cannula being long enough.

05

Stepwise Technique

Needle decompression — fastest, least reliable
1
Insert over the top of the rib, syringe attached
Perpendicular to the chest wall, immediately superior to the rib to avoid the neurovascular bundle. Advance while aspirating — a sudden free draw of air is more dependable confirmation than trying to hear a hiss in a working resuscitation bay.
2
Advance the cannula, withdraw the needle, secure
Slide the plastic cannula fully in and remove the needle. Secure it, and accept that it will kink, clot or dislodge — a soft cannula in a moving chest wall has a short working life.
3
Treat success as temporary and move on
Improvement buys minutes. Arrange the definitive tube immediately — and if there was no improvement, do not conclude the diagnosis was wrong until you have decompressed properly with a finger.
Finger thoracostomy — slower by seconds, far more reliable
4
Incise in the triangle of safety
Same site as a chest tube: 4th–5th intercostal space, anterior to the mid-axillary line, arm abducted. A 3 cm incision over the rib below your target space, parallel to the rib.
5
Blunt-dissect up and over the rib, then through the pleura
Spread with the clamp, tunnelling superiorly over the top of the rib, and push through the parietal pleura with deliberate, controlled force. Expect a rush of air under tension — though absence of a dramatic hiss does not mean you are in the wrong place.
6
Put your finger in and sweep
Confirm you are in the pleural space, feel for lung, and sweep for adhesions and for abdominal contents from an unrecognised diaphragmatic injury. In a traumatic arrest this sweep is also diagnostic — you may find a massive haemothorax that changes the entire resuscitation.
7
Leave it open, and follow with a tube
In a ventilated patient the open thoracostomy continues to decompress, and many services deliberately leave it open with a simple dressing during transfer rather than tubing immediately. In a spontaneously breathing patient an open hole can entrain air — place the tube promptly.
In traumatic arrest, do both sides

Bilateral finger thoracostomies are performed in traumatic cardiac arrest without waiting for examination findings, alongside haemorrhage control and volume. Auscultation is unreliable during CPR, and a unilateral decision based on breath sounds in that setting is a guess.

06

Did It Work?

  • Haemodynamic improvement — blood pressure rising within a minute or two is the most meaningful endpoint
  • Falling peak airway pressures in a ventilated patient
  • Free rush of air on entry — supportive, but its absence proves nothing
  • Return of lung sliding on POCUS over the decompressed side
  • Your finger feeling lung — the most direct confirmation available
No improvement does not mean no tension pneumothorax

It commonly means the needle was too short, kinked, blocked, or in the wrong place. Before you discard the diagnosis, decompress properly with a finger. And if a correctly performed bilateral finger thoracostomy genuinely changes nothing, the diagnosis was something else — go back to haemorrhage, tamponade, and the rest of the arrest algorithm.

Complications & Pitfalls

  • Failed decompression from an inadequate needle — the dominant failure mode, and entirely a matter of equipment and site selection
  • Lung laceration from a needle inserted into a chest that was not under tension
  • Vascular injury — internal mammary or subclavian if too medial at the 2nd space; intercostal vessels if dissecting along the wrong rib margin
  • Entry into the abdomen through an unrecognised diaphragmatic injury
  • Creating a pneumothorax in a patient who did not have one
  • Treating decompression as definitive — it never is
  • Infection in the survivor, from a necessarily unsterile field
09

Pearls

If you are going lateral, cut. A finger is more reliable than any cannula, and you are already at the site the tube needs.

Length is the whole argument for needles. A 5 cm cannula frequently does not reach the pleura in an adult chest; carry 8 cm.

Aspirate rather than listen. A syringe gives you an answer in a room too noisy for a hiss.

Tracheal deviation and distended neck veins are late and often absent — especially in the hypovolaemic. Rising airway pressure with falling pressure is the earlier tell.

Bilateral, in traumatic arrest, without debate.

The finger sweep is diagnostic as well as therapeutic. Blood, bowel or adhesions all change what happens next.

Nothing here is definitive. The tube always follows.
