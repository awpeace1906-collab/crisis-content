Finger Thoracostomy / Needle Decompression

Immediate decompression of tension pneumothorax — the bridge to a formal chest tube, not a replacement for one.

THORACIC
⚡ TECHNIQUE Indications Contraindications Equipment Complications & Pitfalls Pearls References
01

Indications

  • Clinically diagnosed tension pneumothorax: hypotension, absent/decreased breath sounds unilaterally, tracheal deviation (late finding), distended neck veins, respiratory distress in a trauma or critically ill patient
  • Diagnosis is clinical — do not delay for imaging in a decompensating patient with a compatible presentation
02

Contraindications

  • None absolute in a true tension pneumothorax with hemodynamic compromise — the risk of inaction outweighs procedural risk
  • Misdiagnosis (simple pneumothorax without tension physiology) is the real risk to guard against, not a formal contraindication
03

Equipment

Needle decompression

  • Large-bore needle/angiocath (10-14 ga, adequate length for chest wall thickness — standard needles are frequently too short in larger patients)

Finger thoracostomy

  • Scalpel, Kelly clamp or equivalent for blunt dissection
  • Gloves, basic sterile prep if time allows (do not delay for this in a decompensating patient)
04

Stepwise Technique

Needle decompression
1
Identify the insertion site — traditionally 2nd intercostal space, midclavicular line, though 4th/5th intercostal space, anterior axillary line (same site as finger thoracostomy) is increasingly preferred given shorter chest wall distance and lower vascular injury risk.
2
Insert the needle perpendicular to the chest wall, just above the rib margin, advancing until a rush of air or loss of resistance is felt.
3
Listen/feel for the hallmark rush of air confirming tension physiology and successful decompression.
4
Secure the catheter in place; this is a temporizing measure — a formal chest tube (tube thoracostomy) is required promptly after.
Finger thoracostomy
5
Identify the same site as tube thoracostomy — 4th or 5th intercostal space, anterior to mid-axillary line.
6
Make an incision through skin and subcutaneous tissue with a scalpel.
7
Bluntly dissect with a Kelly clamp to the pleura, staying superior to the rib to avoid the neurovascular bundle.
8
Puncture through the pleura with the clamp or a finger, feeling for the rush of air.
9
Sweep a finger into the pleural space to confirm decompression and clear any adhesions.
10
Leave the tract open or pack loosely until a formal chest tube can be placed — this technique can serve as decompression alone or as the first step of a full tube thoracostomy.
This is a clinical diagnosis and a clinical decompression

Don't wait for a chest X-ray in a patient who is actively decompensating — a needle that's too short to reach the pleural space is a common, preventable cause of a failed decompression in larger patients.

05

Complications & Pitfalls

  • Needle too short to reach the pleural space in patients with a thick chest wall — a common cause of failed decompression
  • Vascular or lung injury from an overly medial or poorly angled needle insertion
  • False negative (no rush of air) does not rule out tension pneumothorax — reassess clinically and consider finger thoracostomy or repeat attempt at an alternate site
  • Needle decompression alone is temporizing only — failure to follow with a formal chest tube risks recurrence
06

Pearls

Finger thoracostomy at the 4th/5th intercostal space, anterior axillary line site is increasingly favored over the classic 2nd intercostal space needle site — shorter distance to the pleura, more reliable decompression in larger patients.

This is a clinical diagnosis and a clinical decompression — don't wait for a chest X-ray in a patient who is actively decompensating.

Always follow decompression with a formal chest tube; neither needle decompression nor finger thoracostomy alone is definitive management.
