Escharotomy

Releasing the constricting, unyielding burn eschar before it compromises circulation or ventilation.

01

Indications

Full-thickness eschar is inelastic. Resuscitation edema underneath it turns the burn into a tourniquet.

  • Circumferential or near-circumferential full-thickness limb burn with developing vascular compromise — rising compartment pressure beneath a skin envelope that cannot expand
  • Circumferential chest or torso burn restricting ventilation — falling chest wall compliance, rising peak airway pressures, falling tidal volumes on pressure-controlled ventilation, worsening hypercapnia
  • Circumferential neck burn with airway compromise or venous congestion of the face
  • Abdominal compartment syndrome from circumferential abdominal eschar, usually on a background of large-volume resuscitation

Timing is the thing most often misunderstood. The need for escharotomy usually develops 6 to 24 hours after the burn, as resuscitation fluid accumulates in the tissue under a rigid eschar. A limb that is warm and well perfused on arrival can be threatened by morning. This is a serially reassessed decision, not a one-time assessment at presentation — and the corollary is that a patient with circumferential burns needs an hourly perfusion check written down, not a single note on admission.

Absent pulses are a late sign — do not wait for them

By the time a radial pulse disappears, muscle has been ischemic for some time. The earlier and more useful signals are loss of the Doppler signal in the palmar arch or digital vessels (not just the named artery), a falling or lost pulse oximetry waveform on the affected digit, progressive pain, paresthesia, and tense, woody swelling distal to the burn. Compartment pressures above about 30 mmHg support the decision where the picture is unclear. Assess with a Doppler and a pulse oximeter, hourly, and act on the trend.

02

Contraindications — and What This Is Not

  • None absolute where there is genuine vascular or ventilatory compromise from constricting eschar.
  • Partial-thickness burns without circumferential involvement. Superficial and superficial-partial burns retain elasticity and do not constrict. Cutting them creates a painful, bleeding, full-thickness wound in a patient who did not have one.
  • Distal ischemia with another explanation — arterial injury, a tight dressing, positioning, hypotension, or hypothermic vasoconstriction. Take the dressings off and look before you reach for a blade.

Escharotomy is not fasciotomy. Escharotomy divides constricting skin and stops at the subcutaneous fat. Fasciotomy opens muscle compartments. They address different pressures at different depths, and the mistake goes in both directions:

  • Cutting too deep in a simple thermal burn turns a clean release into a bleeding fasciotomy the patient did not need.
  • Stopping at escharotomy when the muscle itself is the problem leaves the limb ischemic. High-voltage electrical injury, crush, and deep thermal burns involving muscle need fasciotomy, and escharotomy alone will not save them.

If perfusion does not improve after an adequate escharotomy, measure compartment pressures. That is the branch point.

03

Equipment

  • Electrocautery, if available — strongly preferred over a scalpel. These incisions bleed from viable dermis and fat at the base, and the patient is frequently coagulopathic. Cautery cuts and controls in one pass.
  • Scalpel (a No. 10 or 20 blade) as the alternative, plus hemostats and ties.
  • Doppler probe — for assessing before, and for proving the release worked afterward. Have it before you start, not after.
  • Pulse oximeter to put on a distal digit.
  • Skin marker — mark the lines before cutting. See §04; the landmarks that matter are the ones you cannot see through eschar.
  • Analgesia and sedation. The eschar is insensate; the patient is not. See the warning in §05.
  • Hemostatic dressings, bulky dressings, and a way to elevate the limb.
  • A phone. Burn centers expect and welcome a call before an escharotomy, and most will talk you through the lines in real time. This is one of the few genuinely emergent burn procedures and also one where remote guidance is easy and routine.
04

Where the Lines Go — and the Two Nerves

Mid-medial and mid-lateral, full length. Two places deviate, and both are permanent if you get them wrong.

RegionIncisionsThe hazard
Arm and forearmMid-medial and mid-lateral, from proximal to distal end of the burnUlnar nerve at the elbow. The medial line must pass anterior to the medial epicondyle — the nerve sits in the cubital tunnel just behind it, immediately subcutaneous.
HandDorsal longitudinal incisions over the intermetacarpal spaces (typically 2nd and 4th)Extensor tendons. Stay in the valleys between metacarpals, not over the tendons.
DigitsMid-axial, on the side that is not the working pinch surface (ulnar side of the thumb, radial side of the little finger)Digital neurovascular bundles. Value is debated — discuss with a burn center before cutting fingers.
Thigh and legMid-medial and mid-lateral, full length of the burnCommon peroneal nerve at the fibular neck. It wraps the neck just distal to the fibular head and is subcutaneous there. FootDorsal medial and lateralDorsalis pedis and the extensor tendons.

AbdomenExtend the torso pattern inferiorly; a midline or transverse release as neededConsider true abdominal compartment syndrome — escharotomy may not be enough.

Cross the joints. An incision that stops at the wrist or the ankle because the anatomy looks intimidating leaves the constriction exactly where it is tightest. The incision runs the entire length of the circumferential burn, through normal-appearing skin at each end by 1–2 cm. Incomplete length is the single commonest reason an escharotomy fails to restore perfusion.

Mark before you cut. Eschar obliterates the surface landmarks you would normally navigate by. Palpate the medial epicondyle and the fibular head through the burn, mark them, and draw the line around them.

05

Technique

1
Document the deficit first
Record Doppler signals at the palmar arch or digital vessels, the pulse oximetry waveform, capillary refill, and sensation — before you cut. Without a baseline you cannot tell afterwards whether the release worked, and “it looks better” is not a finding you can hand over.
2
Give real analgesia and sedation
Opioid plus ketamine, or formal procedural sedation. See the warning below — the widely repeated line that escharotomy needs no anesthesia is half-true and causes real suffering.
3
Position and mark
Limb in anatomic position, slightly extended. Palpate and mark the medial epicondyle or fibular head, then draw the full line including where it curves around them and where it extends into unburned skin at each end.
4
Cut through the eschar into fat — and stop
Full thickness of the burned skin, down to and just into the subcutaneous fat. Do not cut the fascia. The endpoint is visible and unmistakable: the wound edges spring apart by a centimeter or more and the fat bulges out of the incision. If the edges sit together, you are not through.
5
Expect bleeding — and use it
The base of a correct incision is viable tissue and will bleed. That bleeding is a useful confirmation of depth, not a sign you have gone too far. Control it as you go with cautery. A completely bloodless incision means either that you are still within dead tissue, or that the tissue beneath is dead too — both change what happens next.
6
Reassess immediately, with the Doppler
Signals should return or improve within minutes. Compliance should improve at once on a chest release, with a visible fall in peak airway pressure. If nothing changes, the commonest reason is that the incision is not long enough — extend it before you conclude the diagnosis was wrong.
7
Second line if needed, then reassess again
A single mid-medial incision is often sufficient. If perfusion is still inadequate, add the mid-lateral line. Only after both lines are complete and full-length should you move on to considering compartment pressures and fasciotomy.
8
Dress, elevate, and set a reassessment interval
Hemostatic or bulky absorbent dressing, limb elevated above heart level. Then hourly Doppler and perfusion checks, documented — edema continues to accumulate for another day and a released limb can constrict again.
“The eschar is insensate” is only half the story

Full-thickness burn is anesthetic, so the middle of the incision may be painless. But the incision must extend into viable skin at both ends, it passes through sensate deep dermis wherever the burn is not truly full thickness, and the patient beneath it is awake, frightened, and in pain from the burn already. Give analgesia and sedation as a default. The bedside myth that escharotomy needs none has produced a great deal of avoidable suffering.

07

Complications

  • Hemorrhage — from viable dermis and fat, and worse in the coagulopathic large-burn patient. The reason cautery is preferred.
  • Nerve injury — ulnar at the medial epicondyle, common peroneal at the fibular neck, digital nerves in the fingers. All permanent, all avoidable by marking.
  • Incomplete release with continuing ischemia — the commonest and most consequential technical failure.
  • Cutting too deep, converting an escharotomy into an unintended fasciotomy with its attendant morbidity.
  • Escharotomy performed on a partial-thickness burn — a full-thickness wound created where none existed, which now needs grafting.
  • Infection — the incision opens a deep plane in a burn wound that will colonize.
  • Conversion of burn depth from desiccation of the exposed wound bed.
  • Scarring and contracture, particularly across joints, later requiring reconstruction.
  • Missed abdominal compartment syndrome, treated as a chest wall problem.
08

Afterwards — and the Resuscitation Behind It

  • Hourly documented perfusion checks — Doppler signal, oximetry waveform, capillary refill, sensation, and compartment softness. Edema peaks well after the procedure.
  • Elevate the limb above heart level and keep it there.
  • Transfer to a burn center. Escharotomy is a temporizing procedure; the patient needs burn surgical care. Call early — before the procedure if time allows.
  • Revisit the fluid plan. Resuscitation edema is what created the constriction. Standard resuscitation is roughly 2–4 mL/kg per %TBSA of crystalloid over the first 24 hours, half in the first 8 hours from the time of injury, titrated to urine output — about 0.5 mL/kg/h in adults and 1 mL/kg/h in children. Formulas are a starting point, not a prescription. Over-resuscitation drives escharotomy, abdominal compartment syndrome, and conversion of burn depth, and it is a far more common error than under-resuscitation in modern practice.
  • Watch for rhabdomyolysis and hyperkalemia, especially after electrical injury or a prolonged ischemic interval.
  • Tetanus and burn-center-directed wound care. Prophylactic systemic antibiotics are not routine.
  • Document the incisions — where, how long, what improved, and the Doppler findings before and after. The next team needs to know whether this has already been done adequately.
09

Pearls

Not long enough is the usual failure. Full length of the burn, across the joints, into normal skin at both ends.

The endpoint is the edges springing apart and fat bulging. If they sit together, you are not through the eschar.

Absent pulses are late. Use a Doppler on the palmar arch and a pulse oximeter on a digit, hourly, and act on the trend.

Mark the medial epicondyle and the fibular head first. Two nerves, two permanent deficits, both avoidable with a pen.

Bleeding is confirmation. A bloodless incision is telling you something, and it is not good news.

Escharotomy is not fasciotomy. Skin, into fat, stop. If perfusion does not return after adequate release, measure pressures — and think about electrical and crush injury.

Give analgesia. The eschar is insensate; the ends of the incision and the patient are not.

This need develops overnight. Circumferential burns get an hourly documented check, not a single admission assessment.

Call the burn center before you cut if there is any time at all. They do this often and will walk you through the lines.

Repeated escharotomies are a message about the fluids.
