Central Line Placement Under Arrest Conditions

Femoral-first access during active resuscitation — landmark or ultrasound-guided, without interrupting compressions.

01

Indications

  • Cardiac arrest or peri-arrest where peripheral and intraosseous access are inadequate, failed, or unavailable
  • Vasopressor infusion that will run for hours, at doses or concentrations you do not want in a peripheral vein
  • Volume — but only if you place a large-bore introducer sheath. A triple-lumen is worse than a good peripheral for flow (see §03)
  • Anticipated ECPR, transvenous pacing, or a pulmonary artery catheter — access you will need regardless of how this code ends
  • Post-ROSC, when the line placed under compressions needs to become the line you can keep

What it is not indicated for: getting drugs in faster than an IO. An intraosseous line is already running, already works for every arrest drug, and does not require someone to stop resuscitating and start a sterile procedure. Central access during arrest is for the things IO cannot do — sustained pressors, large-bore volume, a pacing wire, a monitoring column.

Below the diaphragm, drugs take longer

A standard 20 cm femoral catheter sits in the iliac vein or low IVC. During CPR, forward flow from below the diaphragm is poor and drug transit to the central circulation is slower than from an upper-body line or a proximal humeral IO. It still works — but every drug gets a 20 mL flush and the leg gets raised. If you have a choice of sites and no reason to avoid the chest, an upper-body line delivers faster.

02

Contraindications

  • A groin you are going to need for ECMO. If ECPR is on the table — even as a maybe — do not put a resuscitation line in the groin the cannulation team wants. Right femoral vessels are conventionally preferred for VA-ECMO cannulation. Use the left groin, or go above the diaphragm. A triple-lumen in the right femoral vein at minute 12 can cost the cannulation at minute 25, and nobody will remember to tell the ECMO team it is there.
  • Vascular injury, graft, or known thrombus at the intended site. A femoral line above a pelvic fracture or an IVC injury delivers drug into the retroperitoneum.
  • Infection or burn over the site — choose another.
  • Subclavian in severe coagulopathy — the site is not compressible. In a coagulopathic bleeding patient, femoral or IJ are the compressible options.
  • Coagulopathy generally is relative, not absolute, in arrest. So is the absence of full sterile barrier. Both are reasons to plan an exchange later, not reasons to withhold access now.
03

Which Catheter — and Why It Is Usually the Wrong One

The kit you grab determines what the line can do. This decision is made before you scrub, and it is frequently made wrong.

CatheterGravity flow (approx.)Use it for
7 Fr triple-lumen, 20 cm~25–50 mL/min through the distal lumenMultiple simultaneous infusions, pressors, monitoring. Not volume.
8.5 Fr introducer sheath (“Cordis”), 10 cm~250–500 mL/minHemorrhage and volume. Also the conduit for a transvenous pacing wire or a PA catheter.
16 G peripheral, 1.25 in~200 mL/minReference point — it beats a triple-lumen, because flow falls with catheter length and rises with the fourth power of radius.

Everything else you need:

  • Ultrasound, linear probe, sterile sheath and gel. In arrest this is not an optional refinement — it is the only reliable way to tell artery from vein when there is no pulse and no color difference. Prepare it while you prep the skin, not after.
  • Chlorhexidine, full barrier drape, gown and gloves — use what the situation allows, and document what it did not.
  • Seldinger components: introducer needle, guidewire, scalpel, dilator, catheter, suture or securement device.
  • Saline flushes for every lumen, and a primed extension set.
  • A pressure transducer or a length of sterile IV tubing (for the manometry check — §06).

Choosing the Site Under Compressions

The practical rule: during active compressions, femoral — unless ECPR is a possibility, in which case femoral on the left, or subclavian. After ROSC, when the room calms and the bed is yours, the decision reverts to the usual one.

Say out loud which groin you are taking. Two operators independently deciding “femoral” and arriving at the same groin, or the ECMO team arriving to find both groins used, are avoidable and entirely typical failures.

05

Femoral Technique During Active CPR

1
Claim the space and set up on the correct side
Announce it: “I am taking the left femoral vein.” Sit or kneel at the groin, outside the compressor's arc. Have the whole kit open and the wire loosened from its dispenser before you start — you will not be able to fish for components one-handed with a needle in a vein that is moving with every compression.
2
Find the inguinal ligament, not the skin crease
Draw the line from the anterior superior iliac spine to the pubic tubercle — that is the inguinal ligament. Puncture 1–2 cm below it. In an obese patient the skin crease can sit several centimeters distal to the ligament and using it as the landmark puts you too low, in the branching femoral vessels. Puncturing above the ligament is worse: that bleed is retroperitoneal, not compressible, and silent.
3
Put the probe on — short axis first
Lateral to medial the structures run NAVEL: Nerve, Artery, Vein, Empty space, Lymphatics. The vein is medial to the artery. On ultrasound, the artery is round, thick-walled and resists compression; the vein is more oval and collapses. Be warned: in arrest, an empty artery compresses too, and during compressions the vein can transmit pulsation. Do not rely on pulsatility or on blood color — deoxygenated arterial blood in arrest is dark. Use position and wall thickness, and then use the wire (step 7).
4
Prep and drape as fully as the clock allows
Chlorhexidine, let it dry if you can, full drape if someone can hand it to you. If a step gets skipped, skip it deliberately and say so — it goes in the note and it sets up the exchange decision later.
5
Needle in at 30–45°, aspirating continuously
Aim just medial to the artery, along the vessel's axis, advancing under constant negative pressure on the syringe. Compressions do not stop. Expect the target to move a centimeter with each one — advance between compressions if you can time it, and accept that this is harder than an elective line. Flash is dark and often non-pulsatile even from an artery, because there is no pulse.
6
Drop the angle, thread the wire, and never let go of it
Flatten the needle toward the skin before feeding the wire so it turns up the vessel rather than through the back wall. The wire should pass with no resistance at all. A hand is on the wire at every instant from the moment it leaves the dispenser until it is withdrawn — a wire released during chest compressions embolizes. Do not advance more than about 20 cm.
7
See the wire in the vein. This is the step that prevents the disaster.
Turn the probe to long axis over the vein and identify the echogenic wire lying inside it. If you cannot see the wire in the vein, you do not have a venous wire. Slide the probe proximally and confirm it stays in the venous channel. Dilation is the point of no return — ten seconds of ultrasound before the dilator is the cheapest insurance in the procedure.
8
Nick and dilate — shallow
Scalpel blade against the wire, cutting away from the wire, just through dermis. Then pass the dilator over the wire only far enough to open the tract — typically 2–3 cm. The dilator is a tissue dilator, not a vessel dilator; burying it perforates the posterior wall.
9
Catheter over wire — wire out the hub first
Thread the catheter until the wire emerges from the distal hub, then advance it into the vessel. Femoral catheters sit at roughly 20–25 cm at the skin. Remove the wire and confirm it is intact and whole when it is out.
10
Aspirate every lumen, flush every lumen, secure
Every port must draw blood back and flush freely. Suture or use the securement device — a line that is only taped will be removed by the transfer to CT. Then announce it: “Left femoral central line is in and working.”

Confirming It Is Venous — When Every Normal Clue Has Failed

In arrest, all three of the usual reassurances are unreliable at once.

  • Pulsatility: useless. There is no pulse — and compressions make everything pulsatile.
  • Color: useless. Arterial blood in arrest is desaturated and dark.
  • Ease of flow / low resistance: useless. There is no arterial pressure to push back.

What actually works, in order of usefulness during CPR:

  • 1. Ultrasound of the wire, long axis. The definitive bedside test, available before dilation, and the only one that is fully reliable during compressions. Make it a habit, not a tiebreaker.
  • 2. Transduce the needle or catheter. Connect to a pressure transducer and read the waveform. Post-ROSC this is decisive. During compressions it is muddied — compression-generated arterial waveforms can be substantial — but a clearly arterial trace is still an answer.
  • 3. Manometry. Attach sterile IV tubing to the needle hub, fill it, and hold it vertically. A venous column rises a few centimeters and stops; an arterial column climbs and swings. Interpretable post-ROSC; confounded during compressions.
  • 4. Blood gas from the catheter. Slow, and in arrest the arteriovenous difference narrows — a last resort, not a plan.
  • 5. Post-procedure imaging. Chest film for upper-body lines to confirm tip position and exclude pneumothorax. It answers a question you needed answered twenty minutes earlier — useful for documentation and tip position, not for preventing arterial dilation.
If you dilated an artery — leave the catheter in

A large-bore catheter that has been dilated into the femoral or subclavian artery is tamponading its own hole. Pulling it produces uncontrolled arterial bleeding, and in the subclavian position that bleeding is into a space you cannot compress. Leave it in place, leave it clamped, and call vascular surgery or interventional radiology now. This instinct — not to pull — is counterintuitive and has to be pre-loaded, because in the moment every reflex says remove it.

Complications

  • Arterial puncture and arterial cannulation — the defining risk of this procedure in arrest, because every normal discriminator fails simultaneously. Needle punctures compress; dilated catheters do not come out at the bedside.
  • Retroperitoneal hemorrhage from a femoral puncture above the inguinal ligament. Not compressible, not visible, and presents as unexplained shock after an apparently uneventful line.
  • Guidewire embolization — rare, entirely preventable, and specifically more likely here because compressions jostle the operator's hands.
  • Pneumothorax and hemothorax — subclavian and IJ. In a ventilated patient a simple pneumothorax can tension quickly.
  • Air embolism — upper-body lines, especially on chest recoil. Keep the hub occluded.
  • Vessel perforation and cardiac tamponade — from over-advancing a dilator or a stiff wire.
  • Arrhythmia from a wire in the right atrium or ventricle. Irrelevant in arrest; genuinely dangerous peri-arrest. Limit the wire to ~20 cm.
  • Infection — elevated, because barrier precautions were compromised. Femoral is the highest-risk site. Plan the exchange; see §09.
  • Thrombosis — femoral lines in an immobile ICU patient.
09

After ROSC

  • Re-verify the line now that there is a pulse. Transduce it. Draw a gas from it if there is any doubt. A line that was “probably venous” during compressions gets a definitive answer within minutes of ROSC, because the answer is now easy to obtain.
  • Image it. Chest film for any upper-body line — tip at the cavoatrial junction, no pneumothorax. Femoral lines do not need a film for position but do need a documented function check.
  • Flag it as an emergent line, explicitly, in the chart and at handover. Write down which barrier precautions were and were not used. Lines placed without full sterile technique should be removed or replaced as soon as the patient is stable, generally within 24–48 hours — and the only way that happens is if the next team knows.
  • Reassess whether you still need it. The indication was arrest. If the patient now needs only maintenance fluids and a single infusion, the line's risk has outlived its purpose.
  • Tell the ECMO, IR, and surgical teams which vessels are occupied. Access planning downstream depends on it.
10

Pearls

See the wire before you dilate. If you take one thing: long-axis ultrasound confirming the wire inside the vein, every time, before the dilator. Everything else in arrest lies to you.

Protect the right groin if ECPR is possible. The line you place in minute 12 can cost the cannulation in minute 25.

If you dilated an artery, do not pull it. The catheter is plugging the hole. Clamp it, leave it, call vascular surgery.

Grab the right kit. A triple-lumen is not a volume line. If you need blood to move, that is an 8.5 Fr introducer — or a short 14 G peripheral, which beats the triple-lumen anyway.

Puncture below the inguinal ligament, judged from bone landmarks, not the skin crease. Above it, the bleed is retroperitoneal and silent.

The vein is empty, so hunt with the probe, not the needle. And watch for the flash on withdrawal — you transfix the back wall more often than you think.

A hand on the wire at all times. Compressions are shaking the field; this is exactly where wires get let go.

Flush hard and raise the leg after every drug through a femoral line during CPR.

An IO already in and working beats a central line you are still placing. Place this line for what only it can do, not for speed.

Say which side you are taking, out loud. Most access collisions are communication failures, not technical ones.
