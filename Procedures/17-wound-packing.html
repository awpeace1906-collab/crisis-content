Wound Packing with Hemostatic Agents

Direct pressure taken to its logical extreme — packing the wound cavity itself, not just covering the surface.

01

Indications

  • Junctional hemorrhage — groin, axilla, gluteal, perineum, base of the neck. The anatomy where a limb tourniquet has nothing to squeeze against.
  • Deep or cavitating extremity wounds where surface pressure closes the skin but not the vessel underneath
  • Bleeding that continues past a correctly applied limb tourniquet — packing is an adjunct, not a competitor
  • A mangled or traumatically amputated limb stump, packed above the tourniquet line
  • Scalp and facial wounds that bleed briskly and are hard to compress against a curved skull

The common thread is a wound with a floor you can reach. Packing works by putting material in direct contact with a torn vessel and holding it against something solid. If you cannot get to the vessel, packing will not find it for you.

02

What You Must Not Pack

This is the section that matters most, and it is the one usually reduced to “none absolute.”

  • An open chest wound. There is nothing to tamponade against — the pleural space is not a cavity that packing can fill, and pushing gauze into it drives air and material toward the lung. An open chest wound gets a vented chest seal and a plan for decompression, not a pack.
  • An abdominal wound or evisceration. The peritoneum is a non-compressible space. Packing achieves nothing, and gauze forced through the wall can perforate bowel. Cover eviscerated contents with a moist dressing and move toward an operating room.
  • The eye or the orbit.
  • An open skull fracture with visible brain. Packing raises intracranial pressure and can drive fragments inward. Cover only.
  • Wounds where the source is clearly proximal and unreachable — a groin wound whose vessel has retracted under the inguinal ligament. Packing the visible hole while the bleeding happens above it wastes the minutes you needed for a junctional device or proximal manual compression.

Relative: a neck wound can be packed, but never wrap the neck circumferentially — you will obstruct the airway and the contralateral carotid. Pack, then hold with a hand or secure over the shoulder and under the opposite axilla.

03

Equipment — and Which Agents Are Obsolete

  • Kaolin-impregnated gauze (Combat Gauze and equivalents) — the current first-line hemostatic dressing in Tactical Combat Casualty Care. Kaolin activates factor XII and the contact pathway. Non-exothermic.
  • Chitosan-based gauze (Celox Gauze, ChitoGauze) — an accepted alternative. Chitosan works by a charge-based mucoadhesive mechanism that is largely independent of the clotting cascade, which makes it a rational choice in a patient who is anticoagulated or profoundly coagulopathic.
  • Compressed expanding sponges (XStat) — injectable mini-sponges for narrow-tract junctional wounds that a hand cannot reach into. Each sponge carries a radiopaque marker, and the count must travel with the patient.
  • Plain gauze — entirely acceptable. A correctly packed plain-gauze wound under sustained pressure outperforms a hemostatic dressing packed badly. Do not delay packing to hunt for the good stuff.
  • Analgesia. Packing a deep wound in a conscious patient is among the most painful things done in prehospital care. Ketamine is the usual answer because it preserves airway and pressure. Plan it before you start, not after the patient fights you.
  • Pressure dressing (elastic wrap or purpose-built) and a marker.

What is no longer recommended: granular and powder agents poured into a wound. The first-generation zeolite products were strongly exothermic and caused full-thickness burns in the wound and to the hands applying them. Granules also migrate, embolize, and are extremely difficult for a surgeon to debride. If a powder is what is in the bag, it is better than nothing — but gauze is the modern form factor for a reason.

04

Find the Source Before You Fill the Hole

The single conceptual error in wound packing is treating it as filling a cavity. It is not. It is putting a thumb on a specific torn vessel and then wedging the wound full so the thumb can leave.

  • Expose completely. Cut the clothing away. A wound you are packing through a trouser leg is a wound you cannot see.
  • Sweep out pooled blood and loose clot with a gloved hand. Established clot sitting in the cavity holds your packing away from the vessel and hides the jet you are looking for.
  • Look for the jet, then put a finger on it. Pulsatile bleeding localizes itself if you clear the pool for a second and watch. Once you have it, keep a finger there while you bring the gauze in with the other hand.
  • Expect the wound to be deeper than it looks. Skin defects are small; tracts are long. Your finger goes in to the knuckles, not to the fingernail.
05

Packing Technique

1
Start at the source, not at the skin
Drive the first few inches of gauze directly onto the bleeding vessel with your fingertip, replacing your finger with the gauze in the same spot. This first contact is what stops the bleeding; everything after it is scaffolding that holds the contact in place.
2
Pack against bone wherever the anatomy allows
A vessel compressed between gauze and bone is occluded; a vessel compressed between gauze and soft tissue is merely pushed aside. See §07 for the specific targets — femoral against the superior pubic ramus, axillary against the humeral head or first rib.
3
One continuous strip, fed in hard, no dead space
Feed the roll in progressively, using your fingers to ram each bight firmly into a corner before bringing in the next. This is not gentle. A loose pack with air in it is functionally an unpacked wound — and it looks fine from the outside while the patient bleeds into the dressing. Use as many rolls as the cavity takes.
4
Fill to just proud of the skin
The last of the packing should sit slightly above the skin surface so the dressing on top transmits pressure into the wound rather than bridging across it.
5
Three minutes of hard, uninterrupted pressure
Both hands, body weight over the wound, no peeking. See §06 — this is the step people get wrong.
6
Pressure dressing over the top, then watch it
Apply an elastic pressure dressing over the pack, firm enough to maintain compression. Then look at it again in a few minutes — and keep looking. Mark the time and the number of rolls used on the dressing and on the patient.
If it fails, remove and repack — do not pile on top

This is the opposite of the rule for a soaked-through dressing, and the difference matters. A dressing is reinforced. A failed pack is removed and replaced, because its failure means the gauze never reached the bleeding vessel — and adding more material on top of a pack that missed just raises the pile. Pull it, find the source again, and pack it properly with fresh material.

06

The Three-Minute Hold

Three minutes of continuous direct pressure, timed on a clock, without lifting to look. Current TCCC guidance specifies a minimum of three minutes for hemostatic dressings; some chitosan products and some coagulopathic patients warrant five. Check the packet of what you are actually using.

Why it is non-negotiable: a clot takes minutes to form and seconds to shear off. Lifting the pack at ninety seconds to see how it is going destroys everything the ninety seconds achieved and resets the clock — and it is the commonest reason a correctly packed wound appears to keep bleeding. The urge to check is strongest at exactly the moment checking is most destructive.

Have someone time it out loud. Three minutes with your weight on a wound feels far longer than three minutes, and untimed holds are consistently short. This is the same failure as the REBOA clock: a task nobody owns is a task that does not happen.

Use the time. It is three minutes in which the person holding pressure can do nothing else — so everyone else gets access, blood, TXA, and the transport decision moving.

09

Complications

  • Ongoing occult hemorrhage beneath an intact-looking dressing — the failure mode of a loose pack, and the reason a packed wound gets looked at repeatedly rather than dressed and forgotten.
  • Retained packing material — a foreign body and an infection source. Count and document.
  • Wound infection and tissue necrosis from prolonged tight packing.
  • Nerve injury from sustained pressure, particularly axillary and inguinal packs.
  • Thermal injury — historical, from first-generation exothermic granular agents.
  • Distal ischemia if the overlying pressure dressing becomes an unintended tourniquet.
  • Airway compromise from a neck pack or a developing hematoma.
  • Air embolism from an open large vein in the neck or axilla — an argument for an occlusive layer over a neck pack.
10

Handover

  • Say what is in the wound and how much. Agent, number of rolls or sponges, time packed. Write it on the dressing and on the patient's skin — verbal handover in a trauma bay is lost.
  • Expanding sponges must be counted, and the count must be spoken to the surgeon. They are radiopaque for exactly this reason, but a film is a slower way to learn a number you already had.
  • Do not unwrap it to show anyone. The receiving team does not need to see the wound; they need to know what you found and what you did. The pack comes out in an operating room.
  • Flag coagulopathy and anticoagulants explicitly — a pack in a patient on a DOAC is holding a line that will not hold itself.
11

Pearls

Pack onto the vessel, not into the hole. The first inch of gauze does the work; the rest holds it there.

Compress against bone. Soft tissue on both sides means the vessel just moves.

Three minutes, timed out loud, no peeking. The urge to check peaks exactly when checking is most destructive.

A failed pack is removed and replaced — not reinforced. Opposite rule to a soaked dressing, and the distinction is the whole point.

Never pack a chest or an abdomen. Seal the chest, cover the abdomen, and go.

Never wrap a neck circumferentially.

Plain gauze packed well beats hemostatic gauze packed badly. Do not spend thirty seconds hunting for the right packet.

Give ketamine before you start. This hurts more than almost anything else you will do to a conscious patient.

Count what goes in, and say the number out loud at handover.
