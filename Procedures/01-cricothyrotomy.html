Cricothyrotomy — Surgical, Percutaneous & Needle

01

Indications

  • Cannot Intubate, Cannot Oxygenate (CICO) — the only indication that matters in the moment
  • Artificial airway obstruction (device failure, blocked ETT or tracheostomy tube) that cannot be cleared
  • Pathologic airway obstruction — angioedema, trauma, burns, tumor, abscess, distorted anatomy — not amenable to oral or nasal intubation
  • Failure of primary and rescue airway techniques when oxygenation cannot be achieved by any other route
The indication is oxygenation, not intubation. A patient you cannot intubate but can oxygenate by mask or supraglottic airway is not a CICO patient — that is a difficult airway, and you have time to think. The front-of-neck decision belongs to the patient who is desaturating despite optimized attempts at all three of mask, supraglottic, and tube. See the Front-of-Neck Rescue Airway entry for the decision framework that sits above this one.
02

Contraindications

  • In true CICO there are no absolute contraindications. The alternative to a front-of-neck airway in that situation is a hypoxic death.
  • A viable, faster alternative airway exists (relative) — if one more optimized laryngoscopy attempt or a supraglottic device will work, do that instead
  • Children under roughly 8–12 years — the cricothyroid membrane is tiny, soft, and hard to identify; needle technique is the default here and the age threshold varies between guidelines, so know which one your institution follows
  • Coagulopathy, overlying infection, or anterior neck tumor — all relative, and none of them outrank hypoxia
03

Equipment — What Should Be on the CICO Cart

Scalpel-bougie-tube (the adult default — this is the set that matters)

  • #10 scalpel — the broad belly is deliberate; a #11 point is easy to bury and harder to control
  • Bougie, ideally coudé-tipped
  • Cuffed tube, size 6.0 — a standard 7.0–8.0 ETT will often not pass a cricothyroid membrane. Have the 6.0 pre-identified, not hunted for mid-procedure.
  • Syringe for the cuff, tube tie or suture, capnography

Needle cricothyrotomy (pediatric default, adult fallback)

  • Large-bore cannula, 12–14 ga in adults, smaller in children
  • Syringe (saline-filled, so aspirated air shows as bubbles)
  • A purpose-built ventilation device with a pressure-limited source and an exhalation route — not an improvised setup assembled for the first time under pressure. A 3 mm ETT connector or a 3-way stopcock arrangement works, but know which one is on your cart and how it assembles.

Percutaneous (Seldinger) kit — and the things people forget

  • Introducer needle, guidewire, dilator, airway catheter (commercial kits vary — open yours once in daylight before you need it at 3 a.m.)
  • Ultrasound with a linear probe — the single most useful addition to a difficult-neck CICO cart
  • Suction, working and within reach — the field will bleed
  • Gloves you can actually feel through. This is a tactile procedure.
Know which kit is on your cart before the emergency

The most common equipment failure in a real CICO event is not a missing item — it is an operator meeting an unfamiliar commercial kit for the first time while a patient desaturates. Open your institution's kit once, lay the parts out, and assemble it. That fifteen-minute exercise is worth more than any amount of reading.

04

Finding the Membrane

This is where the procedure is actually won or lost

The laryngeal handshake. Stand at the patient's side. With your non-dominant hand, grasp the larynx between thumb and middle finger and walk down the midline: the hyoid first, then the thyroid cartilage (the broad shield, with the notch superiorly), then the soft depression immediately below it — that is the cricothyroid membrane — and then the firm ring of the cricoid cartilage below that. Rock the larynx gently side to side as you go; the whole assembly moves as a unit, which is how you distinguish it from surrounding soft tissue.

In an adult the membrane sits roughly a finger's breadth below the thyroid notch and is about 1 cm tall and 2–3 cm wide — a small target, and smaller still in women.

When you cannot feel anything — obesity, edema, hematoma, burns. This is common and it is the situation that kills.

  • Ultrasound, if it is already in the room and someone can drive it. Linear probe transverse across the anterior neck: the tracheal rings appear as a row of hypoechoic arcs, and sliding cephalad the cricoid is the largest and most prominent. Do not go looking for the machine mid-arrest.
  • If there is no ultrasound, the incision is the assessment. Make a long vertical midline incision — 8–10 cm if you have no landmarks at all — and dissect bluntly with your finger down to the larynx. Midline is relatively avascular; this is precisely why vertical is the correct choice when you are unsure.
  • Then palpate inside the wound. Your finger, not your eye, finds the membrane in a bleeding neck.
Never let go of the larynx

Once your non-dominant hand has the larynx, it stays there until the tube is in. Every time you release to reposition, adjust a glove, or take an instrument, you lose the anatomy and start again — in a neck that is now bleeding. Hand off instruments, accept awkward angles, but do not let go.

05

Scalpel–Bougie–Tube — The Adult Default

Fastest, most reliable, and the technique to default to when seconds matter
1
Position and expose
Extend the neck if not contraindicated — it brings the larynx forward and makes everything easier. Stand at the patient's side, not the head. Get the neck exposed properly; do not operate through a gown collar or a cervical collar's front panel (open it, and have someone hold manual in-line stabilization if the spine is a concern).
2
Stabilize the larynx and choose your incision
Laryngeal handshake with the non-dominant hand. If you can confidently palpate the membrane, a transverse stab through it is reasonable. If you cannot, make a long vertical midline skin incision first — it stays out of the anterior jugular veins, it can be extended in either direction, and it lets you feel your way down. When in doubt, vertical.
3
Stab through the membrane, then turn the blade
With the membrane identified, insert the scalpel through it transversely. Then rotate the blade so the sharp edge points caudally (toward the feet). This orients the opening and, critically, gives the bougie a surface to slide along. Keep the blade in place — it is now your guide, and removing it means finding the hole again.
4
Slide the bougie along the blade, angled caudally
Pass the bougie into the trachea alongside the scalpel blade, directed toward the carina. Some operators substitute a finger for the blade at this point to hold the tract open — the finger-and-bougie combination is what confirms you are in the trachea in a dark, bloody field. You are looking for tracheal clicks as the coudé tip runs over the cartilage rings, and hold-up at 20–30 cm. Soft, endless advancement with neither clicks nor hold-up means you are in tissue or esophagus.
5
Railroad a 6.0 cuffed tube
Remove the scalpel, keeping the bougie and your stabilizing hand exactly where they are. Railroad the tube over the bougie with a gentle rotating motion — if it catches at the membrane, twisting usually walks it through where pushing will not. Advance only about 5–6 cm past the membrane. This is not an oral intubation; pushing to a normal 22 cm mark puts you in the right mainstem or through the carina.
6
Remove bougie, inflate cuff, confirm, secure
Withdraw the bougie, inflate the cuff, ventilate, and confirm (see below). Then secure the tube immediately and obsessively — a surgical airway sits in a short, slippery tract with nothing anatomical holding it, and it will fall out during the first transfer if it is only being held by the person who placed it.
Bleeding is expected. Keep going.

The anterior neck bleeds, mostly venous, and it will obscure your view almost immediately. This is not a complication to stop and fix — it is the normal appearance of the procedure. In CICO the bleeding does not kill the patient in the next two minutes and the hypoxia does. Work by feel, keep the larynx stabilized, and deal with hemostasis once the airway is secured.

06

Needle Cricothyrotomy — And Why Exhalation Is the Whole Problem

The pediatric default, and an adult temporizing measure only
1
Puncture caudally, aspirating as you go
Stabilize the larynx. Insert the cannula through the membrane angled caudally at about 45°, with a saline-filled syringe on the hub, aspirating continuously. Free bubbling of air confirms tracheal entry — the saline is what makes that unmistakable.
2
Advance the cannula, withdraw the needle, hold on
Slide the cannula off the needle into the trachea and remove the needle. Hold the cannula continuously by hand — it kinks and dislodges with almost no provocation, and it is the only airway the patient has.
3
Connect the ventilation device you already know
Attach your pressure-limited oxygen source or the 3 mm connector/stopcock arrangement from your cart. A standard bag-valve will generate almost no flow through a 14 ga cannula — this needs a high-pressure source to work at all.
4
Ventilate with a long expiratory phase and watch the chest fall
Short insufflation, then a markedly prolonged expiratory phase — think in the region of 1:4 or longer. Watch the chest actually come back down between breaths. If it does not, stop insufflating. Stacking breaths into a chest that cannot empty is how this technique kills people.
Gas goes in through the cannula; it comes out through the upper airway

A 14 ga cannula is an inadequate exhalation route. Expiration depends on the patient's own upper airway being at least partially patent. If the obstruction is complete — total supraglottic obstruction, severe angioedema, a foreign body — insufflated gas has nowhere to go, intrathoracic pressure climbs with every breath, and you produce barotrauma, pneumothorax, and circulatory collapse. In complete obstruction a needle technique is the wrong answer; you need a cuffed surgical airway.

Needle cricothyrotomy is a bridge, not a destination. Ventilation through a narrow cannula does not clear CO2 adequately, so hypercarbia climbs steadily — usable for roughly 30–45 minutes. Start arranging the definitive airway the moment the cannula is working, not when the gas goes bad.

Percutaneous (Seldinger) Cricothyrotomy

Familiar sequence, but it costs time you may not have
1
Puncture and confirm
Stabilize the larynx; introducer needle through the membrane angled caudally, aspirating a saline-filled syringe until air bubbles freely.
2
Wire in, needle out
Thread the guidewire caudally into the trachea and remove the needle over it. From this point the wire is the airway — never let go of it, and never pull it back through the needle.
3
Nick the skin, then dilate
Small vertical skin incision at the wire to let the dilator through, then advance the dilator-and-airway assembly over the wire with a firm, steady, rotating push. The resistance at the membrane is real and expected; it is also exactly where a false passage gets created if your angle has drifted.
4
Airway in, dilator and wire out, confirm
Slide the airway off the dilator into the trachea, remove dilator and wire together, inflate the cuff if present, confirm and secure.
Right sequence, wrong emergency

Seldinger technique is elegant and most clinicians are fluent in it from central lines — which is exactly why it is tempting under stress. It is also measurably slower than scalpel–bougie–tube, has more steps to drop, and depends on fine motor control that degrades badly with adrenaline. In a true CICO, reach for the scalpel. Keep this technique for the semi-elective, better-lit, slightly-more-time situation.

08

Confirming You Are in the Trachea

  • Waveform capnography is the standard — but read the caveat below before you rely on its absence.
  • Bougie feel, at the time of insertion: tracheal clicks and hold-up at 20–30 cm. Soft endless passage is a red flag you get before committing the tube.
  • Chest rise, bilateral breath sounds, absence of gastric insufflation.
  • Bedside ultrasound — tracheal widening on tube passage, or lung sliding bilaterally.
  • Rising SpO2, allowing for the lag in a profoundly hypoxic patient.
Absent capnography in an arrested patient does not mean you missed

End-tidal CO2 depends on pulmonary blood flow. In cardiac arrest, or in a patient who has been profoundly hypoxic with a failing circulation, the trace may be flat or minimal even with a perfectly placed tube. Do not pull a correctly sited surgical airway because the capnograph is unconvincing during arrest — corroborate with bougie feel, chest rise, and ultrasound instead.

Complications & Pitfalls

  • Delayed recognition of CICO — consistently the biggest driver of bad outcomes, ahead of any technical failure. The scalpel is rarely the problem; the twelve minutes before it was picked up usually are.
  • False passage into pretracheal tissue, with ventilation producing surgical emphysema and no gas exchange
  • Posterior wall injury — through the trachea into the esophagus, from an over-deep stab or a blade angled posteriorly
  • Barotrauma and pneumothorax from needle ventilation without an exhalation route
  • Bleeding — expected and survivable acutely; anterior jugular veins and, rarely, an aberrant high thyroid vessel
  • Tube dislodgement, especially during transfer — the most common way a successful airway is subsequently lost
  • Subglottic stenosis as a late consequence, which is why conversion to a definitive airway is planned rather than indefinite
  • Choosing the slow technique — reaching for a Seldinger kit because it feels familiar, when the situation demanded a scalpel
11

After the Airway Is In

  • Secure it as though someone is going to pull on it, then check it again after every move.
  • Now get hemostasis — the bleeding you correctly ignored still needs dealing with.
  • Sedate and analgese. A patient who arrives at this point conscious has just had their neck cut open.
  • Chest radiograph for tube position and to look for the pneumothorax you may have caused, particularly after needle ventilation.
  • Plan the definitive airway early. A cricothyrotomy is a bridge; conversion to a formal tracheostomy or an oral tube is an elective, controlled decision made in daylight — not a 3 a.m. improvisation. See the Tracheostomy entry.
  • Debrief the team. These events are rare, fast, and frightening, and the learning evaporates within hours if nobody captures it.
12

Pearls

If you are doing this, you have already failed two other airway plans. Commit fully. Mid-procedure second-guessing is what turns a survivable CICO into a death.

The hand on the larynx never leaves. If you remember one motor habit from this page, make it that one.

When you cannot feel the landmarks, cut vertically and use your finger. The midline is relatively avascular and the incision is how you find the anatomy — not something you do after finding it.

Tracheal clicks and hold-up are your confirmation, and they arrive before you commit the tube. Soft, endless bougie passage is the cheapest warning you will get; heed it.

Use a 6.0. The tube that will not fit is a predictable, preventable delay.

Needle technique needs somewhere for the gas to go. In complete obstruction it is not a rescue — it is a barotrauma generator.

Practice on a model, regularly. This is the definitive low-frequency, high-stakes psychomotor skill, and under adrenaline you will do what you have rehearsed, not what you have read.
