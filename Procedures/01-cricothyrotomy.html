Cricothyrotomy — Surgical, Percutaneous & Needle

Three techniques for one problem: Can't Intubate, Can't Oxygenate. Adapted from the author's original “Introduction to Surgical Airways” teaching deck, used with permission.

AIRWAY
⚡ TECHNIQUE Indications Contraindications Equipment Complications & Pitfalls Pearls References
01

Indications

  • Cannot Intubate, Cannot Oxygenate (CICO)
  • Artificial airway obstruction (device failure, blocked ETT/trach)
  • Pathologic airway obstruction (angioedema, trauma, burns, abnormal anatomy) not amenable to oral/nasal intubation
  • Failure of primary and rescue airway techniques when oxygenation cannot otherwise be achieved
02

Contraindications

  • A viable, faster alternative airway exists (relative — in true CICO there are no absolute contraindications to a front-of-neck airway)
  • Pediatric patients under ~1 year for needle/surgical cricothyrotomy — anatomy strongly favors alternative approaches where any other option exists
  • Distorted or unidentifiable anterior neck anatomy (relative — may require the scalpel-finger-bougie approach over needle-based techniques)
03

Equipment

Needle cricothyrotomy

  • Large-bore needle/angiocath (12–14 ga)
  • 3 mm ID ETT connector/BVM adaptor
  • 3-way stopcock, jet insufflation source

Percutaneous (Seldinger) cricothyrotomy

  • Introducer needle, flexible guidewire, dilator
  • Cricothyrotomy tube or ETT

Surgical (open) cricothyrotomy

  • #10 scalpel, bougie
  • Cuffed ETT or cricothyrotomy tube
04

Stepwise Technique

Needle cricothyrotomy
1
Palpate and stabilize the cricothyroid membrane.
2
Insert large-bore needle/angiocath through the membrane at a caudad angle, aspirating for air to confirm tracheal placement.
3
Advance catheter, remove needle, secure.
4
Connect 3 mm ID ETT adaptor and 3-way stopcock to an oxygen/jet insufflation source.
5
Ventilate via jet insufflation — expect a prolonged expiratory phase due to narrow-lumen exhalation limitation.
6
Treat as a temporizing measure only — plan definitive airway within ~45 minutes to avoid progressive hypercarbia; this is the primary technique of choice in pediatric patients due to small anatomy.
Percutaneous (Seldinger) cricothyrotomy
7
Palpate and stabilize the cricothyroid membrane.
8
Insert introducer needle through the membrane, confirm tracheal entry (air aspiration).
9
Thread flexible guidewire through the needle into the trachea; remove needle, leaving wire in place.
10
Make a small vertical skin incision superficial to the membrane.
11
Advance dilator over the guidewire to expand the tract through the membrane.
12
Remove dilator; advance cricothyrotomy tube or ETT over the wire into the trachea.
13
Remove wire, confirm placement, secure tube.
14
Slower than the surgical technique — not the preferred choice when seconds matter most in a true CICO emergency.
Surgical (open) cricothyrotomy
15
Palpate and stabilize the cricothyroid membrane with non-dominant hand, maintaining laryngeal control throughout.
16
Make a vertical skin incision superficial to the membrane.
17
Insert the scalpel laterally through the cricothyroid membrane itself, then rotate/withdraw to open the tract.
18
Insert a finger along the blunt side of the scalpel to maintain the opening and confirm tracheal entry by feel.
19
Insert a bougie alongside the finger into the trachea.
20
Railroad a cuffed ETT or cricothyrotomy tube over the bougie into the trachea.
21
Remove bougie, inflate cuff, confirm placement (chest rise, EtCO2, auscultation), secure tube.
Declare it, then commit

The biggest driver of a bad CICO outcome is hesitation to escalate, not technique failure — once the front-of-neck decision is made, further attempts at oral intubation or mask ventilation only cost the time the procedure needs.

05

Complications & Pitfalls

  • Delayed recognition of CICO — the biggest driver of bad outcomes is hesitation to escalate, not technique failure
  • Needle cricothyrotomy: hypercarbia beyond the ~45-minute usable window due to inadequate exhalation through a narrow lumen
  • False passage (paratracheal placement) — confirm with air aspiration/tactile feel before committing to dilation or tube passage
  • Percutaneous technique speed — the Seldinger sequence costs time that may not exist in true CICO; know when to default to open technique
  • Pediatric anatomy makes the open surgical technique disproportionately difficult for inexperienced providers — needle technique is the default in this population
  • Bleeding obscuring the field, especially with vascular anterior neck anatomy
06

Pearls

If you're doing a front-of-neck airway, you've already failed two other airway plans — commit fully and move fast, don't second-guess the decision mid-procedure.

The finger-bougie technique in open cricothyrotomy is what actually confirms you're in the trachea — trust tactile feel over visual confirmation in a bloody or dark field.

Needle technique buys time in kids and true emergencies; it is not a destination airway. Know your institution's definitive airway plan before you start.

Practice the open technique on a model regularly — this is a low-frequency, high-stakes skill where muscle memory under stress is the entire game.
