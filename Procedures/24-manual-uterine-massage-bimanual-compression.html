Manual Uterine Massage & Bimanual Compression

First-line mechanical management of uterine atony — the hands-on step that often works before any medication does.

01

Indications

  • Postpartum hemorrhage with a soft, boggy, poorly contracted uterus — uterine atony, which causes 70–80% of postpartum hemorrhage
  • Prophylactically, in the third stage, as part of active management alongside a uterotonic
  • Any postpartum bleeding that looks more than ordinary, before you have worked out the cause — massage costs nothing and buys time

The current definition of postpartum hemorrhage is cumulative blood loss of ≥1000 mL, or blood loss accompanied by signs of hypovolemia, within 24 hours of birth — regardless of route of delivery. The older 500 mL vaginal threshold is retained by many units as a trigger for heightened attention, which is reasonable.

You are underestimating the blood loss — everyone does

Visual estimation of obstetric blood loss under-reads by 30–50%, and the error grows as the volume grows. Quantify it: weigh pads, drapes and sponges (1 g = 1 mL, minus dry weight) and use a graduated under-buttocks drape. And remember that a healthy pregnant woman's blood volume is up 40–50%, so she compensates beautifully and then collapses: vital signs can stay near-normal until 1500 mL is gone. Watch the shock index (HR ÷ SBP) — above 0.9 to 1.0 is an early and validated trigger, well before hypotension appears.

02

When Massage Is the Wrong Answer

  • A firm uterus with ongoing bleeding is not atony. Stop massaging and go looking. Inspect the cervix, vagina and perineum for lacerations, consider retained placental tissue, consider uterine rupture, and consider coagulopathy — including amniotic fluid embolism, which presents as coagulopathic bleeding with cardiorespiratory collapse.
  • Uterine inversion. If the fundus is not palpable abdominally and there is a mass at or through the cervix, with shock out of proportion to the visible loss, this is inversion, and massaging or giving uterotonics makes it irreversible. See §06 — the treatment is the opposite of everything else on this page.
  • Retained placenta. A uterus cannot contract around retained tissue. It has to come out first.
  • Continuing massage as a substitute for escalation. A uterus that firms under your hand and softens the moment you stop is telling you atony is ongoing and unresponsive. That is a signal to escalate, not to keep rubbing.

The four T's, in order of frequency: Tone (atony, ~70–80%) · Trauma (lacerations, rupture, hematoma, ~20%) · Tissue (retained products) · Thrombin (coagulopathy). Inversion sits outside the list and is the one that punishes the reflexive response.

03

Equipment

  • Gloved hands — long gloves if you have them, because bimanual compression puts your forearm in the vagina.
  • A urinary catheter. Frequently skipped and genuinely effective: a full bladder mechanically prevents the uterus from contracting. Empty it early.
  • Uterotonics, drawn and ready — see §05 for doses and the two contraindications that matter.
  • Large-bore IV access ×2, crystalloid, and the massive transfusion pathway on standby.
  • Tranexamic acid.
  • Analgesia or sedation. Sustained bimanual compression is extremely painful in a woman who is usually awake and alert.
  • Escalation equipment within reach: intrauterine balloon (Bakri, or a condom catheter improvised over a Foley), vacuum-induced tamponade device, and the number for whoever performs compression sutures, embolization, or a hysterectomy.
  • A scale for quantitative blood loss, and a whiteboard.
04

Technique

Fundal massage — two hands, not one
1
Empty the bladder first
Catheterize. A distended bladder displaces the uterus and physically prevents contraction, and this single step sometimes resolves the problem. It also gives you urine output as a perfusion marker for everything that follows.
2
Support the lower segment with the second hand
Cup the fundus with one hand and place the other just above the symphysis, pressing back and supporting the lower uterine segment. Vigorous one-handed massage of an atonic uterus can invert it. The lower hand is a guard, not an afterthought.
3
Massage firmly — and know what you are feeling for
A deep, firm, kneading circular motion, not a light rub. The endpoint is a uterus that feels like a clenched fist, globular and hard, at or below the umbilicus. Boggy, wide, and doughy means atony continues. Express retained clot from the lower segment as tone returns — clot in the cavity perpetuates atony.
Bimanual compression
4
Vaginal hand into the anterior fornix, as a fist
Insert the whole hand, make a fist in the anterior fornix, and press upward and forward against the anterior wall of the uterus. Say what you are about to do — she is awake, and this is frightening as well as painful.
5
Abdominal hand behind the fundus, pulling it forward
Reach behind the uterus abdominally, cup the posterior wall, and draw the fundus down and forward onto the vaginal fist. You are not pressing straight down — you are bringing the two hands together.
6
Compress and hold
Squeeze the whole uterus between your hands, hard, and sustain it. This works two ways: it compresses the myometrium directly, and it kinks the uterine arteries in the broad ligament. It is exhausting within minutes — plan to rotate operators before anyone weakens. Do not release intermittently to check; hold, while the drugs and the team catch up.
7
Everything else happens around you
Compression is a holding action. While it is held: uterotonics given, TXA given, two lines in, blood ordered, bladder emptied, the genital tract inspected, and the next step being prepared. Bimanual compression that continues for twenty minutes with nothing else happening is not a plan.
8
Aortic compression, if it is catastrophic
In torrential hemorrhage, a closed fist pressed hard just above the umbilicus, directed posteriorly onto the vertebral column, compresses the abdominal aorta. Confirm by loss of femoral pulses. A crude, tiring, temporary measure — and it buys minutes when nothing else is working.
05

Uterotonics — Doses and the Two That Can Harm

Massage and drugs go together. Neither is the second step.

Oxytocin

The two that catch people: methylergonovine in a preeclamptic woman, and carboprost in an asthmatic. Both are given in a rush by someone reaching for the next ampoule, and both patient groups are common on a labor ward. Say the contraindication out loud as you draw it up.

Uterine artery blood flow at term is roughly 500–700 mL/min. Hemostasis after delivery is mechanical — contracted myometrium strangling the spiral arteries, the “living ligatures” — not clot formation. That is why a woman with normal coagulation can exsanguinate from an atonic uterus in minutes, and why a profoundly coagulopathic woman stops bleeding once the uterus contracts.

06

Uterine Inversion — the Opposite Treatment

If the fundus is missing, stop and think before you massage

Recognize it: the fundus cannot be palpated abdominally, or there is a dimple where it should be; a soft mass is felt at the cervix or seen at the introitus; and there is shock out of proportion to the visible blood loss, from the vagal response to traction on the peritoneum. Often follows cord traction on a fundally implanted placenta.

Everything this page otherwise recommends is wrong here. Uterotonics make the cervical ring clamp down around the inverted fundus and convert a reducible inversion into a surgical one.

  • Stop all uterotonics.
  • Replace it immediately — every minute makes it harder. Johnson maneuver: grasp the fundus in the palm, direct the fingertips toward the posterior fornix, and push steadily upward along the axis of the vagina toward the umbilicus. Steady pressure, not jabbing.
  • If the ring has already tightened, relax the uterus — nitroglycerin 50–100 mcg IV, terbutaline, magnesium, or a volatile anesthetic — then replace.
  • Leave the placenta attached until after reduction if it is still adherent; removing it first increases blood loss.
  • Only once the uterus is back: give uterotonics, and keep a hand inside until it contracts around it.
  • Resuscitate throughout, and call for surgical help early — failed reduction needs an operating room.
07

When Compression Is Not Enough

Massage and compression are the first rung. Bleeding that persists despite a well-contracted uterus, or atony that will not hold, moves up the ladder — and the movement should be by the clock, not by feel.

  • Intrauterine balloon tamponade — a Bakri balloon filled with 300–500 mL of saline, or a condom tied over a catheter where no device exists. The “tamponade test”: if bleeding stops with the balloon up, laparotomy is usually avoidable; if it does not, the patient needs an operation and you have learned that in minutes.
  • Vacuum-induced tamponade — a suction device that collapses the cavity rather than distending it. Fast where stocked.
  • Examination under anesthesia — for lacerations, retained tissue, and rupture. Do not persist with medical management on an undiagnosed cause.
  • Compression sutures (B-Lynch and variants) at laparotomy.
  • Uterine artery embolization, where interventional radiology is available and the patient is stable enough to travel.
  • Uterine or internal iliac artery ligation.
  • Hysterectomy — definitive, and the decision that is most often made too late. In refractory hemorrhage, deciding early saves lives.
  • Aortic compression, REBOA Zone 3, or a non-pneumatic anti-shock garment as bridges, particularly during transfer.
  • Massive transfusion throughout, with attention to fibrinogen — obstetric hemorrhage depletes it early, and the target in obstetric bleeding is higher: above 200 mg/dL.

09

Complications

  • Uterine inversion from vigorous unsupported fundal massage or cord traction — the reason the second hand exists.
  • Delayed escalation — the dominant harm. Compression is comfortable to keep doing and feels like treatment, and hours are lost inside it.
  • Delayed recognition of a non-atonic cause, because the reflex response was applied to the wrong problem.
  • Pain and distress in an awake patient — treatable, and frequently untreated.
  • Uterotonic adverse effects — hypertensive crisis, bronchospasm, hypotension, fever, vomiting.
  • Consequences of massive hemorrhage: dilutional and consumptive coagulopathy, transfusion reactions, acute kidney injury, Sheehan syndrome, ARDS, and hysterectomy with its own permanent consequences.
  • Psychological sequelae for the patient — postpartum hemorrhage is a recognized cause of birth trauma and post-traumatic stress. Debrief her, afterwards, properly.
10

Pearls

Empty the bladder. Simple, frequently skipped, and sometimes the whole answer.

Two hands on the fundus. The lower hand supports the segment and prevents the inversion your massage could cause.

Firm uterus plus bleeding means look elsewhere. Cervix, vagina, retained tissue, rupture, coagulopathy.

Missing fundus plus disproportionate shock means inversion — and every instinct on this page is then backwards. Stop the uterotonics and replace it.

You are underestimating the loss. Weigh it. Watch the shock index, not the blood pressure.

She looks fine until she does not. Forty to fifty percent extra blood volume buys compensation that ends abruptly.

Methylergonovine and hypertension; carboprost and asthma. Say it aloud as you draw it up.

Oxytocin goes in dilute.

Hemostasis here is muscle, not clot. A contracted uterus stops bleeding even in a coagulopathic woman.

Compression is a holding action. If nothing else is being organized while you hold it, the plan has stopped.

Rotate before you tire.
