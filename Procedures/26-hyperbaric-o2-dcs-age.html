Hyperbaric Oxygen Coordination & Prep

Decompression sickness and arterial gas embolism — 100% oxygen first, chamber logistics in parallel, altitude avoided at every step of transport.

01

Indications

  • Decompression sickness, Type I (musculoskeletal, cutaneous, lymphatic) or Type II (neurologic, inner ear, cardiopulmonary)
  • Arterial gas embolism — from diving barotrauma, or iatrogenic: central line insertion or removal, cardiac and neurosurgery, hemodialysis, lung biopsy, contrast injection
  • Any diver with new symptoms after a dive, however vague — fatigue, a rash, an odd ache. Dive profiles that look safe do not exclude it.
  • Severe carbon monoxide poisoning — managed by the same chambers and teams, though the evidence is more contested than for DCS
  • Related chamber indications: clostridial myonecrosis, necrotizing soft tissue infection, crush injury with compartment syndrome, compromised flaps and grafts

Why pressure treats this — three separate mechanisms:

  • Boyle's law: bubble volume falls as pressure rises. At 2.8 ATA a bubble is roughly a third of its surface volume, which mechanically relieves vascular obstruction and tissue distortion.
  • Inert gas washout: breathing 100% oxygen removes nitrogen from the breathing mixture, creating a steep diffusion gradient that drives nitrogen out of the bubble and out of the tissue.
  • Dissolved oxygen delivery: by Henry's law, plasma-dissolved oxygen rises enough at treatment pressures to meet resting tissue metabolic demand without hemoglobin at all, oxygenating tissue downstream of an obstruction.

The first two of these begin the moment you put on a non-rebreather. That is why oxygen is not a holding measure — it is the same treatment at lower intensity.

02

Recognizing It — and the Ten-Minute Rule

PresentationFeaturesNote
Arterial gas embolismSudden stroke-like deficit, loss of consciousness, seizure, or arrest — typically within 10 minutes of surfacing, often immediately on surfacingSymptoms within 10 minutes of surfacing are AGE until proven otherwise. Classically follows breath-holding during ascent. Can occur after a very shallow dive.
DCS Type II — spinalLow back or girdle pain, band-like paresthesia, ascending weakness, urinary retentionThe commonest serious neurologic DCS. Urinary retention is easy to miss and should be asked about directly.
DCS Type II — cerebralConfusion, visual disturbance, ataxia, personality changeOften attributed to fatigue or alcohol.
DCS Type II — inner earVertigo, tinnitus, hearing lossDistinguish from inner ear barotrauma — the treatments differ, and this needs specialist input.
DCS Type II — “chokes”Chest pain, dyspnea, cough — massive venous gas loadUncommon and serious.
DCS Type I — bendsDeep aching joint pain, typically shoulder or elbow, unrelieved by movementFull neurologic examination anyway — Type I and Type II coexist often.
Cutis marmorataMarbled, mottled violaceous rash, usually trunkNot a trivial skin finding. It is now regarded as a marker of substantial bubble load and serious DCS.

The dive history to take, and to hand over verbatim: maximum depth and bottom time; number of dives and surface intervals; ascent rate and any missed decompression stops; gas mixture; any rapid or uncontrolled ascent or breath-hold; the exact time of surfacing and the exact time of symptom onset; and any flying or altitude exposure afterwards. The onset interval is what separates AGE from DCS, and it is the detail most often lost.

03

Contraindications

  • Untreated pneumothorax — absolute. Gas trapped in the pleural space expands on decompression at the end of treatment and will tension. A chest tube goes in before the chamber door closes, and the drainage arrangement must be one the chamber team can manage under pressure. This is the hard gate on the whole pathway.
  • Relative — and rarely enough to withhold treatment for a serious injury: bleomycin and certain other chemotherapy agents (pulmonary toxicity), disulfiram, uncontrolled seizure disorder, severe COPD with bullae and CO2 retention, untreated middle ear or sinus pathology preventing equalization, and claustrophobia — which a multiplace chamber with an attendant usually accommodates.
  • Pregnancy is a relative consideration for elective indications; for DCS and AGE it is not a barrier.
  • None of these delays 100% oxygen, fluids, or the phone call.
04

Equipment and the Phone Numbers

  • A tight-fitting non-rebreather with a reservoir, or a demand valve. The distinction matters — a loosely applied mask delivers perhaps 60–70% oxygen, and the treatment depends on the inspired fraction being as close to 100% as you can manage. Check the seal and the reservoir.
  • IV access and isotonic crystalloid. Divers are dehydrated by immersion diuresis and DCS causes capillary leak. Avoid glucose-containing fluid in suspected neurologic injury.
  • Continuous oximetry and cardiac monitoring.
  • A urinary catheter if there is any spinal involvement — retention is common and the bladder gets forgotten in a chamber.
  • Chest imaging or ultrasound to exclude pneumothorax before transfer.
  • The numbers, saved in advance: your nearest operational chamber, and the Divers Alert Network emergency line, +1 919-684-9111, which provides 24-hour diving medical consultation and knows which chambers are actually open.
  • A transport plan that does not gain altitude — see §06.

Not every chamber can take every patient.

  • A monoplace chamber holds one patient and limits what can be done to them inside;
  • a multiplace chamber is pressurized with an attendant inside and can manage a ventilated, unstable, or critically ill patient.

An intubated patient needs a multiplace chamber, and many chambers are elective-hours only.

Confirm capability on the phone before committing to a destination.

05

What to Do, in Order

1
100% oxygen now — before anything is arranged

The single highest-yield intervention available to you, free, immediate, and already in the room.

It shrinks bubbles and washes out nitrogen by the same mechanisms the chamber uses.

Start it while someone else makes the phone calls, and do not stop it because symptoms improve — improvement on oxygen confirms the diagnosis, it does not treat it.

2
Lie them flat — supine, not head-down

Trendelenburg is no longer recommended. The old rationale was to keep bubbles out of the cerebral circulation; the position worsens cerebral edema and raises intracranial pressure, and the bubbles have already arrived.

Supine and flat.

The left lateral decubitus position belongs to venous air embolism trapping gas in the right ventricle — a different entity from arterial gas embolism.

3
Fluids

Isotonic crystalloid, generously — orally if fully alert, intravenously otherwise.

Hemoconcentration worsens bubble-related tissue injury, and these patients are dry before they are ever injured.

4
Document a full neurologic examination, with the time
  • Cranial nerves,
  • power,
  • sensation,
  • coordination,
  • gait if safe, and
  • bladder function.

Serial examinations are how response to treatment is judged, and without a recorded baseline the chamber team cannot tell whether the patient is improving.

This is worth the five minutes.

5
Exclude a pneumothorax — the hard gate

Imaging or ultrasound.

If present, it is drained before the chamber. Do not send an undrained pneumothorax to be pressurized.

6
Call the chamber and DAN, in parallel with everything above

Describe:

  • the presentation,
  • the dive profile,
  • the onset timing, and
  • what you have given.

Confirm the chamber is staffed, open, and able to take this specific patient — ventilated, or unstable, or not.

This call also gets you expert advice on a condition most clinicians see rarely.

7
Transport without gaining altitude

Ground transport by preference.

If air transport is unavoidable, the cabin must be pressurized to sea level, or the aircraft kept below about 1000 feet.

Altitude reduces ambient pressure, and by Boyle's law every bubble in the patient expands. This is the error that turns a treatable injury into a catastrophic one.

8
Hand over the profile, the times, and the examination

The hyperbaric team chooses the treatment table — commonly a US Navy Table 6 at 2.8 ATA for serious DCS and AGE, with shorter tables for resolved pain-only presentations, and repeat sessions daily until improvement plateaus.

That decision is theirs; the history and the serial examination are yours.

Late presentation is not a reason to withhold treatment

Recompression benefits patients who present hours or even days after the event, particularly with neurologic symptoms. A diver who flew home, or spent a day hoping the numbness would settle, is still a candidate. “It has been too long” is a misconception — make the call anyway and let the hyperbaric physician decide.

07

Complications of Treatment

  • Middle ear barotrauma — the most frequent, and a particular problem in the sedated or unconscious patient who cannot equalize.
  • Sinus squeeze and dental barotrauma.
  • CNS oxygen toxicity seizure — managed with an air break, self-limiting.
  • Pulmonary oxygen toxicity with repeated or prolonged exposures.
  • Pneumothorax converting to tension on decompression — the reason for the absolute contraindication.
  • Confinement anxiety, and reversible myopia with repeated treatment.
  • Hypoglycemia in diabetic patients during long tables.
  • Risk to the inside attendant in a multiplace chamber, who is also being exposed and has their own decompression obligation.
  • Fire. An oxygen-enriched pressurized environment is a serious fire risk, which is why chamber teams control absolutely everything that goes through the door.
08

Pearls

Oxygen first, logistics in parallel. It is the same treatment the chamber gives, at lower intensity, and it is already in the room.

Supine and flat. Trendelenburg is obsolete and harmful.

Symptoms within ten minutes of surfacing means arterial gas embolism. Later onset means decompression sickness. Both get recompressed.

Ask about urinary retention. Spinal DCS is the common serious form and the bladder is the sign people forget to look for.

Cutis marmorata is not a rash to reassure about.

Exclude a pneumothorax before the chamber. The only absolute gate.

Never gain altitude. Ground transport, or sea-level cabin pressure.

No nitrous oxide.

Improvement on oxygen is not a reason to cancel the chamber.

Late is still treatable. Days later, make the call anyway.

Save the numbers now — your regional chamber and DAN. Searching for a chamber during the emergency is a predictable and avoidable delay.
