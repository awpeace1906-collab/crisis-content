Resuscitative Endovascular Balloon Occlusion of the Aorta (REBOA)

Zone selection, balloon inflation, and the discipline of treating occlusion time as a hard clock. A bridge to hemorrhage control, not a treatment in itself.

01

Indications

  • Non-compressible torso hemorrhage — pelvic, junctional, or intra-abdominal — with shock refractory to initial resuscitation
  • Peri-arrest from suspected sub-diaphragmatic bleeding: SBP <70–80 mmHg despite product, or a patient losing their pulse in front of you with a mechanism that fits
  • Bridge to definitive control in a patient who will not survive transport, induction of anesthesia, or the walk to CT without proximal aortic control
  • An alternative to resuscitative thoracotomy in a patient who still has a rhythm and a blood pressure — it achieves aortic occlusion without opening the chest
  • Non-trauma uses: ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm as a bridge to repair, and refractory postpartum hemorrhage (Zone 3), where it is an established adjunct in some centers
Survival benefit is not established — this is a bridge, not a proven mortality intervention

The 2023 UK-REBOA trial randomized exsanguinating trauma patients to standard care with or without REBOA and found no survival benefit, with numerically higher mortality in the REBOA arm. Earlier support came almost entirely from registry data (AAST AORTA) and non-randomized comparisons, which carry real selection bias — the patients who die fastest never reach REBOA at all, which flatters its apparent outcomes.

None of this means REBOA has no role. It reliably and immediately raises proximal blood pressure, and that is sometimes exactly what is needed to reach an operating room.

But it belongs in centers with dedicated training, pre-positioned equipment, rapid femoral access, and a defined path to definitive control — not as a default first move. Treat it as a technique with a real physiologic effect and an unresolved outcome benefit.

02

Contraindications

  • Suspected thoracic aortic injury or dissection proximal to the intended zone. A balloon inflated in a dissected aorta can extend the dissection or rupture it.
  • Hemorrhage above the diaphragm. Occluding below a thoracic source does not control it — it raises the pressure driving it, and it costs you the minutes in which you might have found it. A chest that is bleeding gets a thoracostomy and a thoracotomy, not a balloon.
  • Cardiac arrest from a non-hemorrhagic cause. REBOA does not replace compressions, defibrillation, or reversing the cause.
  • Prolonged arrest with no signs of life and no realistic path to control — the same futility judgment that governs thoracotomy.
  • No obtainable common femoral access — bilateral vascular injury or destroyed groins.
  • No definitive control available. If there is no OR, no interventional radiology, and no transfer plan, the balloon buys ischemic time for no purpose. Do not inflate a balloon you have no plan to deflate.
  • Pediatric patients — not established; catheter and sheath sizes do not fit small vessels.
03

Equipment

  • REBOA catheter. Current low-profile systems (ER-REBOA and partial-occlusion variants) run through a 7 Fr sheath and carry an integrated distal pressure port — a significant advantage, because it lets you transduce proximally and distally without a second line. Older compliant occlusion balloons need 11–12 Fr, which changes the access and closure problem entirely.
  • Sheath matched to the catheter. Note the length — the sheath length counts against your insertion depth measurement.
  • Ultrasound with a linear probe. The access is the procedure; see §05.
  • Guidewire (0.035″ for most systems), and a stiffer exchange wire if you plan to upsize.
  • Two transducers — proximal (the catheter's own port, or a radial line) and distal (the sheath side-arm). The proximal/distal pressure pair is how you know the balloon is working, and it is what makes partial occlusion possible.
  • Saline for inflation — or dilute contrast if fluoroscopy is available. Never air. An air-filled balloon that ruptures embolizes air into the aorta and onward to brain and coronaries; a saline balloon that ruptures does nothing.
  • Syringes with fine graduations (a 20 mL syringe, ideally a Luer-lock) — balloon volumes are small and the difference between occlusion and over-inflation is a couple of milliliters.
  • Plain radiograph capability or fluoroscopy for position confirmation. A portable chest or abdominal film in the resuscitation bay is usually achievable and is worth taking.
  • A visible clock or timer, started at inflation. See §07.
  • Cutdown tray as the access backup — and as what you will need at the end to take the sheath out.
04

The Zones — and the One You Never Occlude

ZoneExtentUse forSafe dwell
Zone 1 (supraceliac)Origin of the left subclavian → celiac axisIntra-abdominal hemorrhage; peri-arrest from any sub-diaphragmatic source. The functional equivalent of a cross-clamp at thoracotomy.Aim ≤30 min; 15–20 is better. Every abdominal and pelvic organ plus both kidneys is downstream.
Zone 2 (visceral)Celiac axis → lowest renal arteryNothing. This is the “no-fly” zone. A balloon here occludes across the origins of the celiac, SMA, and renal arteries without the benefit of full proximal control.—
Zone 3 (infrarenal)Lowest renal artery → aortic bifurcationIsolated pelvic or junctional hemorrhage; postpartum hemorrhage. Spares the kidneys, gut, liver, and cord.Substantially longer — up to 60 min, and beyond with care. Only the pelvis and legs are ischemic.

Choosing between them.

  • Zone 3 is safer and should be used whenever the source is clearly pelvic — an unstable pelvic fracture with a binder on and a negative FAST.
  • Zone 1 is for an intra-abdominal source, an uncertain source in extremis, or a patient who is arresting.

Zone 1 buys more pressure at much higher cost.

Getting the depth. External measurement is the usual method in a resuscitation bay. Lay the catheter on the patient from the access site and read off the target landmark:

  • Zone 1: to the sternal notch / manubrium — roughly 45–50 cm in an adult
  • Zone 3: to the umbilicus (or just above it) — roughly 27–30 cm

Protocols vary in which landmark they cite, and body habitus shifts both.

Confirm the position radiographically whenever you can — on a plain film:

  • the balloon in Zone 1 should sit at the level of the mid-to-lower thoracic vertebrae, and
  • in Zone 3 below the renal arteries around L2–L3.

Treat the external measurement as the starting estimate and the pressure response as the functional check.

05

Technique

Access is the procedure. Everything that follows is quick; the femoral artery is where REBOA succeeds or fails.

1
Get the arterial line in before you need the balloon

The highest-value operational habit in REBOA has nothing to do with the balloon.

Place a common femoral arterial line early in any shocked trauma patient — it gives you real-time pressure you need anyway, and if REBOA becomes necessary it converts a difficult new puncture in a pulseless patient into a straightforward wire exchange and upsize.

Teams that do this reliably get balloons up in a few minutes; teams that don't spend those minutes hunting a femoral pulse that no longer exists.

2
Target the common femoral artery, under ultrasound

Puncture below the inguinal ligament but above the bifurcation into superficial and profunda femoris.

  • Too low: a sheath in the superficial femoral artery is too big for the vessel and produces limb ischemia and thrombosis.
  • Too high: above the ligament, a failed puncture bleeds into the retroperitoneum.

That is a narrow window, and in a pulseless patient ultrasound is the only way to find it — look for the vessel before it divides, image the bifurcation and come up from it.

3
If ultrasound access fails, cut down early

Set a limit in advance — two or three passes, or 90 seconds — and then convert to a femoral cutdown.

The commonest way REBOA fails is not a technical error at the balloon but ten minutes burned on percutaneous attempts in a vessel with no pressure in it.

Decide the escalation point before you start, out loud.

4
Sheath in, transduce the side-arm

Place the sheath over the wire.

Connect the side-arm to a transducer — this is your distal pressure, and it will be the thing that tells you the balloon is occluding and, later, the thing that lets you run partial occlusion.

5
Measure, then advance to the zone

Choose the zone (§04), measure externally from the access site, and mark the catheter.

Advance smoothly — it should pass with no resistance.

Resistance means the wire or catheter has found a branch vessel or a dissection plane. Stop, withdraw, reassess. Do not push through resistance in an aorta.

6
Inflate slowly, watching two numbers

Inflate with saline in 1–2 mL increments while watching the proximal pressure rise and the distal waveform flatten.

Typical volumes: Zone 1 ≈ 8–10 mL, Zone 3 ≈ 2–6 mL — but the endpoint is the pressure response, not the volume.

Stop at the minimum volume that achieves occlusion. Over-inflation risks aortic injury and rupture, and the aorta of a young exsanguinating trauma patient is small and empty.

7
Confirm, and start the clock out loud

Occlusion is confirmed by a rising proximal MAP and loss of the distal arterial waveform and femoral signal.

Then say it to the room: “Balloon up at 14:32, Zone 1. I will call it out every five minutes.”

Write it on the board.

The clock is the safety system — see §07.

8
Go to definitive control now

The moment the balloon is up, the only remaining question is where the bleeding gets stopped and how soon:

  • OR,
  • angiography suite,
  • pelvic packing,
  • preperitoneal packing.

Every minute of inflation should be a minute of movement toward that.

A stable patient with a balloon up is not a stable patient; they are an ischemic patient with a normal-looking monitor.

06

Partial and Intermittent Occlusion

Complete occlusion maximizes proximal pressure and maximizes distal ischemia. The refinement that has come out of the last decade is to give up some of the first to buy a great deal of the second.

  • Partial REBOA (pREBOA): deflate back from full occlusion until a small amount of distal flow returns — titrating to a target such as a proximal MAP around 80–90 mmHg, or a measurable distal systolic pressure. Distal perfusion continues at a reduced rate, the acid and potassium load accumulates more slowly, and the safe dwell extends substantially. This requires a distal transducer and continuous attention: a partial balloon drifts, and one that drifts closed is a full occlusion nobody is timing.
  • Intermittent REBOA (iREBOA): cycles of inflation and deflation. Simpler to do without a distal transducer, but each deflation produces a hemodynamic swing and can dislodge fresh clot. Generally the less favored of the two.
  • Purpose-built partial-occlusion catheters exist and make the titration far more controllable than throttling a standard compliant balloon by hand.

If your service is going to do REBOA at all, partial occlusion with distal pressure monitoring is the version most likely to do more good than harm — but it demands a dedicated person watching two pressures and nothing else.

07

The Clock, and Coming Down

Deflation is more dangerous than inflation, and it is the part people prepare for least.

Inflation time is the entire safety margin

Everything distal to the balloon is ischemic from the second it goes up, and the reperfusion injury is proportional to the dwell.

  • Zone 1: target under 30 minutes, and treat 15–20 as the aspiration.
  • Zone 3 is far more forgiving — up to an hour and sometimes beyond — because only the pelvis and legs are at stake.

Announce the time every five minutes. Nobody should be surprised at minute 30, and in practice they are, because in a busy resuscitation nobody owns the clock unless someone is told to.

Before you deflate, have all of this ready:

  • The surgeon present and the source controlled or controllable
  • Blood products running, not ordered
  • Vasopressors drawn up and connected
  • Calcium ready — the reperfusion washout carries potassium and acid, and calcium is both the antiarrhythmic and the inotrope for that moment
  • Consideration of sodium bicarbonate for the acid load
  • A current potassium if you can get one

Then deflate in 1–2 mL increments, over minutes, watching the pressure.

If the pressure falls, stop — and re-inflate if it collapses. You can go back up.

The washout of lactate, potassium, and inflammatory mediators from ischemic viscera and limbs can cause profound vasoplegia, arrhythmia, and arrest, and the magnitude scales with how long the balloon was up and how fast you let it down.

A fast deflation after a long Zone 1 occlusion can kill a patient whose bleeding you have just successfully stopped.

09

Complications

  • Ischemia–reperfusion injury — the dominant harm, proportional to dwell time and zone. Acute kidney injury, gut ischemia, hepatic injury, rhabdomyolysis, spinal cord ischemia with Zone 1, and a systemic inflammatory response that outlasts the procedure by days.
  • Limb ischemia, thrombosis, and amputation — from the sheath itself, from a superficial femoral puncture, or from the occlusion interval. Reported in a meaningful fraction of cases; the larger the sheath, the higher the rate.
  • Access-site complications — pseudoaneurysm, arteriovenous fistula, dissection, retroperitoneal hemorrhage from a high puncture.
  • Aortic injury or rupture from over-inflation, particularly in a small, empty, young aorta.
  • Balloon rupture or migration.
  • Reperfusion arrest on deflation — hyperkalemia, acidemia, and vasoplegia arriving together.
  • Compartment syndrome of the legs after reperfusion.
  • Missed proximal injury — the most consequential cognitive error. Occluding below an undiagnosed thoracic source delays the correct operation while the patient keeps bleeding.
  • Sheath removal problems — a 7–12 Fr arterial sheath is not pulled at the bedside and forgotten. It needs a closure device or surgical repair, and a plan made in advance.
10

After Deflation

  • The sheath needs an owner and a plan. Percutaneous closure device or open repair, by vascular or trauma surgery. Document who is responsible and when it comes out. Sheaths left in overnight because nobody owned them are a recognized source of limb loss.
  • Hourly limb assessment — pulses (Doppler if not palpable), color, temperature, capillary refill, and compartment tension in both legs. An ankle-brachial index once the patient is stable.
  • Serial potassium, lactate, creatinine, CK, and blood gas. Hyperkalemia and rhabdomyolysis are expected after a substantial ischemic interval, not incidental findings.
  • Urine output and color — pigmented urine means myoglobin; start fluids and follow CK.
  • Record the occlusion interval precisely — zone, inflation time, deflation time, partial-occlusion periods. Every downstream team needs this number and it cannot be reconstructed later.
  • Watch for delayed gut and cord injury after Zone 1 — abdominal examination, lactate trend, and a neurologic assessment of the legs once sedation allows. Paraplegia after Zone 1 occlusion is rare but real.
11

Pearls

Put a femoral arterial line in early. The single change that makes REBOA achievable is having the access already there, so the balloon is an upsize and not a puncture.

If the proximal pressure doesn't rise, suspect a chest. A correctly placed, properly inflated balloon raises pressure. When it doesn't, the most important possibility is that you are occluding below the bleeding.

Zone 3 whenever the source is pelvic. It costs you less pressure and buys a far longer safe window.

Zone 2 is a no-fly zone. All of the visceral cost, none of the proximal benefit.

Saline, never air. A ruptured saline balloon is an inconvenience; a ruptured air balloon is an arterial air embolism.

Inflate to the pressure, not to the volume. Stop at the least volume that occludes. The published volumes are a range, not a target.

Own the clock out loud. Zone 1 under 30 minutes, called every five. This is a communication task, and it is the one that fails.

Deflation needs more preparation than inflation. Blood up, pressors connected, calcium drawn, surgeon present, and come down a milliliter at a time.

Cut down early rather than probe long. Set the escalation point before you start.

Somebody owns the sheath. Name them before you leave the resuscitation bay.

A balloon buys time and nothing else. The moment it is up, the only meaningful activity in the room is getting to definitive control.
