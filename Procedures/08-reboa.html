Resuscitative Endovascular Balloon Occlusion of the Aorta (REBOA)

Zone selection, balloon inflation, and the discipline of treating occlusion time as a hard clock. A bridge to hemorrhage control, not a treatment in itself.

VASCULAR ACCESS / CIRCULATORY

01

Indications

  • Non-compressible torso hemorrhage (pelvic, junctional, abdominal) with hemorrhagic shock refractory to initial resuscitation
  • Bridge to definitive hemorrhage control (OR, IR) in a patient too unstable to survive transport or induction without proximal aortic control
  • Peri-arrest or profound hypotension (SBP <70–80 mmHg) from suspected sub-diaphragmatic hemorrhage
  • Alternative to resuscitative thoracotomy in patients with a viable but rapidly deteriorating hemodynamic profile
Survival benefit is not established — this is a bridge, not a proven mortality intervention

The 2023 UK-REBOA trial (Jansen et al., JAMA) randomized exsanguinating trauma patients to standard care with or without REBOA and found no survival benefit from adding REBOA, with a numerically higher mortality in the REBOA arm. Earlier support for REBOA came almost entirely from registry data (AAST AORTA) and non-randomized comparisons, which carry real selection bias — sicker/faster-dying patients are less likely to reach REBOA at all, flattering its apparent outcomes. None of this means REBOA has no role: it still reliably raises proximal blood pressure as a temporizing bridge, and most guidance restricts it to centers with dedicated training, rapid access, and a clear path to definitive control — not as a default first move for non-compressible hemorrhage. Treat it as a technique with a real physiologic effect and an unresolved outcome benefit, not a settled standard of care.

02

Contraindications

  • Known or suspected thoracic aortic injury or dissection proximal to the zone of occlusion
  • Cardiac arrest from a non-hemorrhagic cause — REBOA does not substitute for compressions/defibrillation
  • Inability to obtain femoral arterial access (severe bilateral lower-extremity vascular injury)
  • Prolonged anticipated occlusion time without a clear path to definitive control (irreversible ischemia risk beyond ~30–60 min at Zone 1)
03

Equipment

  • REBOA catheter kit (7 Fr low-profile system where available, or 11–12 Fr standard system)
  • Ultrasound with linear probe for femoral access
  • Arterial sheath, guidewire, compliant occlusion balloon
  • Pressure monitoring line/transducer (proximal, and distal if possible)
  • Fluoroscopy or external landmark measurement for balloon positioning if fluoro unavailable
  • Saline for balloon inflation — never air
04

Stepwise Technique

1
Obtain common femoral artery access via ultrasound-guided Seldinger technique; confirm arterial waveform.
2
Place introducer sheath appropriate to catheter system.
3
Determine target zone: Zone 1 (left subclavian to celiac trunk) for suspected intra-abdominal hemorrhage; Zone 3 (lowest renal artery to aortic bifurcation) for isolated pelvic hemorrhage.
4
Measure catheter insertion length externally (xiphoid for Zone 1, umbilicus for Zone 3) or confirm with fluoroscopy if available.
5
Advance catheter to target zone.
6
Inflate balloon slowly with saline while monitoring proximal pressure rise and loss of distal pulse/waveform — stop inflation at the minimum volume achieving occlusion.
7
Confirm occlusion: rising proximal MAP, loss of distal femoral signal.
8
Document inflation time immediately; set a hard countdown for the team.
9
Proceed to definitive hemorrhage control (OR/IR) without delay — REBOA is a bridge, not a treatment.
10
Deflate gradually in a controlled setting, titrating to hemodynamics — rapid deflation risks reperfusion collapse.
Occlusion time is the whole safety margin

Zone 1 REBOA carries a real ischemia-reperfusion burden that climbs with dwell time — treat balloon inflation time as a hard countdown communicated out loud to the team, not a detail to check later.

05

Complications & Pitfalls

  • Ischemia-reperfusion injury distal to occlusion, worsening with time — minimize occlusion duration
  • Distal limb ischemia/thromboembolism from sheath or catheter
  • Balloon rupture or malposition — confirm zone before inflation
  • Rebound hypotension on deflation — deflate incrementally with volume/pressor support ready
  • Missed proximal (thoracic) injury — occluding downstream of an undiagnosed thoracic injury will not control hemorrhage and delays recognition
  • Vessel injury/dissection from access or catheter advancement
06

Pearls

Zone selection matters more than perfect technique — get the level right before worrying about elegance.

Treat occlusion time like a ticking clock out loud to the team; nobody should be surprised at minute 30.

REBOA buys time, it doesn't fix anything — the moment it's inflated, the next question is where definitive control is happening and how fast.

If ultrasound-guided access is failing under time pressure, don't burn the clock — escalate to cutdown or your backup access plan early.
