Retrograde Intubation

A backup technique for the difficult airway when fiberoptic and video laryngoscopy aren't available or aren't working — a wire from below guiding the tube from above.

AIRWAY
⚡ TECHNIQUE Indications Contraindications Equipment Stepwise Technique Troubleshooting & Failure Modes Complications & Pitfalls Pearls References
01

Indications

  • Difficult airway where laryngoscopy and video-assisted techniques have failed or are unavailable
  • Blood or secretions obscuring the view — the one situation where this technique has a genuine edge, because it does not depend on seeing anything
  • Limited mouth opening that prevents a rigid scope, where the cricothyroid membrane is still accessible
  • Resource-limited settings without fiberoptic or video laryngoscopy
  • A patient who is maintaining oxygenation while you work — this is a difficult-airway technique, not a rescue one
Know where this sits on the ladder. Slower than video laryngoscopy, slower than fiberoptic, and far slower than a scalpel. It belongs to the difficult-but-not-yet-critical airway in a patient who is still oxygenating. If the patient is desaturating and you cannot oxygenate, this is the wrong page — go to Cricothyrotomy.
02

Contraindications

  • True CICO. Absolutely. This technique takes minutes you do not have; go directly to a front-of-neck airway.
  • Distorted or unidentifiable anterior neck anatomy preventing safe membrane puncture
  • Coagulopathy, or infection/tumor overlying the puncture site (relative)
  • Any faster appropriate option is available — retrograde intubation is a backup, and choosing it over something quicker is itself the error
03

Equipment

  • Introducer needle (18 ga or the needle from a percutaneous cricothyrotomy/central line kit), with a saline-filled syringe so tracheal air shows as bubbles
  • A long wire — this is the critical spec. You need enough length to span from the neck, up through the larynx, out of the mouth, and still have the tube railroaded over it. A standard 45–50 cm central line wire is marginal; a long (≥110 cm) J-wire or an epidural catheter is what you actually want.
  • Magill forceps and a laryngoscope for retrieving the wire from the pharynx
  • An airway exchange catheter or the Aintree-type catheter — strongly preferred over railroading the ETT directly (see the technique note below)
  • ETT, a size smaller than you would normally pick
  • Local anesthetic if the patient is awake, plus topicalization as for any awake technique
  • A clamp or hemostat to grip the wire at the neck — losing the wire into the trachea is a real and avoidable complication
04

Stepwise Technique

A wire from below, guiding a tube from above
1
Identify and stabilize the cricothyroid membrane
Laryngeal handshake, as for cricothyrotomy. Topicalize the skin and, if the patient is awake, the airway itself — a transtracheal injection of lidocaine at this point conveniently anesthetizes the very structures the wire is about to irritate.
2
Puncture the membrane aiming CEPHALAD
This is the one direction that is opposite to every other neck procedure. Cricothyrotomy, percutaneous tracheostomy, and transtracheal blocks all angle caudally, toward the carina. Here you angle cephalad, toward the head, because the wire has to travel up through the cords and out of the mouth. Confirm tracheal entry with free air bubbles into the saline-filled syringe before threading anything.
3
Thread the wire up and out
Advance the wire cephalad through the needle; it passes through the larynx and coils in the pharynx. Feed generously — you want plenty of length in the mouth. Then remove the needle over the wire, and immediately clamp the wire at the skin so it cannot migrate inward.
4
Retrieve the wire from the mouth
Laryngoscope in, Magill forceps to grab the wire and bring the end out through the mouth. If you cannot see it, a finger sweep will often hook it. Maintain tension at both ends from here on — one hand at the neck, one at the mouth. Slack is how wires get lost.
5
Pass a catheter over the wire — not the tube directly
Thread an airway exchange catheter over the oral end and advance it down to the larynx. This is the step that makes the technique work reliably: the catheter is stiff, small-calibre, and follows the wire faithfully, whereas an ETT bevel catches at the cords and the step-off between wire and tube is enormous. Advance the catheter until it meets the neck puncture site.
6
Release the wire from below and advance the catheter deeper
Unclamp and withdraw the wire out through the mouth while holding the catheter still. The catheter now sits in the trachea, but only as far as the cricothyroid membrane — so advance it further toward the carina before railroading. A catheter that stops at the membrane gives the tube almost nothing to track over.
7
Railroad the ETT, rotating counterclockwise if it hangs up
Advance the tube over the catheter with a gentle 90° counterclockwise rotation if it catches — the right arytenoid is the usual obstacle, exactly as in fiberoptic technique. Keep the catheter absolutely still.
8
Remove the catheter, confirm, secure, and dress the neck
Withdraw the catheter, inflate the cuff, confirm with capnography and auscultation, secure the tube. Then apply a dressing and light pressure to the puncture site and document that a transtracheal puncture was made — someone will otherwise be puzzled by subcutaneous air on a later film.
Cephalad here; caudad everywhere else

The needle direction is the most commonly inverted detail in this procedure, precisely because every other anterior-neck technique you know goes the other way. Angling caudally sends the wire toward the carina and the technique simply fails. Say the direction out loud before you puncture.

The wire is the procedure — never let both ends go

From the moment the wire is through the membrane until it is removed, one end is clamped or held at all times. A wire released at the neck can be pulled entirely into the trachea; a wire released at the mouth retracts and has to be retrieved again. Both are avoidable with a hemostat and a habit.

Complications & Pitfalls

  • Time cost — the defining drawback, and the reason it is the wrong choice in a deteriorating patient
  • Wire loss into the trachea, or loss of position during tube advancement
  • Failure at the glottic inlet, from railroading an ETT directly over a thin wire
  • Bleeding, subcutaneous emphysema, and rarely pneumothorax or pneumomediastinum
  • Esophageal or posterior tracheal wall puncture from an over-deep needle
  • Infection at the puncture site, particularly if the field was rushed
  • False reassurance — persisting with a technique that is not progressing, and thereby delaying the surgical airway the patient actually needed
07

Pearls

Cephalad. Say it out loud before you puncture. Every neighbouring procedure goes the other way.

Use an exchange catheter as an intermediate. Railroading a tube straight over a wire is where this technique earns its reputation for failing at the cords.

One end of the wire is always held. A hemostat at the neck costs nothing and prevents the worst complication.

Advance the catheter deeper before railroading — the guide otherwise only reaches the membrane, which is not deep enough to steer a tube.

Its niche is blood. When the view is obliterated and fiberoptics are useless but the patient is still oxygenating, this technique does something nothing else does.

Set a time limit before you start, and when you hit it, convert. You are already standing over an identified, anesthetized cricothyroid membrane — the fastest surgical airway of your career is one decision away.
