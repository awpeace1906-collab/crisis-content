Intraosseous Access

Proximal humerus, sternal, and tibial sites — the fastest reliable vascular access when peripheral IV attempts have failed or time doesn't allow for them.

VASCULAR ACCESS / CIRCULATORY
⚡ TECHNIQUE Indications Contraindications Equipment Complications & Pitfalls Pearls References
01

Indications

  • Emergent need for vascular access when peripheral IV access is difficult, delayed, or has failed
  • Cardiac arrest (any age)
  • Hemorrhagic or other shock states requiring immediate fluid/medication access
  • Any critically ill or injured patient where seconds of delay matter
02

Contraindications

  • Fracture at or proximal to the intended insertion site
  • Previous IO attempt or orthopedic hardware at the same site within the prior 24-48 hours
  • Infection (cellulitis) overlying the insertion site
  • Inability to identify landmarks confidently (relative — choose an alternate site)
03

Equipment

  • IO driver (manual or powered, e.g., spring-loaded or drill-based device)
  • IO needle appropriate to site and patient size
  • Chlorhexidine or available skin prep
  • Extension tubing, syringe for aspiration/flush
  • Lidocaine for conscious patients (both at the skin and injected into the marrow space before infusion)
04

Stepwise Technique

Proximal humerus
1
Position the arm adducted, internally rotated, with the hand resting on the abdomen (this exposes the greater tubercle landmark).
2
Palpate the surgical neck of the humerus, approximately 1-2 cm above it for the insertion point.
3
Insert the IO needle perpendicular to the bone surface, driving through the cortex until a give/pop is felt.
4
Remove the stylet, confirm placement (needle stands firmly without support, marrow aspirate if attempted, free flow of flush without extravasation).
5
Secure and connect to extension tubing.
Sternal
6
Identify the manubrium as the insertion site (device-specific — sternal IO devices use a dedicated targeting mechanism).
7
Follow the specific device protocol for insertion depth and technique.
8
Confirm placement per device indicator and flush test.
Tibial
9
Identify the anteromedial tibial surface, approximately 1-2 cm below and medial to the tibial tuberosity in adults (adjust per pediatric landmarks).
10
Insert perpendicular to the bone, driving through the cortex until loss of resistance.
11
Remove stylet, confirm placement, secure and connect to extension tubing.
Confirm placement before infusing

A needle that doesn't stand firmly on its own, or a flush that causes visible swelling, means stop and reassess — extravasation into a closed compartment is the complication that turns a good access decision into a bad outcome.

05

Complications & Pitfalls

  • Extravasation from failure to fully penetrate the cortex or from a through-and-through insertion — monitor the limb for swelling during infusion
  • Compartment syndrome from unrecognized extravasation
  • Fracture, especially in pediatric or osteoporotic bone with excessive force
  • Infection/osteomyelitis with prolonged dwell time — convert to standard IV access as soon as feasible
  • Pain on infusion in conscious patients — pre-treat with intraosseous lidocaine before fluids/medications
06

Pearls

IO access is not inferior to IV for emergency medication and fluid administration — treat it as a full-status line, not a placeholder.

The proximal humerus site allows faster flow rates than tibial sites and is preferred when rapid volume resuscitation is the goal.

Always confirm placement before infusing — a needle that doesn't stand firmly on its own, or flush that causes visible swelling, means reassess before continuing.

Convert to standard IV/central access as soon as the situation allows; IO is a bridge, not a destination line.
