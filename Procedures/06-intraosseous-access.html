Intraosseous Access

Proximal humerus, sternal, and tibial sites — the fastest reliable vascular access when peripheral IV attempts have failed or time doesn't allow for them.

01

Indications

  • Cardiac arrest, any age, when a peripheral IV is not already running
  • Shock of any cause where access is the rate-limiting step — hemorrhagic, septic, anaphylactic, cardiogenic
  • Two failed peripheral attempts, or a patient in whom you can see at a glance that attempts will fail (burns, IV drug use, obesity, profound vasoconstriction, hypothermia)
  • Status epilepticus, status asthmaticus, hypoglycemia with altered mental status — time-critical drug delivery in a moving target
  • Pre-hospital and austere settings where ultrasound-guided peripheral or central access is not available
  • As a bridge during RSI when you need induction drugs now and the line you have is unreliable
What the 2025 trials actually showed

Two large randomized trials of out-of-hospital arrest — PARAMEDIC-3 and IVIO — found that an IO-first strategy did not improve survival over an IV-first strategy, and PARAMEDIC-3 found lower rates of ROSC in the IO-first arm.

This does not make IO a bad route. It means IO is the answer to “I cannot get a vein,” not a shortcut to be reached for reflexively when a vein is available.

  • If a peripheral IV will be in within about 90 seconds, place the IV.
  • If it won't, drill.
02

Contraindications

Absolute — pick a different bone:

  • Fracture in that bone, at or proximal to the site. Infusate leaks out the fracture; a tibial IO above a tibial fracture perfuses the hematoma, not the patient.
  • Previous IO attempt in the same bone within 48 hours, successful or not. The old cortical hole is a vent — the new line will run straight out of it into the soft tissue. Cross to the other side or another site.
  • Orthopedic hardware, prosthetic joint, or prior surgery at the site (knee replacement, IM nail, shoulder arthroplasty).
  • Infection, burn, or cellulitis over the insertion point. Seeding the marrow through infected skin produces osteomyelitis. Burned skin over an otherwise good site is not an absolute bar in extremis — but choose unburned skin if any exists.

Relative:

  • Osteogenesis imperfecta, osteopetrosis, severe osteoporosis — fragile or sclerotic bone. Osteopetrotic bone may simply not be penetrable.
  • Landmarks you cannot confidently palpate. Body habitus that defeats palpation is a reason to change sites or use ultrasound, not a reason to guess. A blind drill into soft tissue is the commonest cause of a failed IO.
  • Sternal IO: add prior sternotomy, sternal fracture, and small children to the exclusion list.
03

Equipment — Opening the Kit

A powered driver with the wrong needle in it fails exactly like no driver at all.

  • Powered driver (battery drill-type) or manual driver. Check the battery indicator at the start of any shift you might need it. A driver that stalls partway through the cortex leaves a needle you must remove and a site you can no longer use.
  • Needle sets — three lengths, and the length is the decision. The color codes below are the widely used EZ-IO convention; confirm against the set in your own kit.
    • 15 mm (pink): 3–39 kg
    • 25 mm (blue): ≥3 kg — the adult tibial default
    • 45 mm (yellow): ≥40 kg, or any site with heavy overlying tissue. The proximal humerus in an adult is a 45 mm needle, always — the deltoid is thicker than people estimate, and a 25 mm needle in a humerus is the single most common cause of an IO that “placed fine” and then infiltrated.
  • Chlorhexidine or whatever prep is within reach. In arrest this is one pass, not a scrub.
  • Extension set with a Luer lock, primed. Prime it — pushing the air column in the tubing into the marrow first is both wasted time and extra pain.
  • Two 10 mL syringes: one of normal saline for the flush, one for aspiration.
  • Preservative-free 2% lidocaine (cardiac lidocaine) for any patient with a pulse and any perception. This is not optional comfort care — see §09.
  • Pressure bag or infusion pump. Gravity through an IO delivers roughly a milliliter a minute. Without pressure you have access that does nothing.
  • Securement dressing (the winged stabilizer in the kit, or a bulky dressing and tape).
The 5 mm rule

Seat the needle through the skin until the tip touches bone, then look at the hub. At least one black depth marking must be visible above the skin.

If no line shows, the needle is too short to cross the cortex and still sit in the marrow — swap up a size before you pull the trigger.

Checking this takes two seconds and prevents the failure you won't detect until you flush.

Choosing the Site

SiteFlow (pressurized)Choose it whenWatch for
Proximal humerus~5 L/hrVolume resuscitation; drugs you want centrally fast; awake patients (least painful site)Displacement with any arm movement — secure the arm to the torso. Not during unmechanized CPR on that side.

The default pairing worth committing to memory: humerus for flow and speed to the central circulation, tibia for a moving resuscitation. Humeral flow is roughly five times tibial, and drug transit to the central compartment is faster — which matters for adenosine and for arrest drugs.

But the humeral needle sits in a bone attached to a limb that everyone in the room will move, and it dislodges.

In a running code with manual compressions, the tibia is usually the right call: nobody is near it and nothing displaces it. If you place a humeral IO in that setting, tape the arm down across the abdomen and tell the team it is there.

05

Proximal Humerus — The One People Miss

Almost every humeral failure is a positioning failure, and it happens before the drill comes out.

1
Position the arm — this is the procedure

Adduct the arm tight to the chest wall, then internally rotate so the palm rests on the umbilicus and the elbow points posteriorly.

If you can, have someone hold it.

Internal rotation is what swings the greater tubercle anteriorly into your target line and moves it clear of the acromion. Without it you are drilling into the joint capsule or the deltoid.

2
Find the golf ball on the tee

Place the heel of your hand on the anterior shoulder and slide your thumb up the anterior humeral shaft.

You will feel the shaft narrow — that notch is the surgical neck — and then a broad, round prominence above it. That prominence is the greater tubercle: the ball sitting on the tee.

The target is the most prominent point of that ball, roughly 1–2 cm above the surgical neck.

If you never felt a narrowing, you have not found the neck, and your landmark is a guess.

3
Angle 45° down and back

Enter perpendicular to the surface of the tubercle, which means aiming roughly 45° caudad (toward the elbow) and slightly posteromedial.

Straight-in aiming at the ceiling walks you into the joint space.

Think of the needle as pointing at the opposite hip.

4
Push to bone, then check the line

Push the needle through the skin and soft tissue manually until the tip stops on bone.

Now look: at least one black depth line must be showing. In an adult humerus this should be a 45 mm needle.

Only then squeeze the trigger.

5
Let the drill do the work — then feel the change

Apply steady, gentle axial pressure and let the driver cut. Leaning on it stalls the motor.

You are drilling through a thicker cortex than the tibia, so it takes longer and the “pop” is less obvious.

The endpoint is a sudden loss of resistance and a change in the driver's pitch as it unloads, or the hub reaching the skin.

Stop there. Continuing drives the needle out the far cortex.

6
Uncouple, unscrew, secure
  1. Hold the hub and pull the driver straight off — do not let it torque the needle.
  2. Remove the stylet by turning counterclockwise.
  3. Attach the primed extension set.
  4. Then tape the arm to the torso.

A humeral IO not secured to the body has an hourly dislodgement rate that makes it unreliable exactly when you stop watching it.

06

Proximal Tibia — Adult and Pediatric

1
Find the tuberosity, then move off it

Run a finger up the shin to the bony bump below the patella — the tibial tuberosity. You are not drilling into it.

  • Adult: about 2 cm medial and 1 cm proximal to the tuberosity, on the flat anteromedial face of the tibia.
  • Child: about 1 cm medial and 1–2 cm distal — distal, to stay clear of the proximal tibial physis.
  • In an infant, one finger-breadth below and medial.
2
Support the knee, don't suspend the leg

Put a rolled towel under the knee so it rests in slight flexion on a solid surface.

A leg that bounces on the mattress absorbs the drill and turns a clean insertion into a wandering one.

3
Perpendicular — 90° to the flat surface

Unlike the humerus, the tibial approach is straight in, 90° to the flat medial surface.

Angling drags the needle toward the thin cortex and out the side.

4
Drill to the give

The tibial cortex is thin and the pop is distinct — often the first clear tactile landmark a trainee ever gets.

Stop at the pop.

In a child the cortex is thinner still and the marrow cavity is shallow; a through-and-through insertion is the classic pediatric error. Feather the trigger rather than holding it down.

5
Stylet out, extension on, palpate the calf

After the flush, put your hand behind the calf.

The tibia extravasates posteriorly where you cannot see it, and the first sign of a failed tibial IO is often a firm posterior compartment rather than a visible bulge.

Alternate sites

Distal femur (pediatric): leg straight, patella identified. Insert 1 cm proximal to the superior border of the patella and 1–2 cm medial to the midline, aiming straight back (slightly cephalad if anything). Wide target, thick marrow, forgiving — a good choice in a small child when the tibia has failed.

Distal tibia: 2 cm proximal to the most prominent point of the medial malleolus, on the flat medial surface, perpendicular. Thin cortex — easy to over-drill.

Sternum: device-specific (FAST1 / FASTResponder and similar), targeting the manubrium about 1.5 cm below the sternal notch via the device's own locating collar. Fixed depth, no freehand angle to judge. Do not improvise a sternal IO with an extremity needle.

07

Confirming You Are in the Marrow

Four checks, in order. Only one of them is decisive.

  • 1. The needle stands unsupported. Let go. A correctly seated IO is held rigidly by cortical bone and stands like a flagpole. One that wobbles, leans, or falls is in soft tissue. This is a fast and powerful negative test.
  • 2. Loss of resistance was felt. Corroborative, not proof — in the humerus it can be subtle, and in osteoporotic bone it can be absent entirely.
  • 3. Marrow aspirates. Nice when it happens. Its absence proves nothing — a substantial fraction of correctly placed IOs never yield aspirate, particularly in arrest. Do not pull a good needle because nothing came back.
  • 4. The flush runs without swelling. This is the test that decides. Push 10 mL of saline briskly in an adult (2–5 mL in a child) while watching and feeling the limb. Free flow with no swelling, no boggy tissue, no resistance building under your thumb — you are in. Anything else means stop.
The flush is not a confirmation step — it is part of the insertion

The marrow cavity is a sponge of trabecular bone and fat, not an open tube.

Without a forceful flush the space does not open and nothing will infuse afterward, no matter how perfectly the needle sits.

The commonest complaint about IO access — “it's in but it won't run” — is nearly always an under-flushed line.

Push it hard, and push it again if flow is sluggish.

08

Lidocaine, Pressure, and What Can Go Through It

Lidocaine — the sequence matters more than the dose. The saline flush into a conscious patient's marrow is described by patients as among the worst pain in medicine. Lidocaine must go in before the flush, not after.

  • Adult: preservative-free 2% lidocaine, 40 mg (2 mL) slowly over about 120 seconds → let it dwell 60 seconds → flush 5–10 mL saline → a further 20 mg (1 mL) over 60 seconds.
  • Pediatric: 0.5 mg/kg, max 40 mg, same dwell-then-flush sequence.
  • Repeat doses are appropriate for ongoing pain during infusion. Account for the total in any systemic lidocaine ceiling.
  • This is for any patient with a pulse and any plausible perception. It is not indicated in arrest — and if they wake up, give it then.

Pressure. Gravity produces roughly 1 mL/min through an IO. Every infusion that needs to be fast needs a pressure bag at 300 mmHg or a pump.

If a resuscitation is running slowly through an IO, check the pressure bag before you blame the needle.

What can go through it: everything.

  • Crystalloid, blood and all components,
  • vasopressors, induction and paralytic agents,
  • antibiotics, TXA, sodium bicarbonate, hypertonic saline,
  • contrast for CT.

Onset and peak levels approximate IV. There is no drug class that is IV-only.

The proximal humerus is preferred for adenosine and other agents whose effect depends on a tight, fast bolus.

Labs from the aspirate: the first aspirate can be sent for type and screen, chemistry, lactate and blood gas, and correlates acceptably for most.

It is unreliable for platelet and white cell counts, and marrow will clog an automated analyzer.

Call the lab and the blood bank before you send it — some will not accept it, and you need to know that before it is the only sample you have.

Complications

  • Extravasation → compartment syndrome. The complication that matters, and it is essentially always a recognition failure rather than a technique failure. The tibia is the highest-risk site because leakage tracks posteriorly and stays invisible. Any IO limb gets a documented baseline and scheduled re-checks while it is running.
  • Through-and-through penetration — classically the pediatric tibia, from holding the trigger through the marrow cavity and out the far cortex. Presents as a needle that stands but will not flush.
  • Fracture — excess force in osteoporotic or pediatric bone, or a manual needle levered sideways.
  • Osteomyelitis — well under 1%, and rises with dwell time and with insertion through non-prepped or infected skin.
  • Growth plate injury — the reason pediatric tibial landmarks sit distal to the tuberosity and femoral landmarks sit proximal to the patella.
  • Fat and marrow embolization — demonstrable, but not associated with clinically meaningful harm in humans. It is not a reason to withhold or under-flush an IO.
  • Skin necrosis from vesicant or vasopressor extravasation into soft tissue — a downstream consequence of a missed infiltration, not a separate event.

Dwell Time and Removal

24 hours is the ceiling under most manufacturer instructions, and sooner is better. IO is a bridge.

Once the patient is stabilized and a peripheral or central line is in, the IO comes out — leaving it “just in case” accumulates infection risk for access you are not using.

Removal:

  1. Attach a Luer-lock syringe to the hub (a slip-tip will pull off).
  2. Rotate clockwise while drawing straight out along the needle's axis.
  3. Do not rock, lever, or bend. Side-loading can shear the needle and leave a fragment in the bone — a problem that becomes an orthopedic consult.
  4. Dress the site; it will ooze a little.

Document: site, needle size, time in, time out, lidocaine given, and the limb assessment. If the patient goes to CT or the OR, tell the receiving team the IO is there — humeral needles under a gown are easy to miss and easy to knock out.

12

Pearls

Flush like you mean it. If you remember one thing: the marrow cavity does not open until you force it open. Almost every “the IO doesn't work” complaint is an under-flushed line.

The needle stands up or it isn't in. Letting go of the hub is a free, instant, highly specific test, and it is the one people skip in a hurry.

No aspirate is not a failure. More correctly placed IOs yield nothing back than most clinicians expect. Confirm with the flush, not the syringe.

The humerus needs the long needle and the right rotation. Hand on the belly, 45° toward the elbow, 45 mm needle. Get those three and the humerus is the best line in the room; miss any one and it fails quietly.

Secure the arm. A humeral IO not taped to the torso will be pulled out by someone doing something reasonable.

Pressure bag, every time. Access without pressure is not resuscitation.

Never re-drill the same bone. Within 48 hours, the previous hole vents everything you put in. Cross to the other side.

IO is not a second-class line. Every drug, blood product, and contrast agent that goes IV goes IO. Treat it as full access — and then replace it within 24 hours.
