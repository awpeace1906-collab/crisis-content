Transcutaneous & Transvenous Pacing

From pads on the chest to a wire in the right ventricle — bridging unstable bradycardia to a definitive rhythm.

CARDIAC / NEURO
⚡ TECHNIQUE Indications Contraindications Equipment Complications & Pitfalls Pearls References
01

Indications

  • Symptomatic bradycardia unresponsive to atropine and/or refractory to initial pharmacologic management
  • High-grade AV block (Mobitz II, third-degree) with hemodynamic instability
  • Bradycardic peri-arrest rhythms where pacing is part of the resuscitation algorithm
  • Transvenous pacing specifically indicated when transcutaneous pacing fails to capture, is not tolerated, or a more durable/reliable pacing solution is needed pending definitive management
02

Contraindications

  • Severe hypothermia with bradycardia (pacing is generally ineffective and not recommended until rewarming, per most protocols — the heart is often refractory to pacing at low core temperatures)
  • Transvenous pacing: coagulopathy or inability to obtain safe central venous access (relative, weighed against the urgency of the indication)
03

Equipment

Transcutaneous pacing

  • Cardiac monitor/defibrillator with pacing capability
  • Pacing pads (anterior-posterior placement preferred)
  • Sedation/analgesia for a conscious patient — transcutaneous pacing is uncomfortable

Transvenous pacing

  • Central venous access kit (internal jugular or subclavian preferred for lead stability)
  • Transvenous pacing wire/catheter
  • External pacing generator
  • Fluoroscopy or ECG-guided placement confirmation if available
04

Stepwise Technique

Transcutaneous pacing
1
Apply pacing pads in the anterior-posterior position for optimal capture.
2
Set the pacer to demand mode (synchronized to the patient's intrinsic rhythm) unless asystole, in which case use fixed/asynchronous mode.
3
Set the initial rate (typically 60-80 bpm as a starting target).
4
Increase output (mA) gradually from a low starting point until electrical capture is confirmed — a paced QRS complex following each pacing spike, correlating with a palpable pulse.
5
Set the final output slightly above the capture threshold for a safety margin.
6
Provide sedation/analgesia promptly, as transcutaneous pacing causes significant discomfort in an awake patient.
7
Confirm mechanical capture (palpable pulse corresponding to the paced rate), not just electrical capture on the monitor.
Transvenous pacing
8
Obtain central venous access, typically right internal jugular or left subclavian for the most direct path to the right ventricle.
9
Advance the pacing wire through the introducer sheath toward the right ventricle, using ECG guidance (injury pattern on the pacing wire's intracardiac ECG) or fluoroscopy if available.
10
Connect the wire to the external pacing generator once positioned.
11
Set output and sensitivity, confirm capture (paced rhythm on the monitor with correlating pulse).
12
Secure the wire and generator, obtain a confirmatory chest radiograph for lead position.
Electrical capture is not mechanical capture

Always confirm a palpable pulse correlating with the paced rhythm before considering the patient stabilized — pacing spikes on the monitor without a pulse behind them is still pulseless electrical activity.

05

Complications & Pitfalls

  • Failure to capture — reassess pad placement/wire position and increase output as needed
  • Electrical capture without mechanical capture (pulseless electrical activity despite pacing spikes) — always confirm a palpable pulse, not just the monitor tracing
  • Significant discomfort with transcutaneous pacing if sedation is inadequate
  • Lead perforation, arrhythmia, or cardiac tamponade with transvenous wire placement
  • Lead dislodgement with patient movement if not adequately secured
06

Pearls

Electrical capture on the monitor is not the same as effective pacing — always confirm a palpable pulse correlating with the paced rhythm before considering the patient stabilized.

Transcutaneous pacing is a bridge while transvenous access is arranged in most non-transient cases — don't treat it as a definitive solution if the bradycardia is expected to persist.

Sedate early for transcutaneous pacing in an awake patient; this is one of the more uncomfortable interventions in the resuscitation toolkit.
