Targeted Temperature Management Initiation

Post-ROSC neuroprotection — starting the cooling/normothermia protocol in the critical early window after return of spontaneous circulation.

01

Indications

  • Comatose after ROSC from cardiac arrest — not following commands, no purposeful response to voice. Out-of-hospital or in-hospital, shockable or non-shockable initial rhythm.
  • The practical test is the motor response: a patient who localizes to voice or follows a command does not need this. Everyone else does.
  • Start as soon as ROSC is established and the patient is stabilized — airway, hemodynamics, and a 12-lead first, then temperature.
What the trials actually changed

The field has moved, and the entry point has moved with it. TTM (2013) found 33 °C no better than 36 °C. TTM2 (2021) found 33 °C no better than normothermia with active fever prevention. HYPERION (2019) suggested possible benefit at 33 °C specifically in non-shockable rhythms. The result is that current guidance has shifted from “cool everyone to 33” to “actively prevent fever in everyone, and 33 remains a permitted option.”

The consensus that survived all of it: continuous core temperature monitoring and active prevention of fever above about 37.5 °C for at least 72 hours. That is the part not to get wrong. Post-arrest fever is independently associated with worse neurologic outcome, and it is common, and it is preventable.

02

Contraindications

  • Following commands after ROSC. Not indicated — though fever avoidance still applies.
  • Goals of care that do not support ongoing intensive treatment.
  • Major uncontrolled hemorrhage — a reason to choose strict normothermia rather than 33 °C, since hypothermia impairs platelet function and enzyme kinetics. Rarely a reason to permit fever.
  • Sepsis, recent major surgery, or established coagulopathy — the same reasoning: target higher, not warmer than normal.
  • Already hypothermic on arrival — do not actively rewarm to a target. Hypothermic arrest is a different pathway entirely and drowning or environmental exposure changes the prognosis.
03

Equipment

  • A device with closed-loop feedback — surface pads or an intravascular catheter. Both work. Feedback control is what keeps you at target instead of oscillating, and manual ice packs cannot do it.
  • Continuous core temperature probe. Esophageal is the most accurate and most responsive; bladder is acceptable but lags badly when urine output is low; rectal lags. Tympanic and axillary are not adequate for this. Two sites is better than one, because a single failed probe is otherwise invisible.
  • Sedation and analgesia, titratable and short-acting where possible.
  • Neuromuscular blockade available — but as the last anti-shivering step, not the first.
  • Continuous EEG, or at least access to it. Essential if the patient is paralyzed.
  • Arterial line, and frequent electrolytes and glucose.
  • What not to use: cold intravenous fluid boluses in the prehospital setting. Trials of prehospital cold saline showed increased pulmonary edema and re-arrest without benefit. Cool in hospital, with a device.
04

Shivering — the Thing That Defeats This

Shivering raises metabolic rate and oxygen consumption sharply, defeats the target, and is treated in the wrong order almost everywhere.

Assess it formally and repeatedly using a structured scale — the Bedside Shivering Assessment Scale grades it 0 (none) to 3 (generalized). Shivering is often missed because it starts in the masseter and neck and can be felt on the jaw or chest before it is visible.

Escalate in this order:

  • 1. Counterwarm the skin. Warm the face, hands and feet with a forced-air blanket while cooling the core. This is the most underused intervention in temperature management: shivering thresholds are driven substantially by skin temperature, so warming the periphery raises the threshold and lets you cool the core with far less sedation.
  • 2. Acetaminophen, plus buspirone — both lower the shivering threshold and are non-sedating.
  • 3. Magnesium infusion to a level around 3–4 mg/dL — lowers the threshold and is generally well tolerated.
  • 4. Opioids — fentanyl, or meperidine, which lowers the shivering threshold more than any other opioid but accumulates a proconvulsant metabolite.
  • 5. Sedation — propofol or dexmedetomidine.
  • 6. Neuromuscular blockade — last. It abolishes shivering without treating it, and it does something worse: it masks seizures. A paralyzed post-arrest patient needs continuous EEG, and if you cannot provide that, think hard before paralyzing.

Running It

1
Stabilize first, then cool
Airway secured, hemodynamics supported, 12-lead done and the decision made about urgent coronary angiography. Temperature control is important and it is not the first thing.
2
Core probe in before the device goes on
Esophageal where possible. Record the starting temperature — many post-arrest patients arrive spontaneously hypothermic, and knowing that changes what the device needs to do.
3
Set the target your institution uses, and reach it
Somewhere in the 32–37.5 °C band depending on local protocol — commonly strict normothermia at 36–37.5 °C, or 33 °C where that is the chosen strategy. Choosing a number is less important than holding it: the failure that matters is drifting into fever.
4
Get ahead of shivering
Counterwarm the skin from the start. Assess formally every hour. Escalate up the ladder in §04 rather than reaching for paralysis.
5
Maintain for 24 hours, and watch the chemistry
Electrolytes and glucose every 4–6 hours. Potassium at the lower end of normal, deliberately. Continuous EEG if paralyzed, and a low threshold for it otherwise — non-convulsive seizures after arrest are common and invisible.
6
Rewarm slowly — no faster than 0.25–0.5 °C per hour
Use the device to control the rate; do not simply switch it off. This is the most dangerous phase. Expect hyperkalemia, hypoglycemia, and vasodilatory hypotension arriving together. Stop potassium replacement before you start, reduce insulin as you go, and have volume and a pressor available.
7
Then keep them out of fever for at least 72 hours
Leave the device on in normothermia mode. Rebound hyperthermia after rewarming is extremely common and is the part everyone stops watching for, because the protocol feels finished. It is not.
07

The Rest of Post-Arrest Care

Temperature is one element of a bundle, and the others are what the patient is most likely to be harmed by in the first hours.

  • Oxygen: avoid hypoxia and hyperoxia. Titrate to SpO2 92–98%. Leaving the FiO2 at 1.0 after ROSC is a real and common harm.
  • Ventilation: normocapnia, PaCO2 35–45 mmHg. Hyperventilation causes cerebral vasoconstriction; hypercapnia raises intracranial pressure.
  • Blood pressure: MAP ≥65 mmHg, and many units target 80–100 in the hope of better cerebral perfusion through impaired autoregulation. Avoid hypotension above all.
  • Coronary angiography: immediately for ST elevation. For patients without ST elevation and no shock, early routine angiography has not shown benefit, so it becomes a case-by-case decision.
  • Glucose: roughly 140–180 mg/dL. Avoid hypoglycemia more than you chase tight control.
  • Seizures: treat electrographic seizures. Distinguish myoclonic status from post-hypoxic myoclonus — they are not prognostically equivalent and the distinction needs EEG.
  • Neuroprognostication: not before 72 hours after return to normothermia, and not while sedation is on board. Hypothermia and accumulated drug both suppress the examination. Use multiple modalities — clinical examination, bilaterally absent N20 on SSEP, EEG pattern, neuron-specific enolase, and imaging — and never a single test. Early pessimistic prognostication is a self-fulfilling prophecy, and it remains one of the commonest serious errors in post-arrest care.

09

Pearls

Prevent fever. If only one thing survives from this page, it is that — it is the element every trial still supports, and it lasts 72 hours.

Holding the target matters more than which target. Drift into fever is the failure; the exact number is institutional.

Warm the skin while you cool the core. The single most underused anti-shivering measure, and it saves sedation.

Paralysis is last, and it hides seizures. If you paralyze, you need EEG.

Stop replacing potassium before you rewarm. It comes back out of the cells.

Rewarming is the dangerous phase. Hyperkalemia, hypoglycemia and hypotension arrive together, and switching the machine off is not rewarming.

Do not leave the FiO2 at 1.0. Hyperoxia after arrest is a harm, not a safety margin.

Bradycardia at target is physiology. Leave it alone if the patient is perfusing.

Esophageal probe. Bladder lags when it matters most.

No prognosis before 72 hours off sedation and at normothermia — and never from a single test.
