Synchronized Cardioversion vs. Defibrillation

Knowing which button to press, and why the timing of the shock relative to the QRS is the entire distinction between the two.

CARDIAC / NEURO

01

Indications

Synchronized cardioversion — an unstable tachyarrhythmia with a pulse and a discernible, organized QRS:

  • Unstable SVT refractory to vagal maneuvers/adenosine
  • Unstable atrial fibrillation or flutter with rapid ventricular response
  • Unstable monomorphic VT with a pulse

Defibrillation (unsynchronized) — no pulse, or no synchronizable QRS:

  • Pulseless VF or pulseless VT
  • Polymorphic VT, including torsades — the QRS varies beat to beat, so the machine cannot reliably find an R wave. Unsynchronized, even if a pulse is present.
“Unstable” means the rhythm is causing the problem: hypotension, altered mentation, ischemic chest pain, or acute heart failure attributable to the rate. A heart rate alone is not an indication — and a rate below about 150 rarely explains shock by itself in an adult, so look hard for another cause before shocking.
02

Contraindications & Cautions

  • Stable tachyarrhythmia — treat pharmacologically; you have time
  • Do not attempt to synchronize a rhythm without a consistent QRS. The machine will either refuse to discharge or sync to the wrong deflection. Polymorphic VT goes unsynchronized.
  • Digoxin toxicity — cardioversion can precipitate refractory ventricular arrhythmias. If unavoidable, use the lowest effective energy and have magnesium and antidote available.
  • Atrial fibrillation of >48 hours or unknown duration in a stable patient — elective cardioversion carries thromboembolic risk and needs anticoagulation or TOE first. In the genuinely unstable patient this consideration does not override shocking.
  • Implanted device — not a contraindication; place pads at least 8 cm from the generator, ideally anteroposterior, and have the device interrogated afterwards.

Escalate if the first shock fails

If a synchronized shock does not convert the rhythm, step the energy up for the next attempt rather than repeating the same dose. Repeating an identical failed shock is the same error as repeating an identical failed laryngoscopy.

04

Stepwise Technique

Synchronized cardioversion
1
Confirm instability and a synchronizable rhythm

Pulse present, organized QRS, and symptoms attributable to the rate.

Print or freeze a strip — you want to be certain this is monomorphic before choosing sync.

2
Prepare the skin and place pads

Dry the chest, clip dense hair, remove transdermal patches.

  • Anteroposterior placement is generally preferred for atrial fibrillation and for patients with implanted devices;
  • anterolateral is acceptable and faster.

Keep pads ≥8 cm from any device generator.

3
Sedate — this is a conscious patient about to be shocked

If there is any time at all, provide procedural sedation and analgesia.

Titrate carefully: the blood pressure you are worried about is already low.

Small doses, pre-oxygenation, airway equipment and a vasopressor within reach.

4
Press SYNC, then look at the screen

Activate synchronization and visually confirm a marker sitting on every R wave — and only on R waves.

If markers are landing on T waves or appearing intermittently, increase the ECG gain or switch to a lead with a taller R.

Do not shock until the markers are right. This look at the screen is the entire safety mechanism of the procedure.

5
Charge, clear, and hold the button

Announce and confirm everyone is clear.

Hold the shock button down — in sync mode the machine waits for the next R wave, so there is a perceptible delay between pressing and discharge.

Releasing early is a common reason “the shock didn't work.”

6
Reassess — and re-arm SYNC before the next shock

Check rhythm and pulse.

If still unstable and unconverted, escalate energy and shock again — but first re-confirm sync is active.

Most defibrillators revert to unsynchronized after every delivery, by design, so that a patient who arrests can be defibrillated immediately.

Defibrillation
7
Confirm pulselessness, charge during compressions

Keep compressions going while the machine charges — this is the single biggest modifiable factor in minimizing the peri-shock pause.

Ensure sync is off.

8
Clear, shock, and resume compressions immediately

Brief clear, deliver, and go straight back to compressions without a pulse or rhythm check.

Reassess at the next scheduled rhythm check.

A perfusing rhythm rarely appears instantly after a shock, and pausing to look costs coronary perfusion pressure that took a minute to build.

Check SYNC every single time

Two failures, opposite directions, both common.

  • Sync left off when cardioverting a patient with a pulse: the shock can land on the T wave and induce VF — the R-on-T phenomenon that synchronization exists to prevent.
  • Sync left on when the patient has arrested into VF: the machine hunts for an R wave it will never find and refuses to discharge, and precious seconds are lost to a defibrillator that appears broken.

Look at the mode indicator before every shock.

Complications & Pitfalls

  • R-on-T induction of VF from an unsynchronized shock in a patient with a pulse
  • Failure to discharge because sync was left on in a pulseless patient
  • Thromboembolic stroke after cardioverting AF of >48 hours without anticoagulation
  • Ventricular arrhythmia in digoxin toxicity
  • Sedation-related hypotension or apnoea in an already marginal patient
  • Skin burns, and myocardial injury from unnecessarily high repeated energies
  • Implanted device malfunction from pads placed too close to the generator
  • Shocking a stable patient who needed a drug and a conversation instead
07

Pearls

Sync protects a pulse. That is the whole distinction — if there is an organized QRS and a pulse to preserve, time the shock away from the T wave; if there is neither, there is nothing to protect.

Look at the markers, not just the mode light. Sync engaged but marking T waves is more dangerous than no sync at all.

Hold the button. The delay is the machine doing its job.

Re-arm sync after every shock. The revert-to-unsync default is deliberate, and it will catch you.

Polymorphic means unsynchronized, pulse or no pulse.

Charge during compressions and resume them immediately after — the peri-shock pause is the thing you control.

Internal paddles are 10–20 J. Two orders of magnitude of error are possible here.

If it converts and immediately recurs, the problem is not the shock.
