Arterial Line Placement During Resuscitation

Real-time hemodynamic monitoring when non-invasive measurement isn't keeping up with how fast the patient is changing.

VASCULAR ACCESS / CIRCULATORY
⚡ TECHNIQUE Indications Contraindications Equipment Complications & Pitfalls Pearls References
01

Indications

  • Need for continuous, beat-to-beat blood pressure monitoring during active resuscitation or hemodynamic instability
  • Frequent arterial blood gas sampling anticipated
  • Titration of vasopressors/inotropes where non-invasive cuff measurements are too slow or unreliable (e.g., low-flow states)
02

Contraindications

  • Inadequate collateral circulation at the intended site (e.g., abnormal Allen's test for radial site, though rarely a hard stop in an emergency)
  • Local infection or significant trauma overlying the intended site
  • Severe peripheral vascular disease at the intended site (relative — choose an alternate site)
03

Equipment

  • Arterial line catheter kit (typically 20 ga for radial, larger for femoral)
  • Ultrasound with linear probe (recommended, especially in low-flow/hypotensive states)
  • Pressure transducer, tubing, pressure bag with flush solution
  • Local anesthetic if patient is responsive
04

Stepwise Technique

1
Select the site — radial artery is typical first choice for accessibility and complication profile; femoral artery for faster access in profound shock or when radial pulses are difficult to palpate.
2
Position the extremity appropriately (wrist extension for radial access).
3
Prep the site; use ultrasound guidance if available, especially with weak or absent palpable pulses.
4
Insert the catheter-over-needle or catheter-over-wire (Seldinger) system at a shallow angle, advancing until arterial flash is seen.
5
Advance the catheter into the vessel, confirm pulsatile return.
6
Connect to the pressure tubing/transducer system, zero and level the transducer.
7
Secure the catheter, confirm waveform quality on the monitor.
Palpable pulses can be absent even when a line is achievable

In profound shock, ultrasound guidance becomes far more valuable in exactly the situations where the line is most urgently needed — don't let a difficult radial site delay you from moving to femoral access.

05

Complications & Pitfalls

  • Thrombosis or distal ischemia — monitor the extremity distal to the line regularly
  • Hematoma or bleeding at the site, especially with coagulopathy
  • Infection with prolonged dwell time
  • Inadvertent intra-arterial medication injection if the line is mistaken for venous access — label clearly
  • Damped or inaccurate waveform from catheter kinking, air in the system, or poor positioning
06

Pearls

In profound shock, palpable pulses may be absent even with adequate arterial pressure for a line — ultrasound guidance becomes far more valuable in exactly the situations where you need the line most urgently.

Femoral access is often faster and more reliable than radial in a critically hypotensive patient — don't force a technically harder site when time and accuracy both matter.

Zero and level the transducer at the phlebostatic axis for femoral or central-adjacent sites to avoid a systematically inaccurate reading.
