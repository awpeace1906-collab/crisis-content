Arterial Line Placement During Resuscitation

Real-time hemodynamic monitoring when non-invasive measurement isn't keeping up with how fast the patient is changing.

01

Indications

  • Any patient on a vasoactive infusion. A cuff cycling every three minutes cannot titrate a drug whose effect changes in thirty seconds — and in low-flow states the cuff is not merely slow, it is wrong.
  • Shock of any cause where the trend between cuff readings is the information you need
  • Frequent blood gases — ventilated patients, DKA, toxic ingestions, post-arrest care
  • During CPR — a genuinely different indication with genuinely different value; see §08. It lets you measure whether your compressions are generating perfusion and detect ROSC without pausing.
  • Titrating blood pressure to a tight target — aortic dissection, post-arrest neuroprotection, intracranial hemorrhage, permissive hypotension in trauma
  • Dynamic preload assessment (pulse pressure variation) in the appropriate ventilated patient
02

Contraindications

  • Infection, burn, or trauma over the site — choose another artery.
  • Known vascular compromise or a previous surgical or interventional injury to that vessel — prior radial harvest for CABG, an AV fistula in that arm, a vascular graft.
  • Raynaud phenomenon, thromboangiitis obliterans, or severe peripheral vascular disease at the intended site.
  • End arteries without collateral supply. The brachial artery is the notable one — it is effectively the sole supply to the forearm, and thrombosis there threatens the whole limb. The median nerve runs alongside it. Prefer radial, femoral, or axillary.
  • Coagulopathy is relative — but it makes site choice matter. Radial is superficial and compressible; femoral bleeding can dissect into the retroperitoneum if the puncture is above the inguinal ligament.

On the Allen test: it has been shown repeatedly to predict neither ischemic complications nor their absence, and a “failed” Allen test in a patient who needs a line should not stop you.

Its residual value is as a prompt to look at the hand and think about collateral flow before and after. It is not a gate.

03

Equipment

  • Catheter. 20 G, 4.5–5 cm for radial — either a plain catheter-over-needle or an integrated-guidewire kit. 18 G, 10–16 cm for femoral, placed by Seldinger technique. Pediatric radial: 22–24 G.
  • Ultrasound, linear probe, sterile sheath. Ultrasound guidance improves first-pass success and reduces attempts, and its advantage is largest in precisely the patients you are placing the line in — hypotensive, edematous, pulseless. Default to it.
  • Transducer set: pressure tubing (short and stiff — see §07), transducer, three-way stopcock, cable to the monitor.
  • Flush bag under a pressure bag at 300 mmHg, delivering roughly 3 mL/h continuously. Check the pressure bag is actually inflated — a deflated bag is a common cause of a line that clots and a trace that damps.
  • Lidocaine 1% for any patient with perception. A small wheal also reduces arterial spasm.
  • Arm board, rolled gauze or a 500 mL bag, and tape for radial positioning.
  • Clear labeling — red-striped tubing, an ARTERIAL label on the stopcock, and no injection ports that can be mistaken for an IV.

Choosing the Site — and the Radial-to-Femoral Gradient

SiteForAgainst
RadialSuperficial, compressible, good collateral via the palmar arches, lowest complication rate, keeps the groin freeSmall and spasm-prone. Under-reads central pressure in shock (see below). Hardest exactly when you need it most.
FemoralLarge, easy to find with ultrasound even when pulseless, accurate in shock, stays patent when radial arteries have clamped down. Often the right first choice in profound shock.Groin contamination and higher infection risk. Retroperitoneal bleed if punctured above the ligament. Competes with ECMO/REBOA access.
AxillaryCentral pressures, large vessel, good collateral through the scapular network. Underused.Brachial plexus adjacent. Not compressible. Requires ultrasound.
Dorsalis pedis / posterior tibialAvailable when arms are burned, injured, or occupiedSmall, distal, and systolic pressure is markedly amplified — often 10–20 mmHg above central. MAP remains usable.
BrachialLarge, easy to palpateEnd artery with the median nerve alongside. Reserve for when nothing else is available.
The radial line may be lying to you in vasopressor shock

In distributive shock on high-dose vasopressors, peripheral vasoconstriction creates a real pressure gradient between the aorta and the radial artery. Radial MAP can read 10–30 mmHg below simultaneous femoral MAP.

The failure mode is specific and dangerous:

  1. the radial number looks inadequate,
  2. so the norepinephrine goes up,
  3. so the gradient widens,
  4. so the number still looks inadequate.

If a patient is on escalating pressors and the radial trace looks disproportionately bad for how they appear, place or compare against a femoral line before climbing further.

05

Radial Technique

1
Position — extended, not hyperextended

Rolled gauze or a small fluid bag under the wrist, arm on a board, palm up.

Extend to about 30–45° and tape the thumb down in gentle traction to stabilize the vessel.

Hyperextension flattens the artery and reduces flow — a wrist bent back as far as it goes is a common and self-inflicted reason a line will not thread. If the pulse weakens as you extend, back off.

2
Map it with ultrasound before you prep

Short axis, just proximal to the wrist crease.

Identify the radial artery: round, pulsatile, non-compressible, with the two small venae comitantes flanking it.

Note the depth (usually 2–5 mm) and the course — if it dives or branches, move proximally to a straighter segment. A palpable pulse tells you nothing about whether the vessel runs straight.

3
Prep, drape, and wheal

Chlorhexidine and dry.

A small lidocaine wheal for anyone with perception — it also blunts the spasm that follows a painful first pass.

4
Enter at 30–45°, then go flat

Puncture the skin, then advance slowly toward the vessel.

Flash means the needle tip is in the lumen — not the catheter.

  1. Drop the hub toward the skin to about 10–15° and advance the whole assembly a further 1–2 mm so the catheter's leading edge crosses the arterial wall.
  2. Only then advance the catheter alone.

Skipping this is the single commonest reason a line flashes beautifully and then will not thread.

5
Ultrasound: walk the tip down, don't chase the shaft

In short axis, what you see is a cross-section of the needle — and a bright dot may be the shaft, not the tip.

Use dynamic needle tip positioning:

  1. advance the needle a millimeter,
  2. slide the probe a millimeter forward,
  3. re-find the bright dot,
  4. repeat.

You are always looking at the tip because you keep moving the probe past it.

Alternatively use long axis in-plane, which shows the whole needle at once and is excellent for a straight superficial radial — at the cost of needing to hold the probe exactly in the vessel's plane.

6
If it won't thread: transfixion

A legitimate rescue, not a failure.

  1. Deliberately pass through both walls,
  2. remove the needle,
  3. then withdraw the catheter slowly until pulsatile flow returns — you are now in the lumen from behind.
  4. Advance.

With an integrated-wire kit, thread the wire at the moment of pulsatile return instead.

Transfixion punctures the back wall, so expect a little more bruising.

7
Connect, flush, secure

Occlude the artery proximally with a fingertip while you disconnect the needle — it keeps the field clean and loses less blood.

Connect the primed transducer tubing, flush, and secure with a sutureless device or a suture.

Keep the wrist in gentle extension in the final dressing; a line that works on the arm board and damps when the wrist flexes is positional, not broken.

Femoral arterial line

Same Seldinger sequence as a central line, with the target switched: the artery is lateral to the vein (NAVEL, lateral to medial: Nerve, Artery, Vein, Empty space, Lymphatics). Puncture 1–2 cm below the inguinal ligament, measured from the ASIS–pubic tubercle line rather than the skin crease.

  1. Enter at 45°,
  2. take pulsatile return,
  3. thread the wire with no resistance,
  4. nick,
  5. dilate shallowly, and
  6. advance an 18 G, 10–16 cm catheter.

Two things differ from the venous version.

  • Above the ligament, an arterial bleed is retroperitoneal — unrecognizable and uncompressible.
  • And if ECPR or REBOA is on the table, this is the vessel those teams need; place it on the side they are not planning to use, and tell them it is there.
06

Zeroing, Leveling, and Why the Number Is Wrong

Two different actions that get conflated, and one of them has to be repeated all day.

Zeroing tells the monitor which pressure is atmospheric.

Open the stopcock to air at the transducer, press zero, close it.

Done once at setup and after any disconnection or suspicion of drift.

Leveling tells you what you are measuring.

The transducer must sit at the phlebostatic axis — fourth intercostal space, mid-axillary line.

This must be re-checked every time the bed height or the patient's position changes, which in a busy unit is many times a day and is the most frequently skipped step in arterial monitoring.

The number that makes it concrete: every 10 cm of vertical offset is a 7.4 mmHg error. A transducer taped to the bed rail 13 cm below the patient's chest reads about 10 mmHg too high — more than enough to justify not escalating a pressor on a patient who needs one. Above the axis, it under-reads by the same rule.

For cerebral perfusion — post-arrest care, traumatic brain injury, stroke — some units level to the external auditory meatus (approximating the circle of Willis) instead, which reads lower than the phlebostatic axis by the height difference. Know which convention your unit uses and make sure everyone at the bedside is using the same one; two teams levelling differently on the same patient will disagree about the MAP by 10–15 mmHg and never work out why.

07

Reading the Waveform

The trace carries more information than the three numbers next to it.

The fast-flush (square wave) test — do this before you believe any arterial number. Pull the flush tab for about a second and release. The trace squares off at the flush pressure, then snaps back. Count the oscillations after release:

  • 1–2 oscillations, then a clean return to baseline — optimally damped. Trust all the numbers.
  • Fewer than 1 — sluggish, rounded return: over-damped. The trace loses its dicrotic notch and looks flattened. It under-reads systolic and over-reads diastolic. Causes: air bubbles anywhere in the circuit, a clot or fibrin at the tip, a kink, the wrist flexed, compliant or over-long tubing, too many stopcocks, a flush bag below 300 mmHg, or arterial spasm.
  • More than 2–3 oscillations — a whippy, spiky trace: under-damped (hyper-resonant). It over-reads systolic by as much as 20–30 mmHg and under-reads diastolic. Causes: long stiff tubing, tachycardia, high contractility, catheter whip. A damping device or a shorter tubing run fixes it.

The rule that saves you in both cases: MAP is the robust number. Mean arterial pressure survives damping error far better than systolic or diastolic, because it is an integrated area rather than a peak. When the waveform is imperfect and you cannot fix it immediately, titrate to MAP, not to systolic.

What else the shape tells you:

  • Upstroke slope — steepness tracks contractility and the rate of ventricular ejection. A slurred, slow upstroke suggests poor contractility or aortic stenosis.
  • Dicrotic notch position — a notch sitting low on the downstroke suggests low systemic vascular resistance (sepsis, vasodilation). High on the limb suggests a vasoconstricted, high-resistance state.
  • Narrow pulse pressure — hypovolemia, tamponade, or a failing ventricle. Wide — vasodilation, aortic regurgitation, or a stiff aorta.
  • Pulse pressure variation (PPV) — respiratory swing in pulse pressure. >13% predicts fluid responsiveness, but only in a patient in sinus rhythm, fully mechanically ventilated with no spontaneous effort, at a tidal volume of at least 8 mL/kg, with a closed chest and no severe right heart failure. Outside those conditions it is not interpretable — and it is quoted outside those conditions constantly.
  • Pulsus paradoxus — an inspiratory systolic fall greater than 10 mmHg, visible directly on the trace. Points to tamponade, severe asthma, or massive pulmonary embolism.
  • Pulsus alternans — alternating strong and weak beats. Severe left ventricular dysfunction.
08

The Arterial Line During CPR

This is the use that changes management most, and it is the least familiar.

During cardiac arrest the arterial line stops being a monitor and becomes a feedback device on the quality of your resuscitation.

  • Diastolic pressure is the number that matters, because coronary perfusion happens in diastole. Target an arterial diastolic above roughly 25–30 mmHg during compressions. Below about 20 mmHg, ROSC becomes very unlikely.
  • A low diastolic is actionable in two directions: change the compressor or fix the mechanics (rate, depth, recoil, hand position), or give a vasopressor. It converts “are these compressions any good?” from an argument into a reading.
  • ROSC is detectable without pausing. An abrupt rise in diastolic and the appearance of an organized native waveform between compressions signals return of circulation — so you can confirm it without the interruption in flow that a pulse check costs.
  • Use it alongside end-tidal CO2, not instead of it. ETCO2 reflects pulmonary blood flow, arterial diastolic reflects coronary perfusion pressure; they answer adjacent questions and together they cover most of what you want to know.
  • Post-ROSC it is immediately useful for titrating to a MAP target without waiting on a cuff that will not cycle usefully in a poorly perfused patient.

In a prolonged code, particularly one heading toward ECPR, a femoral arterial line is worth the two minutes it costs — and it is often being placed by the same team, at the same time, in the same groin as the rest of the cannulation plan. Coordinate that.

Never inject into an arterial line

Inadvertent intra-arterial injection causes intense vasospasm, distal thrombosis, and tissue necrosis — it has ended in amputation. The defenses are physical and procedural, not vigilance:

  • red-striped tubing,
  • an ARTERIAL label at every stopcock,
  • no needleless injection ports on the arterial circuit, and
  • stating “that's the art line” out loud at every handover.

If it happens: stop immediately, leave the catheter in place, and involve vascular surgery — the catheter is the route for any attempted treatment.

10

Complications

  • Temporary radial artery occlusion — not rare, and usually asymptomatic because of palmar arch collateral. Permanent ischemic injury is well under 1%. Risk rises with catheter size relative to vessel, dwell time, repeated attempts, and vasopressor use.
  • Distal ischemia — the complication that matters. Check the hand or foot at least every shift and document it; the first sign is often mottling or delayed capillary refill rather than pain.
  • Hematoma and bleeding, particularly on removal or with coagulopathy. Retroperitoneal hemorrhage from a high femoral puncture.
  • Pseudoaneurysm and arteriovenous fistula — femoral more than radial; consider in any new pulsatile mass or bruit at an old site.
  • Infection — lower than central venous catheters but not negligible; femoral highest.
  • Nerve injury — median nerve at the brachial site, brachial plexus at the axillary site.
  • Retrograde cerebral embolization — forceful flushing of a radial line with as little as 2 mL can drive air or clot retrograde into the subclavian and then the carotid circulation. Flush gently and in small volumes.
  • Forearm compartment syndrome — rare, from a large hematoma in an anticoagulated patient.
  • Blood loss from sampling — a real and cumulative cause of anemia in a long ICU stay. Use a closed conservation system and question each draw.
11

Maintenance and Removal

  • Re-level at every position change, and square-wave test at the start of each shift and any time the numbers surprise you.
  • Document distal perfusion each shift — color, temperature, capillary refill, sensation.
  • Keep the pressure bag at 300 mmHg and the flush bag full. Most “the art line died overnight” events start here.
  • Remove it when the indication ends. The line is there for pressor titration or frequent gases; when neither is true, it is only accumulating risk and drawing blood.
  • On removal: hold direct pressure for a full 5 minutes at a radial site, longer with coagulopathy or antiplatelet therapy, and 10–20 minutes for femoral. Do not occlude so hard that you obliterate distal flow for the entire period. Then apply a pressure dressing and re-check the site and the distal pulse 15 and 60 minutes later — late re-bleeding after an apparently dry site is the usual pattern.
12

Pearls

Square-wave test before you believe the number. Four seconds, and it tells you whether the systolic in front of you is real.

When the waveform is imperfect, trust the MAP. Damping distorts systolic and diastolic far more than the mean.

Flash is the needle, not the catheter. Drop the angle and advance 1–2 mm more. This single habit fixes most failed radial attempts.

Don't hyperextend the wrist. You flatten the artery you are trying to cannulate.

Ten centimeters is 7.4 mmHg. A transducer that has drifted off the phlebostatic axis is the commonest reason a stable patient appears to have changed.

In vasopressor shock the radial under-reads. Compare against femoral before you escalate on a number you do not trust.

In arrest, watch diastolic. Above 25–30 mmHg during compressions, or change something. It also shows you ROSC without a pause.

PPV needs four conditions. Sinus rhythm, fully controlled ventilation, tidal volume ≥8 mL/kg, closed chest. Outside those, it is a number, not information.

Label it, and never inject into it. The safeguards have to be physical — red tubing and no injection ports — because attention fails.

Check the hand. The complication that costs a patient something is distal ischemia, and it is found by looking.
