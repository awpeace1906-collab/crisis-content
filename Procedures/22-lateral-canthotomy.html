Lateral Canthotomy

Emergent decompression of orbital compartment syndrome — a vision-saving procedure with a narrow therapeutic window.

01

Indications

The orbit is a closed cone with one soft wall. Cutting that wall is the entire procedure.

  • Orbital compartment syndrome — a tense, proptotic orbit with threatened vision, from retrobulbar hemorrhage or any rapidly expanding orbital process
  • After orbital or facial trauma, especially with a fracture and an anticoagulated patient
  • Iatrogenic retrobulbar hemorrhage — after a retrobulbar or peribulbar block, blepharoplasty, or orbital surgery
  • Spontaneous retrobulbar hemorrhage — anticoagulation, vascular malformation, Valsalva
  • Rapidly expanding orbital emphysema, abscess, or edema with the same physiology

The mechanism: the orbit is bone on four sides.

The only structure that can give is the front — the orbital septum, held in place by the medial and lateral canthal tendons.

Blood accumulating behind the globe pushes it forward until those tendons tether it, at which point pressure rises steeply, the central retinal artery perfusion pressure is overcome, and the retina becomes ischemic.

Canthotomy and cantholysis release the anterior tether. Nothing else available at the bedside does.

This is a clinical diagnosis and a 90-minute problem

Retinal ischemia becomes irreversible somewhere around 90 to 120 minutes, and there are reports of loss sooner.

  • Do not send the patient to CT to confirm it.
  • Do not wait for ophthalmology to arrive from home, and
  • do not wait for a formal visual acuity.

The diagnosis is made with a light, a hand, and a tonometer if one is nearby, and the treatment takes about two minutes.

Vision is lost to delay in recognition, essentially never to technical difficulty.

The One Real Contraindication

Globe rupture. A ruptured globe has already decompressed itself; there is no compartment to release, and manipulating the orbit risks extruding intraocular contents. Look for it before you cut:

  • Teardrop or peaked pupil pointing toward the rupture
  • Extruded dark uveal tissue at the limbus or under the conjunctiva
  • Seidel sign — aqueous streaming through fluorescein
  • Shallow or flat anterior chamber, hyphema, marked hypotony
  • 360° bullous subconjunctival hemorrhage, or a visible full-thickness laceration

If the globe is ruptured: rigid shield, no pressure, no further examination, antiemetics, analgesia, antibiotics, and ophthalmology now. A patient can have both a ruptured globe and a tense orbit — that is a specialist decision, not a bedside one.

Not contraindications: anticoagulation, thrombocytopenia, an uncooperative patient, or the absence of an ophthalmologist. Each of these is a reason to proceed more carefully, not to wait.

04

Equipment

  • Local anesthetic with epinephrine — 1–2 mL of lidocaine 1–2% with 1:100,000 epinephrine. The vasoconstriction genuinely helps in a field that will bleed.
  • Straight scissors — iris scissors, tenotomy scissors, or straight Mayo. Curved scissors make the cantholysis harder to direct.
  • Toothed forceps — Adson or similar. You need to grip and pull the lid firmly; smooth forceps will slip.
  • A straight hemostat — for the crush step, which both reduces bleeding and marks the line.
  • Gauze, saline, and a rigid eye shield for afterwards.
  • Tonometer if available, before and after.
  • Nothing else. This does not need a tray, an operating room, or sterile drapes. A prepped skin surface and clean instruments are sufficient.
05

Technique

1
Document vision, pupil and pressure — fast
  • Acuity at whatever level they can give,
  • the presence of an afferent defect, and
  • IOP if a tonometer is at hand.

Thirty seconds, not five minutes. These numbers establish both the indication and the response.

2
Infiltrate laterally, away from the globe

Inject 1–2 mL into the lateral canthus, directing the needle laterally toward the orbital rim, never medially toward the eye.

Adding anesthetic adds volume to a compartment that is already over-pressurized, so use the smallest volume that will work.

3
Crush with the hemostat for 30–60 seconds

Clamp from the lateral commissure horizontally out to the orbital rim, and hold.

This crushes the tissue you are about to cut, which reduces bleeding into a field you need to see — and it leaves a visible blanched line that is exactly your incision.

Optional in principle; worth the minute in practice.

4
Canthotomy — cut horizontally to the rim

Place one blade in front of and one behind the lateral canthus, and cut full thickness, horizontally, about 1–2 cm out to the lateral orbital rim.

Skin, muscle, conjunctiva — all of it.

This step alone relieves almost nothing. It is the exposure, not the treatment.

5
Cantholysis — this is the procedure
  1. Grasp the lower lid at its cut edge with toothed forceps and pull it anteriorly and superiorly, away from the face. That tension puts the inferior crus of the lateral canthal tendon on stretch.
  2. Point the scissors inferiorly and slightly posteriorly — toward the patient's chin, roughly 45° — and strum the tissue with the blade tips. You will feel a taut band like a guitar string.
  3. Cut it.

There is a distinct release, and the lower lid becomes completely floppy.

6
Test the release before you stop

Pull the lower lid away from the globe.

  • If it comes freely, with no tethering at all, the crus is divided.
  • If anything still holds it back, you have not cut the tendon — go back and strum again.

Failing to complete the cantholysis is the classic technical error, and it leaves an orbit that has been cut but not decompressed.

7
Reassess — and release the superior crus if needed

Recheck pressure, proptosis, and vision.

If improvement is inadequate, release the superior crus using the same technique with the upper lid, scissors pointing superiorly.

Do this without hesitation — the risk is ptosis from levator injury, which is trivial against blindness.

8
Still tense? Keep going.

If both crura are released and the orbit is still hard, the septum may need dividing (septolysis), or the hematoma may need direct drainage.

Get ophthalmology or oculoplastics now — but do not stop at a half-measure while waiting.

9
Shield it, and recheck the vision

Saline gauze and a rigid shield — not a pressure patch, which is the opposite of what this orbit needs.

Then document acuity, pupil and pressure again. Serial checks, because a hemorrhage can continue and re-tension the orbit.

Medical adjuncts — alongside, never instead:

  • head of bed up 30–45°,
  • acetazolamide 500 mg IV,
  • mannitol 1–2 g/kg IV, and
  • a topical agent such as timolol.
  • Reverse anticoagulation where relevant.

Each of these lowers pressure by a modest amount over tens of minutes. None of them opens a closed compartment. Start them on the way to the cut, not as an alternative to it.

07

Complications

  • Incomplete decompression — by a wide margin the most important, and always from stopping at the canthotomy.
  • Bleeding at the incision — common, self-limiting, and mitigated by the crush step.
  • Injury to the globe or lateral rectus — avoided by directing the scissors away from the eye and staying at the rim.
  • Lacrimal gland injury with superior crus release.
  • Ptosis from levator damage during superior cantholysis — an accepted trade.
  • Ectropion, lid malposition, or scarring, usually correctable later.
  • Infection — uncommon; the periorbital blood supply is excellent.
  • Cutting an eye that did not need it — a small harm, and a far better error than the other one.
08

Afterwards

  • Do not suture it acutely. The wound is left open and heals by secondary intention with surprisingly good cosmetic results. Formal canthoplasty, if wanted, happens later and electively.
  • Rigid shield, never a pressure patch.
  • Serial visual acuity, pupil and pressure checks — hourly at first. Re-accumulation happens.
  • Ophthalmology review for every patient, regardless of how well it went.
  • CT orbits now, once the emergency is treated — for fracture, foreign body, and the extent of the hemorrhage.
  • Reverse the anticoagulation where one is responsible, and stop antiplatelet agents pending review.
  • Antibiotics in the setting of trauma or fracture; tetanus.
  • Document the before-and-after numbers. Acuity, afferent defect, and IOP at both time points, with times. This is the record that shows the procedure was both indicated and effective.
09

Pearls

The canthotomy is the exposure; the cantholysis is the treatment. If you stop after the skin incision you have done nothing for the pressure.

The endpoint is a floppy lid. Pull it away from the globe — if it tethers at all, the crus is still intact.

Pull the lid up and out, and point the scissors at the chin. The tendon only becomes findable under tension.

Do not go to CT first. This is a clinical diagnosis with a ninety-minute clock.

Retropulsion is the fastest sign. A globe that feels like a marble is the whole examination when time is short.

Look for a ruptured globe before you cut. Teardrop pupil, extruded uvea, Seidel sign.

Acetazolamide and mannitol are adjuncts. They do not open a closed compartment.

If one crus is not enough, take the other. Ptosis is a trivial price.

Leave it open. It heals well and it is not a repair you make at 3 a.m.

Recheck it in an hour. Hemorrhage can continue.
