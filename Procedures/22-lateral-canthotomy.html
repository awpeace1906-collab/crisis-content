Lateral Canthotomy

Emergent decompression of orbital compartment syndrome — a vision-saving procedure with a narrow therapeutic window.

CARDIAC / NEURO
⚡ TECHNIQUE Indications Contraindications Equipment Complications & Pitfalls Pearls References
01

Indications

  • Orbital compartment syndrome from retrobulbar hemorrhage or rapidly expanding orbital process, typically presenting with proptosis, decreased visual acuity, elevated intraocular pressure, and an afferent pupillary defect
  • Clinical diagnosis with a time-critical window — permanent vision loss can occur within approximately 90-120 minutes of sustained elevated intraocular pressure
02

Contraindications

  • None absolute given the time-critical, vision-threatening nature of true orbital compartment syndrome
  • Globe rupture — canthotomy technique should be adapted with caution, avoiding any additional pressure on the globe itself; some sources still support proceeding if compartment syndrome is confirmed, but extra care is warranted
03

Equipment

  • Local anesthetic with epinephrine
  • Straight (Mayo/suture) scissors
  • Toothed forceps
  • Hemostat (for the crush technique on the lateral canthal tendon prior to cutting, optional but reduces bleeding)
04

Stepwise Technique

1
Confirm the clinical diagnosis: elevated intraocular pressure, proptosis, decreased vision, in the setting of trauma or rapid orbital swelling.
2
Infiltrate local anesthetic with epinephrine at the lateral canthus.
3
Using a hemostat, crush the lateral canthal tendon area for several seconds to reduce bleeding (optional step, improves hemostasis).
4
Using straight scissors, perform a lateral canthotomy — a horizontal incision through the lateral canthus, extending from the lateral commissure toward the orbital rim.
5
Identify the inferior crus of the lateral canthal tendon by palpating/pulling the lower lid laterally with forceps.
6
Cut the inferior crus of the lateral canthal tendon (cantholysis) — this is the step that actually releases the compartment pressure; the canthotomy incision alone is often insufficient.
7
Reassess intraocular pressure and visual function after cantholysis; if inadequate improvement, consider releasing the superior crus as well.
8
Arrange urgent ophthalmology follow-up/consultation regardless of immediate improvement.
The canthotomy incision alone is not the therapeutic step

Cutting the inferior crus of the lateral canthal tendon (cantholysis) is what actually relieves compartment pressure — stopping after the skin incision is a common technical error that leaves the orbit undecompressed.

05

Complications & Pitfalls

  • Performing the canthotomy incision alone without cantholysis — this is a common technical error and does not adequately decompress the orbit
  • Bleeding at the incision site
  • Inadequate pressure relief requiring release of the superior crus as well
  • Delayed recognition/intervention — the biggest driver of permanent vision loss is time, not technical difficulty of the procedure itself
06

Pearls

The canthotomy is not the therapeutic step — the cantholysis (cutting the inferior crus of the tendon) is what actually relieves the compartment pressure. Don't stop after the skin incision.

This procedure is time-critical in a way few others in this library are — roughly a 90-120 minute window before vision loss becomes permanent, so recognition speed matters as much as technique.

Reassess after the inferior crus release; if pressure and proptosis haven't adequately improved, proceed to the superior crus without hesitation.
