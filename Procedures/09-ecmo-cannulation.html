ECMO Cannulation Prep & Activation

Peripheral VA/VV cannulation framework, from activation call to first flow — and the timing discipline that drives ECPR outcomes more than technique does.

01

Indications

VA-ECMO — circulatory support
  • Refractory cardiogenic shock — infarction, myocarditis, decompensated cardiomyopathy, post-cardiotomy
  • ECPR — refractory cardiac arrest with a plausibly reversible cause
  • Massive pulmonary embolism with hemodynamic collapse
  • Severe hypothermic arrest — ECMO is both the support and the rewarming method, and outcomes here are far better than for other arrest etiologies
  • Refractory arrhythmia; massive poisoning with cardiotoxic drugs; peripartum cardiomyopathy
  • Bridge to recovery, to a durable device, to transplant — or to a decision
VV-ECMO — respiratory support
  • Severe refractory hypoxemic respiratory failure — typically P/F <80 for more than 6 hours, or <50 for more than 3 hours, despite optimized ventilation, proning, and neuromuscular blockade
  • Refractory hypercapnia with pH <7.20 despite optimal settings
  • Inability to ventilate within lung-protective limits — severe asthma, airway obstruction, bronchopleural fistula

VV-ECMO supports gas exchange; it does nothing for the circulation. A hypoxemic patient who is also in shock from right heart failure is a VA or VAV question, not a VV one.

Activation delay is the biggest modifiable factor — by a wide margin

For ECPR, survival is driven by time from arrest to flow far more than by any technical detail of the cannulation. Every additional interval of conventional CPR before flow costs outcome. The target is flow within about 60 minutes of arrest, which means the call goes out when ECMO first becomes a real possibility — not once every other option has visibly failed. An activation that turns out to be unnecessary costs almost nothing. One that comes twenty minutes late costs everything.

02

Selection — Especially for ECPR

Favorable ECPR criteria (thresholds are institutional — know yours):

  • Witnessed arrest with immediate bystander CPR — short or no no-flow interval
  • Initial shockable rhythm, or a clearly reversible cause such as hypothermia, poisoning, or massive PE
  • Low-flow time under roughly 60 minutes with high-quality, minimally interrupted compressions
  • Age within the program's limit, commonly under 65–70
  • ETCO2 above about 10 mmHg during CPR — a marker of both compression quality and residual viability
  • Signs of life during resuscitation, intermittent ROSC, or preserved neurologic signs
  • No known terminal illness, and an acceptable baseline functional state

Contraindications: unwitnessed arrest with prolonged downtime and poor CPR; an irreversible or non-survivable underlying condition; severe pre-existing multi-organ failure; known severe aortic regurgitation or aortic dissection (for VA); uncontrolled hemorrhage or recent intracranial hemorrhage (relative, and dependent on a source-control plan); severe peripheral vascular disease precluding femoral cannulation; and no institutional capacity to manage the patient afterwards.

Hypothermic arrest deserves separate mention: the usual downtime limits do not apply, neurologically intact survival is documented after hours of CPR, and ECMO is the definitive rewarming modality. Nobody is dead until they are warm and dead — and ECMO is how they get warm.

Equipment and Cannula Sizing

ConfigurationDrainage (venous)ReturnNotes
Peripheral VA21–25 Fr multistage, femoral vein → cavoatrial junction15–19 Fr, femoral artery (retrograde)Fastest to establish. Retrograde aortic flow is the source of most of its specific problems — see §06.
Peripheral VV23–25 Fr, femoral vein19–21 Fr, internal jugularSeparate the tips to limit recirculation.
VV, dual-lumenSingle cannula via right IJ, spanning SVC to IVC with the return port aimed at the tricuspid valveOne site, permits mobility. Requires echo or fluoroscopic guidance — malposition is easy and consequential.
VAVAdds a jugular return limb to a VA circuitThe answer to differential hypoxia (§06).
  • Size by the vessel, not by the patient. Measure the femoral artery with ultrasound. A common rule is that the arterial cannula's outer diameter should not exceed about two-thirds to three-quarters of the vessel's internal diameter — oversizing is how limbs are lost.
  • Flow follows the fourth power of the radius and falls with length. Full support is roughly 60–80 mL/kg/min. Drainage is almost always the limiting side, so do not undersize the venous cannula to save time.
  • A distal perfusion catheter — 5–7 Fr, antegrade into the superficial femoral artery, connected to the arterial limb. Plan it at cannulation. See §06.
  • Ultrasound for access, and TEE or fluoroscopy for wire and cannula tip confirmation.
  • Primed, de-aired circuit — pump, oxygenator, tubing, heater-cooler.
  • Heparin, and ACT or anti-Xa monitoring.
  • A mechanical CPR device for ECPR. It makes cannulation during ongoing compressions genuinely feasible, and it frees a person.
  • NIRS probes for the calves, for limb monitoring.
04

Peripheral VA Cannulation

1
Activate early, and run everything in parallel
Call the moment ECMO is plausible. Circuit priming is frequently the rate-limiting step, not the cannulation — so perfusion starts priming at minute one, while access is being obtained. Two operators work the two vessels simultaneously. Resuscitation does not pause for any of it.
2
Assign the roles out loud before anyone touches the groin
Venous operator, arterial operator, perfusionist, airway and compressions, drugs, and a scribe on the clock. ECPR fails on choreography more often than on technique.
3
Ultrasound-guided access to the common femoral vessels
Below the inguinal ligament, above the bifurcation — the same narrow window as any femoral access, and it matters more here because the sheath is enormous. Measure the artery before you choose the cannula. Vein and artery on the same side is conventional; using opposite groins is an option when anatomy demands it.
4
Confirm both wires before any dilation
The venous wire must be seen in the right atrium or IVC, and the arterial wire in the descending aorta — on TEE or fluoroscopy. Serial dilation over a misplaced wire creates a hole the size of the dilator in a structure that cannot tolerate one. This is the single most important safety step in the procedure, and under ECPR time pressure it is the one most likely to be skipped.
5
Place the distal perfusion catheter now
Antegrade into the superficial femoral artery distal to the arterial cannula, before the leg is committed. Doing it at cannulation takes a few minutes; doing it later means doing it on a leg that is already ischemic, and limb complications are among the commonest serious harms of peripheral VA support.
6
Dilate serially and place the cannulae
Step up in sequence, keeping a hand on the wire throughout. Venous cannula tip at the cavoatrial junction; arterial cannula to the institutional depth. Confirm both positions before connecting.
7
Connect wet-to-wet, and hunt every bubble
Make each connection with both ends flooded. Air on the arterial side goes to the brain and the coronaries. Clamp, connect, de-air, unclamp — deliberately, in that order, spoken aloud each time.
8
Start low and climb
Begin at low revolutions and increase while watching circuit pressures, the drainage line for chatter, the arterial waveform, and the patient. Line chatter means the drainage cannula is sucking against a wall or the patient is underfilled — reduce flow and give volume rather than driving the pump harder.
9
Anticoagulate, image, and hand over properly
Heparin per protocol once bleeding risk is assessed — commonly a bolus at cannulation then an infusion to ACT or anti-Xa targets, and sometimes deliberately withheld initially after traumatic or surgical ECPR. Secure the cannulae to the skin robustly. Chest film, echo, lactate, and limb checks. Then drop the ventilator to rest settings — the lungs have just stopped being the priority.
05

Two Problems Unique to Peripheral VA

Differential hypoxia (“harlequin” or north–south syndrome). Retrograde ECMO flow from the femoral artery meets antegrade native output from a recovering heart somewhere in the aorta — the “watershed.” If the lungs are still bad, that native blood is poorly oxygenated, and it is the blood supplying the aortic root and arch: the coronaries and the brain. The lower body is pink on ECMO blood while the heart and brain are hypoxic.

  • Monitor the right radial artery — it arises most proximally and best reflects what the brain is receiving. A pulse oximeter on the right hand is the cheap version of the same idea.
  • The fix: improve native lung function and ventilation, reduce flow competition, add a jugular return limb (VAV), or move to a central or axillary arterial return.
  • Paradoxically, a recovering heart is what causes this, so it appears just as things seem to be improving.

Left ventricular distension. Retrograde flow raises afterload on a ventricle that may not be able to eject against it. The LV dilates, the aortic valve stops opening, pulmonary edema develops, and stasis risks intracardiac thrombus.

  • Recognize it: a flat, non-pulsatile arterial waveform; worsening pulmonary edema on the film; frank pulmonary hemorrhage; and on echo, a dilated LV with an aortic valve that is not opening and spontaneous contrast.
  • Vent it: inotropic support, reduced ECMO flow, an intra-aortic balloon pump, an Impella, atrial septostomy, or a surgical vent. Pulsatility on the arterial trace is the bedside sign to protect — losing it is the warning.

07

Complications

  • Bleeding — the most frequent complication overall: cannulation sites, retroperitoneum, gastrointestinal, and intracranial hemorrhage, which is among the most feared and carries very poor outcomes.
  • Limb ischemia distal to the arterial cannula, up to and including amputation. Preventable, mostly, by a distal perfusion catheter placed at cannulation.
  • Vessel injury — dissection, perforation, retroperitoneal hemorrhage — usually from dilating over an unconfirmed wire.
  • Air embolism, catastrophic on the arterial limb.
  • Differential hypoxia and LV distension (§05).
  • Stroke, both ischemic and hemorrhagic; and watershed injury from the arrest itself.
  • Hemolysis, and acquired von Willebrand syndrome from shear across the circuit.
  • Thrombosis — circuit, oxygenator, or intracardiac — and heparin-induced thrombocytopenia.
  • Infection, and acute kidney injury frequently requiring renal replacement integrated into the circuit.
  • Recirculation in VV, limiting effective oxygen delivery.
  • A bridge to nowhere — support established in a patient with no recovery, no device candidacy and no transplant route. This is a real and foreseeable harm, and the way to avoid it is to ask what the bridge leads to before cannulating.
08

Immediately After Flow

  • Chest film and echo. Cannula positions, LV function and distension, valve opening, tamponade.
  • Rest the lungs. Lung-protective or rest settings, low driving pressure, modest PEEP. The ventilator is no longer doing the work and continuing aggressive settings adds injury.
  • Check the limb and set up monitoring for it — pulses or Doppler, NIRS, hourly documented assessment.
  • Follow lactate, urine output, and mixed venous saturation as the markers of whether flow is adequate.
  • Right radial arterial line or right-hand oximetry for VA, so differential hypoxia is detectable.
  • Treat the underlying cause. ECMO buys time and treats nothing — coronary angiography, thrombolysis or embolectomy for PE, rewarming, antidote, source control.
  • Set the exit strategy explicitly, and write it down. Recovery, durable device, transplant, or a defined time-limited trial with agreed criteria. Answering this on day four is far harder than answering it on day zero.
  • Talk to the family early about what the support does and does not do.
09

Pearls

Call early. Time from arrest to flow is the outcome variable. Nothing about the technique matters as much.

Prime in parallel. The circuit, not the cannulation, is usually the bottleneck.

Confirm both wires before you dilate. The most important safety step, and the first one sacrificed to urgency.

Place the distal perfusion catheter at cannulation — not when the leg is already turning.

Measure the artery and size to the vessel. Oversizing costs limbs.

Drainage is the limit. Chatter means give volume, not more revolutions.

Watch the right hand. Differential hypoxia appears exactly when the heart starts recovering.

Protect pulsatility. A flattening arterial trace means the LV has stopped ejecting and needs venting.

Nobody is dead until they are warm and dead — and in hypothermic arrest, ECMO is how they get warm.

Know what the bridge leads to before you build it.
