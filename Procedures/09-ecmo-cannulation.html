ECMO Cannulation Prep & Activation

Peripheral VA/VV cannulation framework, from activation call to first flow — and the timing discipline that drives ECPR outcomes more than technique does.

VASCULAR ACCESS / CIRCULATORY
⚡ TECHNIQUE Indications Contraindications Equipment Complications & Pitfalls Pearls References
01

Indications

VA-ECMO

  • Refractory cardiogenic shock
  • ECPR for witnessed arrest with high-quality CPR and a reversible/treatable cause
  • Post-cardiotomy shock
  • Massive PE with hemodynamic collapse
  • Severe hypothermia with cardiac instability (rewarming crossover)

VV-ECMO

  • Severe refractory hypoxemic and/or hypercapnic respiratory failure despite optimized mechanical ventilation and adjuncts (proning, paralysis, inhaled pulmonary vasodilators)

General

  • Bridge to recovery, bridge to durable device/transplant, or bridge to decision
02

Contraindications

  • Unwitnessed arrest or prolonged downtime with no ROSC and poor CPR quality (for ECPR specifically)
  • Irreversible/non-survivable underlying condition
  • Severe pre-existing multi-organ failure incompatible with meaningful recovery
  • Uncontrolled hemorrhage (relative — requires concurrent source control planning)
  • Lack of institutional capability to manage ECMO post-cannulation (relative to transfer feasibility)
03

Equipment

  • ECMO cannulation kit (arterial and venous cannulae, sized per patient)
  • Ultrasound (vascular access, cannula tip confirmation)
  • Guidewires, dilators, introducer sheaths
  • Circuit primed and de-aired (pump, oxygenator, tubing) per institutional protocol
  • Anticoagulation (heparin) and ACT monitoring capability
  • Fluoroscopy or TEE if available for cannula positioning confirmation
  • Full cannulation team: proceduralist, circuit specialist/perfusion, nursing, airway/code team continuing resuscitation in parallel for ECPR
04

Stepwise Technique

Peripheral VA cannulation &mdash; general framework
1
Activate ECMO team early — call the moment criteria are anticipated, not once decompensation is complete.
2
Continue resuscitation (compressions, ventilation, vasopressors) uninterrupted during setup for ECPR cases.
3
Prime and de-air circuit in parallel with access — do not let circuit prep be the bottleneck.
4
Obtain venous access (typically femoral vein) via ultrasound-guided Seldinger technique; serially dilate to accommodate venous cannula.
5
Obtain arterial access (typically femoral artery) via ultrasound-guided Seldinger technique; consider a distal perfusion catheter to mitigate limb ischemia.
6
Advance venous cannula to the cavoatrial junction; advance arterial cannula per sizing/institutional target.
7
Confirm cannula position (fluoroscopy, TEE, or venous blood gas from the venous limb as a surrogate) before connecting to circuit.
8
Connect cannulae to primed circuit, eliminating air at every connection.
9
Initiate flow at low RPM, titrate up while monitoring hemodynamics, oxygenation, and circuit pressures.
10
Anticoagulate per protocol once bleeding risk is assessed; establish ACT/anti-Xa monitoring.
11
Secure cannulae, obtain confirmatory imaging (CXR, or fluoroscopy if not already done), and transition to ICU-level ECMO management.
Activation delay is the single biggest modifiable factor

For ECPR specifically, time from arrest to flow drives outcome more than any technical detail of the cannulation itself — call for activation the moment ECMO becomes a real possibility, not once every other option has failed.

05

Complications & Pitfalls

  • Limb ischemia distal to arterial cannula — have a distal perfusion catheter plan ready, not as an afterthought
  • Air embolism during cannulation or connection — de-air meticulously at every step
  • Malposition (venous cannula against a wall, arterial cannula too proximal/distal) — confirm before committing flow
  • Bleeding at access sites, worsened once anticoagulated
  • Delayed activation — the single biggest modifiable factor in ECPR outcomes is time from arrest to flow
  • Harlequin syndrome (differential hypoxia) in peripheral VA-ECMO with native cardiac recovery and poor lung function — may require venous cannula repositioning or conversion to VAV
06

Pearls

The clock starts at low-flow/no-flow time, not at the decision to cannulate — activate the team the moment ECMO is a real possibility.

Two operators working access sites simultaneously (one venous, one arterial) meaningfully shortens time to flow.

Circuit priming is often the rate-limiting step, not the cannulation itself — get perfusion/circuit specialist moving in parallel from minute one.

Have your distal perfusion strategy decided before you need it, not while a limb is turning white on the table.
