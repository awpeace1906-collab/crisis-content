Junctional Tourniquet Application

Hemorrhage control at the junctional zones — groin, axilla, pelvis — where a standard limb tourniquet has no purchase.

01

Indications

  • Life-threatening hemorrhage in a junctional zone — the groin and inguinal region, the axilla and shoulder girdle, the gluteal region, the perineum, and the base of the neck. Anatomy where there is no cylindrical limb for a standard tourniquet to squeeze.
  • Traumatic proximal amputation — a high thigh or shoulder-level amputation leaves no residual limb above the injury to place a limb tourniquet on
  • Junctional bleeding that continues despite correct wound packing and sustained direct pressure
  • As a bridge while a limb tourniquet is converted, or while proximal surgical control is arranged
  • Abdominal aortic occlusion (specific devices only) as a non-invasive analogue of Zone 3 REBOA in pelvic and high junctional hemorrhage
02

Contraindications

  • None absolute in life-threatening junctional hemorrhage.
  • Bleeding a limb tourniquet can reach. If there is a cylinder of limb above the wound, use a standard tourniquet — it is faster, better understood by everyone downstream, and does not require the specific device knowledge these do.
  • Pelvic fracture, for abdominal aortic compression devices. An inflatable wedge pressed onto a disrupted pelvis can displace the fracture and is generally avoided; a pelvic binder plus a groin-targeted device is the better combination.
  • Abdominal aortic occlusion in penetrating abdominal trauma or suspected abdominal injury — you are compressing bowel and a possibly injured aorta against the spine.
  • Pregnancy, for abdominal devices.
  • Any device you have not used before, if manual pressure is working. A fist held correctly on the femoral artery beats forty seconds spent reading a label.

Know the device in your bag, today

Limb tourniquets are close enough to one another that skill transfers. These do not. A CRoC and an AAJT share no motions, no landmarks, and no endpoints. The failure mode is specific and predictable: an operator meets an unfamiliar device during the two minutes it was bought to save, and reads the instructions while the patient exsanguinates. Find out which one your service stocks and put your hands on it before you need it. If you are improvising because you don't know the device, go back to a fist on the ramus.

04

Before the Device: the Things That Work Immediately

Every junctional device takes time to deploy. The bleeding does not pause for it, so something has to hold the line first — and these techniques are not a lesser substitute, they are the opening move.

  • A fist, a knee, or a heel on the femoral artery. Press at the midpoint of the inguinal ligament — halfway between the anterior superior iliac spine and the pubic tubercle — driving straight down so the artery is compressed against the superior pubic ramus. Use body weight through a straight arm or a knee. This works, it works instantly, and it holds while everything else is organized. It is also exhausting, so rotate people.
  • Pack the wound at the same time (see the wound packing entry). Packing and a junctional device are complementary: the pack controls what is bleeding in the wound, the device controls inflow from above. A device applied over an unpacked cavitating wound often fails.
  • Axillary compression — push the axillary artery against the humeral head, or reach higher and compress the subclavian downward behind the clavicle against the first rib.
  • Abdominal aortic compression by fist — a closed fist or the heel of the hand pressed hard just left of the umbilicus, directed posteriorly toward the spine, with the operator's weight over it. A crude and short-lived measure that can buy minutes in catastrophic pelvic or high groin bleeding.
  • Analgesia. All of this is savagely painful. Ketamine, early.
05

Inguinal Application

1
Manual pressure first, and keep it there
One person holds the femoral artery against the pubic ramus and does not let go until the device is tightened and has taken over. Deploying a device while nobody is compressing means the patient bleeds throughout the assembly.
2
Expose fully and pack the wound
Cut the clothing away to bare skin — a pad over fabric slides. Pack the wound cavity firmly while pressure is maintained proximally.
3
Place the pad over the artery, not over the wound
The target is proximal inflow: the femoral artery at the midpoint of the inguinal ligament, where it can be pressed onto the superior pubic ramus. This is the single most common misapplication — the pad is instinctively centered on the injury, which compresses a hole rather than the vessel feeding it. Find the landmark by bone, not by the wound.
4
Anchor the belt around the pelvis and tension it
Belt-based devices need a firm circumferential anchor before the pad can generate force. Seat the belt, remove the slack, then apply pressure through the pad.
5
Tighten to hemorrhage control — that is the endpoint
Screw down, ratchet, or inflate until bleeding stops. Unlike a limb tourniquet, you cannot rely on loss of a distal pulse: the collateral circulation around the pelvis and shoulder is rich, and a palpable distal pulse does not mean the device has failed. Watch the wound, not the foot.
6
Verify after every movement
Recheck after each log-roll, lift, and transfer. These devices slip — they sit on a mobile, conical, blood-slick surface, and a pad that has migrated a couple of centimeters off the artery is doing nothing while looking correct.
7
Write the time on the patient
On the skin and on the device, in marker. Say it at handover. Ischemic time drives every downstream decision and is the number most often lost.
06

Axillary Application

Harder than the groin, and less often practiced. The axillary artery has no convenient flat bone beneath it and the shoulder girdle is mobile.

  • Target the axillary artery against the humeral head with the arm adducted, or reach proximally and compress the subclavian artery downward behind the clavicle onto the first rib.
  • Anchor over the opposite shoulder — axillary applications need a counter-anchor across the torso, because there is no circumference to grip. A belt that simply encircles the chest will ride.
  • Pack the wound as well, always. Axillary bleeding has substantial collateral supply through the scapular anastomosis, and inflow control alone often leaves brisk back-bleeding.
  • Watch the airway and breathing. A device anchored across the chest restricts ventilation, and high axillary wounds are close enough to the pleura that a pneumothorax should be actively excluded.
  • Brachial plexus injury is a real consequence of sustained axillary compression. It is accepted in exchange for survival, and it is documented.
07

Abdominal Aortic Occlusion — and Its Clock

An inflatable wedge placed over the abdomen at the level of the umbilicus, compressing the infrarenal aorta against the vertebral column. Functionally a non-invasive Zone 3 REBOA: it stops inflow to the pelvis and both legs without any vascular access at all.

  • Indication: catastrophic pelvic, perineal, or high bilateral groin hemorrhage not controllable by any other means.
  • Do not use with pelvic fracture, suspected abdominal injury, penetrating abdominal trauma, or pregnancy.
  • Time limit is approximately 60 minutes, and it is a hard limit. Everything below the device is ischemic from the moment it is inflated, and the reperfusion load on release is the same problem REBOA has: potassium, acid, and vasoplegia arriving at once.
  • Announce the clock the way you would for a balloon. Inflation time called out loud, repeated every five minutes, written down.
  • Prepare for release exactly as for REBOA deflation — blood running, pressors connected, calcium drawn up, and a surgeon present. Release gradually, not by opening the valve.
  • Confirm effect by loss of femoral pulses bilaterally and a rise in proximal blood pressure. If neither happens, the device is malpositioned or under-inflated — or the bleeding is above it.

Complications

  • Ischemia distal to compression, scaling with dwell time — and with abdominal application, that means the pelvis, both legs, and the pelvic viscera.
  • Reperfusion injury on release — hyperkalemia, acidemia, vasoplegia, arrhythmia and arrest. Identical in kind to REBOA deflation, and prepared for the same way.
  • Compartment syndrome of the compressed limbs after reperfusion. Serial checks; low threshold for fasciotomy.
  • Nerve injury — femoral nerve in the groin, brachial plexus in the axilla. Frequently accepted deliberately.
  • Pressure necrosis of skin and underlying soft tissue at the pad.
  • Bowel injury and aortic injury from abdominal compression, particularly in an already injured abdomen.
  • Ventilatory restriction from abdominal or thoracic-anchored devices.
  • Fracture displacement where a device is applied over an unstable pelvis.
  • Incomplete control creating false reassurance — a device that is on and visible is easy to mistake for a problem solved.
10

Handover

  • Name the device. The receiving team may never have seen it. “Junctional tourniquet” is not enough information to release one safely.
  • Give the application time — written on the patient, not only spoken.
  • Say what is packed underneath, and how many rolls or sponges.
  • Say what has already failed, so nobody repeats it.
  • These come off in an operating room, with proximal control obtained and the team ready for reperfusion — not in a resuscitation bay to see whether the bleeding has stopped.
  • For abdominal aortic devices, state the clock as a deadline, not as an elapsed time: “inflated at 14:32, off by 15:32.”
11

Pearls

A fist on the ramus works right now. Start there, keep it there, and let the device take over — never the other way around.

The pad goes on the artery, not on the wound. Find it by bone: midpoint of the inguinal ligament, onto the superior pubic ramus.

Pack the wound too. Inflow control and cavity control are different problems and you usually have both.

The endpoint is a dry wound, not an absent pulse. Collateral flow means the pulse can persist with the device working perfectly.

Know your device before the day you need it. Nothing transfers between a CRoC, a JETT and an AAJT except the intent.

Re-check after every move. These slip, and a slipped device looks applied.

Abdominal aortic occlusion has a one-hour clock — treat it exactly like a Zone 3 balloon, including how you come off it.

Give ketamine.

Write the time on the skin.
